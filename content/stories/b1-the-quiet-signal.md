# A Normal Morning

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_1-1793351640519.mp3
Tom Reed woke up at half past six, two minutes before his alarm. He always woke up before his alarm. It was not because he was an early person. It was because of the man in the flat below. Every morning at the same time, Mr Okafor sat down at his kitchen table with a cup of tea, and every morning Tom felt it through the floor: a heavy, grey sadness, like a wet coat on his shoulders. It came up through the wood and the carpet and into his bed, and it woke him up.
He lay still for a moment and looked at the ceiling. Mr Okafor's wife had moved into a care home in the spring. She did not always remember her husband's name now. Mr Okafor never talked about it on the stairs. He smiled, he said "Good morning, Tom," and he talked about the weather or about Bristol City's last match. But Tom knew. Tom always knew. That was the problem with his life, and it was also the strangest thing about it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_2-1793351655278.mp3
Tom was thirty-two years old, and since the age of nine he had been able to feel what other people felt. Not guess, not imagine. Feel. When someone near him was angry, his own face became hot. When someone was afraid, his stomach went cold and tight. Happiness was like warm sun on the back of his neck. Love, which was rare and always a little surprising, tasted a bit like honey. And there was one more thing, the most important thing for a police officer: when someone told a lie, Tom heard it.
It was not a real sound. Nobody else in the room could hear it. But inside his head, a lie was like a false note, like a guitar string that was not quite in tune. Most of the time it was a very small note, because most lies were small. "I love your new haircut." "I'm fine, thanks." "The bus was late." People told dozens of these little lies every day, and Tom had learned to let them go past, like cars on a busy road.

He got up, had a shower and made coffee. His flat was on the second floor of an old house in Bedminster, south of the river. It was small and quiet, and he liked it because there were only two other people in the building. Fewer people meant fewer feelings. In a big block of flats, with hundreds of families, he could not sleep at all. He had tried it once, when he was a young officer, and he had moved out after three weeks with dark circles under his eyes.
On the stairs he met Mr Okafor, who was carrying a bag of rubbish down to the bins. "Morning, Tom," said the old man. "Cold today, isn't it?" "Very cold," Tom agreed. He felt the grey sadness again, stronger now, so close. For a second he wanted to say something kind about Mrs Okafor. But he could not. Mr Okafor had never told him anything. If Tom spoke about it, the old man would ask how he knew, and Tom had no good answer to that question. He never had.

So instead he took the bag from Mr Okafor's hand. "Let me take that. You'll slip on the steps." The old man laughed and let him take it. A small, warm feeling came from him, like a match in a dark room. It was not much. But it was something, and Tom carried it with him as he walked to the bus stop. The bus to the city centre was full, as always. Tom stood near the back and held the yellow bar. Around him there were forty people, and each of them was a little radio, playing a different song. And a teenage girl by the window was in love, so happily in love that the whole bus tasted faintly of honey.

When he was a child, crowds had frightened him. At school he had often hidden in the library at lunchtime, because the library was quiet and the playground was a storm. His mother had taken him to doctors. The doctors had said he was "a very sensitive boy" and given him exercises for breathing. The exercises did not stop the feelings, but they helped him to stay calm while the feelings passed through him. He still used them now, on buses and in shops and in rooms full of angry people.
He got off at Broadmead and walked the last part. He had ten minutes before he needed to be at the station, and he wanted to buy a new charger for his phone, because the old one had stopped working. There was a phone shop on the corner of Union Street. Tom did not like phone shops very much, and he knew exactly why. As soon as he pushed the glass door open, he felt it.

A young salesman with very white teeth and a badge that said JASON came towards him at once. "Good morning, sir! Are you looking for a new phone today? We have some amazing deals." He smiled widely. And from him came a feeling that Tom knew very well: a hungry, sticky feeling, like fingers that wanted to get into his pockets. It was greed. Not evil, not dangerous. Just greed, small and busy, the kind that grows in people who are paid for every sale.
"Just a charger, thanks," said Tom. "Of course, of course. But can I ask, how old is your phone? Because this week, with a new contract, you get the latest model for only thirty pounds a month." The sticky feeling became stronger. Tom smiled politely. "Just the charger." "And have you thought about insurance? Most people don't think about it until they drop their phone in the toilet. Ha ha!" The laugh was a false note, small and sharp. Jason did not think it was funny at all.

"This charger is our best one," Jason continued, holding up a box. "Twenty-nine ninety-nine. Very fast. Much better than the cheap ones, which can damage your battery." Another false note, a bigger one this time. Tom looked at the shelf behind the young man and saw a smaller box for nine pounds. "I'll take that one," he said, pointing. Jason's disappointment hit him like cold water, and then, under it, Tom felt something else: worry. The young man was worried about money. Maybe he had rent to pay. Maybe his manager shouted at him.
This was the thing that nobody understood about his gift, the thing he could never explain. It did not make people simple. A salesman was greedy, yes, but he was also worried and tired and a little bit ashamed. Every person was a mix of many feelings, one on top of another, like layers in a cake. Tom paid for the cheap charger, and on his way out he said, "Good luck with your sales today." The young man looked surprised. Then, for a moment, he felt almost grateful.

# The Missing Money

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_2.page_1-1793353626379.mp3
Hartley's Bakery was the kind of shop that people photographed. It had a big front window full of golden loaves, round cakes and trays of cinnamon buns, and an old wooden door with a little bell that rang when you opened it. Inside, the walls were painted cream and green, and there were black-and-white photos of the shop in the 1960s, with a young woman in a white apron smiling behind the counter. The young woman was Joan Hartley. Today she was eighty-one, and she was sitting at a small table by the window with a cup of tea that she was not drinking.
The shop was closed. A sign on the door said SORRY, CLOSED TODAY. A young uniformed officer, PC Sam Jones, was standing by the counter with a notebook. He looked relieved when he saw the two detectives. "Morning, Sarge," he said to Nadia. "The manager's in the back office. That's where the safe is. Mrs Hartley's here, as you can see. The bakers have gone home. They start at four in the morning, so they were already working when the manager found the safe empty."

Tom walked slowly into the middle of the shop and stopped. He let the feelings come to him. There were not many people here, so it was easy. PC Jones was bored and a bit hungry; the smell of bread was making his stomach talk. Mrs Hartley, by the window, was a deep, quiet pool of sadness, older and stronger than Mr Okafor's. But there was something else in it too. Shame. The old woman was ashamed. Tom frowned. Why would a woman feel ashamed when somebody had stolen from her?
Nadia went to the old woman first. She sat down opposite her and spoke gently. "Mrs Hartley? I'm Detective Sergeant Cole, and this is Detective Constable Reed. Inspector Harris sent us. We're going to do everything we can to find your money." Mrs Hartley looked up. Her eyes were red, but her voice was strong. "Paul is a good boy," she said. "He was always a good boy. He used to come in here after school for a jam doughnut." Nadia smiled. Tom smiled too. It was difficult to imagine Inspector Harris as a boy with a doughnut.

"Can you tell us about the money?" asked Nadia. Mrs Hartley nodded. "We were saving it for a new oven. The big oven is forty years old, you see. It's tired, like me. A new one costs twenty-five thousand pounds. We had nearly enough. Every Saturday evening my son puts the week's cash in the safe, and on Mondays he takes some of it to the bank and pays the suppliers. But we kept the oven money there too. My husband never trusted banks, God rest him. I suppose I'm the same."
There it was again. When she said "my son", the shame came up like smoke. Tom said nothing. He wrote "son" in his notebook and drew a small circle round it. "How much was in the safe exactly?" asked Nadia. "Twenty thousand, four hundred pounds. Mark counted it on Saturday. He always counts it twice. He's very careful, my Mark." Her voice was proud, and the pride was true. Tom felt no false note at all. But the shame was still there, underneath, like a stone at the bottom of a river.

# Nadia Knows

Then came the day at Temple Meads. They had gone to the railway station to meet a witness who was arriving on the train from London. The station was crowded, as it always was on Friday afternoons. Tom was standing by the ticket machines, doing his breathing, when suddenly he felt something that made his whole body go hot. It was rage. Not ordinary anger, but a deep, red, burning rage, the kind of feeling that wants to break things. And under it, there was a decision. Someone in this crowd had decided to hurt someone else.
