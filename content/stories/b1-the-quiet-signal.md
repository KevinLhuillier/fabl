# A Normal Morning

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_1-1793351640519.mp3
Tom Reed woke up at half past six, two minutes before his alarm. He always woke up before his alarm. It was not because he was an early person. It was because of the man in the flat below. Every morning at the same time, Mr Okafor sat down at his kitchen table with a cup of tea, and every morning Tom felt it through the floor: a heavy, grey sadness, like a wet coat on his shoulders. It came up through the wood and the carpet and into his bed, and it woke him up.
He lay still for a moment and looked at the ceiling. Mr Okafor's wife had moved into a care home in the spring. She did not always remember her husband's name now. Mr Okafor never talked about it on the stairs. He smiled, he said "Good morning, Tom," and he talked about the weather or about Bristol City's last match. But Tom knew. Tom always knew. That was the problem with his life, and it was also the strangest thing about it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_2-1793351655278.mp3
Tom was thirty-two years old, and since the age of nine he had been able to feel what other people felt. Not guess, not imagine. Feel. When someone near him was angry, his own face became hot. When someone was afraid, his stomach went cold and tight. Happiness was like warm sun on the back of his neck. Love, which was rare and always a little surprising, tasted a bit like honey. And there was one more thing, the most important thing for a police officer: when someone told a lie, Tom heard it.
It was not a real sound. Nobody else in the room could hear it. But inside his head, a lie was like a false note, like a guitar string that was not quite in tune. Most of the time it was a very small note, because most lies were small. "I love your new haircut." "I'm fine, thanks." "The bus was late." People told dozens of these little lies every day, and Tom had learned to let them go past, like cars on a busy road.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_3-1793351670660.mp3
He got up, had a shower and made coffee. His flat was on the second floor of an old house in Bedminster, south of the river. It was small and quiet, and he liked it because there were only two other people in the building. Fewer people meant fewer feelings. In a big block of flats, with hundreds of families, he could not sleep at all. He had tried it once, when he was a young officer, and he had moved out after three weeks with dark circles under his eyes.
On the stairs he met Mr Okafor, who was carrying a bag of rubbish down to the bins. "Morning, Tom," said the old man. "Cold today, isn't it?" "Very cold," Tom agreed. He felt the grey sadness again, stronger now, so close. For a second he wanted to say something kind about Mrs Okafor. But he could not. Mr Okafor had never told him anything. If Tom spoke about it, the old man would ask how he knew, and Tom had no good answer to that question. He never had.

So instead he took the bag from Mr Okafor's hand. "Let me take that. You'll slip on the steps." The old man laughed and let him take it. A small, warm feeling came from him, like a match in a dark room. It was not much. But it was something, and Tom carried it with him as he walked to the bus stop. This was how he lived: he could not say what he knew, so he tried to do small things instead.
The bus to the city centre was full, as always. Tom stood near the back and held the yellow bar. Around him there were forty people, and each of them was a little radio, playing a different song. A student was nervous about an exam. A woman with a pushchair was so tired that it made Tom's own eyes heavy. Two builders in orange jackets were bored. Somebody was angry with somebody on the phone. And a teenage girl by the window was in love, so happily in love that the whole bus tasted faintly of honey.

When he was a child, crowds had frightened him. At school he had often hidden in the library at lunchtime, because the library was quiet and the playground was a storm. His mother had taken him to doctors. The doctors had said he was "a very sensitive boy" and given him exercises for breathing. The exercises did not stop the feelings, but they helped him to stay calm while the feelings passed through him. He still used them now, on buses and in shops and in rooms full of angry people.
He got off at Broadmead and walked the last part. He had ten minutes before he needed to be at the station, and he wanted to buy a new charger for his phone, because the old one had stopped working. There was a phone shop on the corner of Union Street. Tom did not like phone shops very much, and he knew exactly why. As soon as he pushed the glass door open, he felt it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_6-1793351713718.mp3
A young salesman with very white teeth and a badge that said JASON came towards him at once. "Good morning, sir! Are you looking for a new phone today? We have some amazing deals." He smiled widely. And from him came a feeling that Tom knew very well: a hungry, sticky feeling, like fingers that wanted to get into his pockets. It was greed. Not evil, not dangerous. Just greed, small and busy, the kind that grows in people who are paid for every sale.
"Just a charger, thanks," said Tom. "Of course, of course. But can I ask, how old is your phone? Because this week, with a new contract, you get the latest model for only thirty pounds a month." The sticky feeling became stronger. Tom smiled politely. "Just the charger." "And have you thought about insurance? Most people don't think about it until they drop their phone in the toilet. Ha ha!" The laugh was a false note, small and sharp. Jason did not think it was funny at all.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_7-1793351726820.mp3
"This charger is our best one," Jason continued, holding up a box. "Twenty-nine ninety-nine. Very fast. Much better than the cheap ones, which can damage your battery." Another false note, a bigger one this time. Tom looked at the shelf behind the young man and saw a smaller box for nine pounds. "I'll take that one," he said, pointing. Jason's disappointment hit him like cold water, and then, under it, Tom felt something else: worry. The young man was worried about money. Maybe he had rent to pay. Maybe his manager shouted at him.
This was the thing that nobody understood about his gift, the thing he could never explain. It did not make people simple. A salesman was greedy, yes, but he was also worried and tired and a little bit ashamed. Every person was a mix of many feelings, one on top of another, like layers in a cake. Tom paid for the cheap charger, and on his way out he said, "Good luck with your sales today." The young man looked surprised. Then, for a moment, he felt almost grateful.

Trinity Road police station was a large, ugly building from the 1970s, made of brown brick and dirty glass. Tom had worked there for six years, first in uniform and then as a detective constable in CID. He showed his card at the door, climbed the stairs to the second floor and walked into the big open office where the detectives worked. It was eight o'clock, and the room was already noisy, full of phones, keyboards and twenty people with twenty different moods.
"You're late," said a voice. "I'm early," said Tom. "You're late for you," said Detective Sergeant Nadia Cole, without looking up from her computer. She was thirty-five, small and quick, with short black hair and a pen that she always held between her teeth when she was thinking. She was Tom's partner, and she was also the only person in the world who knew his secret. Tom sat down at the desk opposite hers. From her he felt what he almost always felt: a calm, cool clearness, like a glass of water.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_9-1793351755177.mp3
This morning, under the clearness, there was something else. Tiredness, heavy and grey-blue. "You didn't sleep," he said quietly. Nadia took the pen out of her mouth and looked at him. "Don't do that," she said. "Do what?" "You know what. Read me like a newspaper before I've had my coffee." But she was not really angry; Tom could feel that too. "Ella had a bad dream at three o'clock," she admitted. "Then she wanted to talk about dinosaurs for an hour." Ella was her daughter. She was six.
Tom stood up, went to the little kitchen at the end of the office and came back with two cups of coffee. He put one on Nadia's desk. "Thank you," she said, and this time she smiled. Across the room, someone else was watching them: Detective Inspector Paul Harris, their boss, standing in the door of his glass office with his arms folded. Harris was fifty-five, big and grey, with a voice like a lorry. He believed in three things: evidence, paperwork and Bristol Rovers. In that order.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_10-1793351772444.mp3
"Reed! Cole!" he called. "My office. Now." They looked at each other and stood up. As they walked across the room, Tom felt Harris's mood coming towards him. Impatience, mostly, like an itch. Under that, a dull headache. And under that, something softer, something that Harris would never show to anyone: he was sorry for somebody. Somebody he had talked to this morning had made him feel sorry for them. Tom wondered who it was.
Harris closed the door behind them and sat down heavily. "Sit," he said. They sat. "Do you know Hartley's Bakery? On Gloucester Road?" "Everybody knows Hartley's," said Nadia. "Best cinnamon buns in Bristol." "Well, somebody has taken twenty thousand pounds out of their safe. Over the weekend. No broken windows, no broken door, no broken safe. The money was just gone when the manager opened up this morning." Harris rubbed his forehead. "The owner is Joan Hartley. She's eighty-one. She rang me herself. She was crying."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-quiet-signal/chapter_1.page_11-1793351784118.mp3
So that was it, thought Tom. That was the soft feeling. "She went to school with my mother," Harris added, a little embarrassed. "I've known her all my life. I want you two on this. Today. Go and see the place, talk to the staff, do it properly." He looked at Tom with his tired, heavy eyes. "Properly, Reed. Evidence. I don't want any of your famous hunches. Last time you had a hunch, I had to explain it to the Superintendent for forty minutes."
"It was a good hunch," said Tom. "It was right." "Being right is not enough," said Harris. "Being right with evidence is enough. A judge doesn't care about your feelings, son. Neither do I." He picked up his phone, which meant the meeting was finished. Outside his office, Nadia gave Tom a long look. "You heard the man," she said. "No hunches." "I never have hunches," said Tom. "I know," she said quietly. "That's what worries me."

They took Nadia's car, an old blue Ford that smelled of crisps and children's crayons. She drove north through the morning traffic, past Cabot Circus and up the hill towards Gloucester Road, with its long line of small shops, cafés and second-hand bookshops. Tom looked out of the window and did his breathing exercises. A new case always made him a little nervous. Each new case meant new people, and new people meant new feelings that he would have to sort, one by one, like letters in a post office.
"Are you OK?" asked Nadia, stopping at a red light. "Fine," said Tom. She looked at him. "That was a lie, wasn't it? Even I could hear that one." He laughed. "A small one." "Well, keep the small ones for other people," she said. "With me, you tell the truth. That was the deal." The light turned green. Ahead of them, on the left, Tom could already see a green sign with gold letters: HARTLEY'S BAKERY, SINCE 1962. There was a police car outside. And there was a smell of warm bread in the cold air.
