# The Hold Shelf

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-library-ghost/chapter_1.page_1-1793560169085.mp3
Every evening at six o'clock, when most students were heading back to their halls for dinner, Imogen Pryce walked the other way. She crossed the old stone bridge over the River Wear, climbed the steep cobbled street behind the cathedral, and pushed open the heavy oak door of Wexcombe Library. The building was nearly three hundred years old. It smelled of dust, floor polish and radiators that had been switched on too high, and for two years now it had paid her rent. Four evenings a week, from six until ten, Imogen sat behind the returns desk, scanned books, answered questions and tried very hard not to fall asleep.
She was twenty, in her second year of English Literature, and she had a talent for noticing things that other people missed. Her flatmate Hattie called it "the curse". Imogen noticed when a lecturer had changed his glasses. She noticed which students borrowed books they never opened, and which ones returned them with sand between the pages, as if they had been read on a beach in another life. And for the past five weeks, ever since the start of the autumn term, she had noticed the young man at the window table.

He arrived at half past six, always. He sat at the same table, the one in the corner of the reading room, where the tall window looked down over the river and the trees. He wore a dark green coat that was too thin for the north of England in October, and he never took it off. He did not bring a laptop, or a phone that he looked at, or a coffee that he was not allowed to drink. He brought a pencil, a notebook with a black cover, and whatever books he had reserved that week. Then he read, without moving, until exactly nine o'clock, when he stood up and left as quietly as he had come.
He never spoke to anyone. Imogen had heard his voice only once, when he had said "thanks" to her at the desk without looking up. It was a low voice, slightly rough, as if he did not use it very often. He was tall, with dark hair that needed cutting and a face that seemed permanently tired, but there was something in the way he read that she found impossible to ignore. He did not read like a student who had an essay to write. He read like someone who was searching for something, and who was afraid that he would never find it.

The rumours about him were everywhere, of course. Durham was a small city, and the university was even smaller. Some people said he had been thrown out of Oxford. Others said he was secretly rich, the son of a Hungarian count, and that the old coat was a disguise. One girl in Imogen's seminar group swore that he had been in prison. Hattie, who had a theory about everything, was convinced that he was a vampire. "Think about it," she said, eating cereal straight from the box. "He only comes out after dark. He's pale. He never eats. Classic."
"He eats," Imogen said. "I saw him with an apple once."
"Vampires can eat apples. It's a known fact." Hattie pointed the cereal box at her. "You like him."
"I don't even know him."
"That's never stopped anyone. You've talked about him three times this week. Four, if we count the coat." Hattie grinned. "I dare you to speak to him. One sentence. Any sentence. 'Nice weather.' 'Is this seat free?' 'Are you a vampire?'"
"Absolutely not," said Imogen, and she went to her room to read Middlemarch, and she did not think about him at all, except for most of the evening.

The truth was that Imogen was not shy, exactly. She could argue with a professor in a seminar. She could phone a stranger to complain about a broken washing machine. But there was a particular kind of situation that turned her into a statue: when something mattered to her and she had no idea how it would end. In those moments, her mind ran ahead of her and imagined every possible disaster, and by the time it came back, the moment had gone. Her mother used to say that Imogen thought so much before jumping that she usually missed the water.
On Tuesday evening, she tried. She waited until he came to the desk to collect his reserved books, and she prepared a sentence in her head: "You must be our best customer." It was friendly, light, slightly funny. She had practised it on the bridge. But when he stood in front of her, his hands in the pockets of the green coat and his eyes on the counter, the sentence dissolved like sugar in tea. She scanned his library card, handed him the books and said, "There you go." He said nothing at all. He nodded and walked back to his window, and Imogen wanted to hide under the desk until Christmas.

It was the hold shelf that gave her the idea. Behind the returns desk, against the wall, there was a long wooden shelf where reserved books waited for the people who had asked for them. Each book had a paper slip sticking out of the top with the reader's surname and the date. Imogen knew, because it was her job to know, that the slips with VARGA written on them appeared two or three times a week. She also knew, because she was Imogen, exactly what kind of books they were: old travel books, books of poems, history books about rivers, maps and coastlines. Never anything for a course. Never anything new.
On Thursday, a book arrived from the stores for him. It was called A Winter on the Northern Coast, by someone called E. M. Halloran, and it had been printed in 1931. Its cover was the colour of weak tea. Nobody had borrowed it for eleven years; the last date stamp on the inside page said so. Imogen was supposed to put it on the hold shelf and forget about it. Instead, during a quiet moment, she opened it. She told herself she was checking for damage. She was not checking for damage.

The book was beautiful in a strange, sad way. Halloran had spent a winter alone in a fishing village on the Northumberland coast after the death of his wife, and he wrote about the sea as if it were a person who had let him down. Imogen read the first chapter standing up, while a queue of two people waited politely. Then she turned to the middle, and on page 113 she found a sentence that made her stop: "We do not choose the people who will understand us; we only choose whether to let them."
She read it three times. Then she did something that she would later describe to Hattie as "temporary madness" and to herself, much later, as the bravest thing she had ever done. She took one of the library's paper bookmarks from the pot on the desk, the ones with a drawing of the cathedral on one side and the opening hours on the other. On the blank space under the opening hours, in small, careful handwriting, she wrote:
"Whoever you are: don't miss page 113. It's the best sentence in the book. Maybe the best sentence in the library."

She did not sign it. She slipped the bookmark between pages 112 and 113, closed the book, and put it on the hold shelf with his slip sticking out of the top, exactly like all the others. Her heart was beating so loudly that she was sure the man returning a pile of chemistry books could hear it. She scanned his chemistry books. She smiled at him. She had no idea what she had just said to him.
At half past six, the young man in the green coat came in, as regular as the cathedral bells. He walked to the desk, and Imogen went to the hold shelf and took down the book with VARGA on the slip, and she gave it to him without dropping it, which she considered a personal victory. He looked at the cover for a moment, and something in his face changed, very slightly, like a window when a light is switched on in a room far behind it. Then he took the book to his table and sat down.

For the next two hours, Imogen could not concentrate on anything. She put a book about Roman Britain on the shelf for German poetry. She told a first-year student that the library closed at eight, which was not true, and then had to run after him. Every few minutes, she looked across the reading room at the window table. He was reading slowly, from the beginning, the way people read books they respect. At a quarter past eight, he reached the middle. She saw him stop. She saw him pick up the bookmark and hold it under the lamp.
He read her words. Then he turned the page and read the sentence on page 113, and he stayed very still for a long time. Imogen held her breath. She expected him to look up, to search the room, to frown, perhaps to throw the bookmark in the bin. He did none of those things. He looked out of the window at the dark river for almost a minute. Then he put the bookmark carefully back between the pages, exactly where she had left it, and he went on reading.

At nine o'clock he left, as always, with the book under his arm. Imogen finished her shift, locked the returns box and walked home across the bridge in a cold wind that came straight off the hills. She was annoyed with herself, and pleased with herself, and confused about which feeling was stronger. Nothing had happened, really. He had read a note. He had not answered it. There was nothing he could answer, because he did not know who had written it. That was the point. That was the safe, clever, cowardly point.
"So?" said Hattie, when Imogen came in. "Did you talk to the vampire?"
"No," said Imogen, taking off her scarf. "I wrote to him."
Hattie put down her spoon. "You what?"
Imogen told her everything. When she finished, Hattie was silent for several seconds, which had never happened before. "That," she said at last, "is either the most romantic thing I've ever heard or the creepiest. I can't decide." She thought about it. "Romantic. Probably. As long as he never finds out you read his name on the slip."

Imogen laughed, but later, lying in bed and listening to the rain against the window, she thought about what Hattie had said. It was true. She knew his surname, and he did not know that she existed. She knew what he read and when he came. It did not feel like spying. It felt like noticing, which was what she always did. But she wondered, for the first time, whether there was a difference, and whether one day it would matter. Then she fell asleep, and dreamed of a cold beach and a man in a green coat, walking away along the sand.

# An Answer in Pencil

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-library-ghost/chapter_2.page_1-1793560315964.mp3
For four days, nothing happened. The young man in the green coat came on Friday and on Monday, read at his window table and left at nine, and he did not look at the returns desk once. A Winter on the Northern Coast did not come back. Imogen started to believe that the whole thing had been a mistake, a small, embarrassing secret that would stay between her, Hattie and a dead writer called E. M. Halloran. She told herself that this was a relief. On Monday night, she almost believed it.
On Tuesday, the returns box was full. It was a big metal box beside the front door, with a slot where people could drop books when the desk was closed, and every evening someone had to empty it, scan everything and put it on the trolley. It was the most boring job in the library, and usually Imogen left it for later. That evening, for no particular reason, she did it straight away. The Halloran book was the ninth one she picked up. She recognised the colour of the cover before she read the title, and her hands went cold.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-library-ghost/chapter_2.page_2-1793560335475.mp3
She did not open it immediately. She scanned it, put it on the trolley, scanned three more books and then took it back, as if she had only just noticed it. The bookmark was still there, between pages 112 and 113. Her own handwriting looked up at her, small and nervous. But underneath it, in pencil, there were new words, written in a hand that sloped to the right as if it were walking into a strong wind.
"You're right about page 113. But you're wrong about it being the best sentence. The best one is on page 201, and it's about herons. Nobody has borrowed this book for eleven years, so you must have read it here, which means you're either a member of staff or a ghost. I don't believe in ghosts. Whoever you are: thank you. I needed that sentence this week more than you can know."
Imogen read it so many times that she could have recited it in her sleep. Then she turned to page 201. Halloran was describing a grey heron that stood every morning in the shallow water at the mouth of a river, waiting for fish. "He has the patience of someone who has already lost everything," Halloran had written, "and has decided that he will not lose this as well." It was a good sentence. She had to admit it was better than hers. It was also, she thought, a slightly worrying sentence for someone to love.

He had guessed that she worked at the library. Of course he had; he was not stupid. But he had not guessed which member of staff she was. There were eleven people who worked at Wexcombe, including the cleaners and the two retired volunteers who repaired old books on Wednesday mornings. He could not possibly know that the note had come from the girl at the returns desk who had said "There you go" to him and nothing else. Imogen realised that she was smiling at a trolley of books, and stopped.
"You look like someone who's won the lottery," said a voice behind her. It was Mrs Dunmore, the head librarian, a small woman in her sixties with silver hair cut very short and reading glasses that hung from a chain around her neck. She had worked at Wexcombe for thirty-one years, and she treated the library the way some people treat a church. Imogen liked her enormously and was slightly afraid of her, which Mrs Dunmore seemed to consider the correct balance.
"Just a good book," said Imogen.
"There's no such thing as just a good book." Mrs Dunmore picked up the Halloran and looked at the date stamps with interest. "Eleven years. Poor thing. I'm glad someone remembered it."

Before she went back to her office, she tapped the computer screen with one finger, the way she always did when she was about to say something important. "Remember the training, Imogen. What our readers borrow is their business, not ours. We look at the records to do our job, and for no other reason. A library is the only place left where people can be curious without anyone watching them." She smiled and walked away, and Imogen felt her face grow hot, although she had not been accused of anything, and although Mrs Dunmore said those words to every new member of staff at least once a month.
The question now was what to do next. If she stopped, he would forget about it in a week. If she continued, she would need a way to send her answers, and there was only one. She checked the system. The young man had reserved another book, which was arriving from the stores on Thursday: a collection of poems by a Welsh poet she had never heard of. That night, she wrote her reply four times. The first version was too long. The second was too funny. The third was so serious that it frightened her. The fourth was the one she kept.

The poems came back on Saturday morning, in the returns box. Saturday was not one of Imogen's evenings, but she had offered to cover a morning shift for a colleague, and she was glad she had. The answer was on a sheet torn from the black notebook, folded twice and hidden inside the back cover. She read it in the staff kitchen, standing next to the kettle, with the door closed.

Imogen put the note in her pocket and kept her hand on it for the rest of the morning, as though it might fly away. She thought about the anniversary he did not want to explain. She thought about the hours he kept: six in the morning by the river, half past six in the evening in the library, nine o'clock exactly to leave. They were the hours of someone with a very strict life, or a very empty one, and she could not tell which. She also thought about her answer. One true thing that nobody in the city knew. It was a harder question than it looked.
The easy answers were not true enough. She hated mushrooms. She could not ride a bike. She had once cried at an advert for toilet paper. Those things were true, but Hattie knew all of them, and they did not cost anything to say. In the end, she wrote something she had never said out loud: "I want to write books. I've started eleven novels since I was fifteen. I've never finished one. Every time I get to the middle, I decide it isn't good enough and I stop. So here's my true thing: I'm studying literature because I'm afraid to make any."

She added her question underneath: "Does the heron have a name? If not, I think he deserves one." # Rules of the Game

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-library-ghost/chapter_3.page_1-1793560459803.mp3
The heron got his name on a wet Wednesday at the end of October. The atlas of sea charts came back with a long note inside, longer than any before, and the first line made Imogen laugh out loud in the staff kitchen. "You're right: he deserves a name. I've been calling him 'the heron' for a year, which is rude. I suggest Halloran, because he looks like a man who has lost his wife and decided to stare at the sea about it. If you disagree, you may propose an alternative. I warn you that he is very difficult to please."
Then the tone changed. "About your true thing. I've read it many times. I don't think you're afraid of writing. I think you're afraid of finishing, because as long as a book isn't finished, it can still be perfect. I know that feeling. I've been not finishing something for a year now. Here's my question: what is the eleventh novel about? Not the first ten. The last one. The one you abandoned most recently. I'd like to know what you were brave enough to start."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-library-ghost/chapter_3.page_2-1793560476190.mp3
Imogen read the note on the bus to her Thursday seminar and missed her stop. She had expected him to be kind about her confession; people were usually kind about other people's failures, in a polite, distant way. She had not expected him to understand it better than she did. As long as a book isn't finished, it can still be perfect. It was so obviously true that she was annoyed she had never thought of it herself. She walked back from the wrong stop in the rain, and she did not mind the rain at all.
Her answer took her two evenings to write. The eleventh novel was about a lighthouse keeper on a small island off the coast of Cornwall, in 1911, who receives letters from a woman he has never met. The letters arrive with the supply boat every two weeks. They are funny and clever and sometimes sad, and he begins to live for them, until one day the boat arrives with no letter at all. "That's where I stopped," she wrote. "Page sixty-three. He's standing on the jetty, and the boat is empty, and I didn't know what happened next. I still don't."

She only realised after she had put the note in his book what she had written. A man who receives letters from a woman he has never met. She had started that novel in April, months before she had ever seen the young man in the green coat. It was a coincidence. It was obviously a coincidence. But she knew exactly what he would think when he read it, and she spent the whole of Friday wishing that she could climb onto the hold shelf, take the note back and replace it with something about the weather.
His reply was waiting for her on Monday, inside a book about the history of Scottish lighthouses. She had not even known that such a book existed in Wexcombe. He must have searched the catalogue for it on purpose. "I promise I'm not laughing," the note began, which immediately made her suspect that he had been. "I know you started that story before you started writing to me. I'm not that vain. But I think you should know what happens on page sixty-four, and I think the only way to find out is to write it. I'm giving you this book as research. You have no excuse now."

So she wrote page sixty-four. Then sixty-five, and sixty-six. She wrote in the mornings before her lectures and at night after her shifts, sitting on her bed with her laptop balanced on a pillow. In her story, the lighthouse keeper took the supply boat back to the mainland for the first time in three years and went looking for the woman who had stopped writing. Imogen did not know if he would find her. For once, not knowing felt like a reason to continue rather than a reason to stop.
Hattie noticed before anyone else. She came home one night at midnight and found Imogen at the kitchen table, typing so fast that the laptop was shaking, with a cold cup of tea beside her and the book about Scottish lighthouses open at a diagram of a lamp room. Hattie stood in the doorway for a while without saying anything. Then she made two fresh cups of tea, put one next to Imogen's hand and went to bed without a single joke, which was, Imogen realised the next morning, the kindest thing she had ever done.
The next day, Hattie made up for it by calling the laptop "your boyfriend" for an entire week.

The notes became a habit, and then a rhythm, like a heartbeat. He reserved two or three books a week; she answered each of his notes in the next one he collected. They kept the rules. One question each, answered honestly. No names. No courses, they decided after the second week, because a course was almost a name: there were only so many people studying any subject in a university as small as Durham. They were allowed to talk about anything else, and they did.
She learned that he hated the smell of hospitals and loved the smell of bread. She learned that he had grown up somewhere near the sea, that he could not swim, and that he found this funny in a way he did not explain. She learned that he had read every book in Wexcombe about rivers, because rivers were the only things he knew that were always leaving and always staying at the same time. He learned that she was the youngest of three sisters, that she sang badly in the shower, and that she had once been sick on a school trip to a chocolate factory and had not eaten chocolate for a year.

One evening, she asked the question she had wanted to ask from the beginning. "Why do you always leave at exactly nine o'clock? I've noticed. Everyone who works here has noticed. You could set a clock by you." His answer came back two days later, and it was the shortest note he had ever written. "Because someone is waiting for me, and I promised I'd never be late again. That's all I can say for now. Sorry."
Imogen read those two lines and felt something cold and small move in her stomach. Someone is waiting for me. A girlfriend, obviously. A girlfriend who lived in the city, perhaps in a little flat near the station, who cooked him dinner at nine and did not know that he spent his evenings writing to a stranger in library books. She was furious with herself for being upset. She had no right to be upset. She had never even told him her name.
"Or," said Hattie that night, lying upside down on the sofa with her feet on the wall, "his mum. Or his dog. Or a very demanding cat. Or his vampire master, who needs to be fed at nine o'clock precisely, otherwise he goes crazy and eats a tourist." She turned her head to look at Imogen. "You don't know anything, Im.
