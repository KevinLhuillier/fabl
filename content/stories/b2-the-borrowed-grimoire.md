# The Wrong Shelf

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-borrowed-grimoire/chapter_1.page_1-1792856976587.mp3
Aubrecourt was the kind of town people drove through on their way to somewhere else. Two slag heaps rose above the rooftops like the backs of sleeping animals, black in winter and covered with thin green grass in spring. The mines had closed forty years earlier, but the town still lived in their shadow: rows of identical red-brick houses, a church that was far too big for the people who still went to it, and a main street where every third shop had a "For Rent" sign in the window.
Noé Garnier had lived there all his life, and at seventeen he had already worked out that Aubrecourt had two kinds of people: those who were planning to leave, and those who had given up on the idea. He belonged firmly to the first group. He just didn't know yet where he would go, or how.

At Lycée Émile-Zola, the four of them had a table. Not an official one, of course, but everyone knew that the small table at the back of the canteen, next to the radiator that never worked, belonged to "the freaks". That was the word Maxence Laborde had chosen for them back in collège, and it had stuck the way chewing gum sticks to the sole of a shoe.
That Tuesday, Inès had her laptop open next to her tray, as usual, and was explaining to nobody in particular why the latest update of their favourite online game had wrecked its economy. Arthur, who was nearly two metres tall but spoke so quietly that people often asked him to repeat himself, was rolling a twenty-sided die between his fingers while he planned Saturday's role-playing session. Adam was drawing. Adam was always drawing. His sketchbook, held together with tape, contained three years of characters, cities and monsters, and a manga he had been working on since he was fourteen.

"If they nerf the blacksmiths again, I'm uninstalling," Inès said.
"You said that last month," Noé pointed out.
"And I meant it last month too."
Noé was still smiling when he felt the room change. It was always the same: a sudden quiet at the tables nearby, a few phones coming out of pockets, faces turning with that particular expression, half nervous and half excited, which meant that something was about to happen to someone else.
Maxence arrived with his usual escort, Lucas and Romain, two boys who laughed at his jokes slightly before he had finished them. He was tall, good-looking in the way of people who have never had to worry about anything, and his father was the deputy mayor of Aubrecourt. Everyone knew it, especially the teachers.
"Hey, Picasso," he said, and before Adam could react, the sketchbook was in his hands.
"Give it back," said Noé, getting to his feet.

Maxence ignored him. He flicked through the pages slowly, the way you might look through the menu of a restaurant you didn't like. "Oh, look. Another girl with giant eyes. Do you draw these because no real girl will ever look at you?"
Romain burst out laughing. A few people at the next table laughed too, then looked away, embarrassed by their own laughter.
Arthur stood up. For a second he was taller than anyone in the canteen, and Noé saw a flicker of doubt cross Maxence's face. Then Arthur opened his mouth, and nothing came out. His hands were shaking. He sat down again, his face bright red, and stared at his tray.
"That's what I thought," said Maxence.

He strolled to the door that led to the courtyard, where it had been raining all morning, and dropped the sketchbook into the largest puddle he could find. Then he stepped on it. Once, carefully, as if he wanted to be sure.
On the other side of the canteen, Monsieur Delattre, the supervisor, was looking at his phone with great concentration.
Adam didn't say a word for the rest of the afternoon. He dried the pages one by one on the radiator in the maths classroom, the one that actually worked, but the ink had run, and three years of work looked as if they had been left out in a storm. When Inès offered to scan what was left and try to restore it, he just shrugged.
"It doesn't matter," he said, which was how Noé knew that it mattered more than anything.

After school, Noé didn't feel like going home. His mother was working a late shift at the hospital, and the flat would be empty and silent. So he walked to the médiathèque instead, telling himself that he needed sources for his history presentation on the miners' strikes.
The médiathèque occupied the old offices of the mining company, a heavy brick building with tall windows and an iron staircase that rang under your feet. Above the main entrance, half hidden behind a modern sign, you could still read the original words carved in stone: COMPAGNIE DES MINES D'AUBRECOURT. Noé had walked past them a thousand times without really seeing them.

The local history section was on the top floor, at the end of a corridor nobody seemed to use. He found what he was looking for quickly: two books on the strikes of 1948 and a collection of old photographs. He was about to leave when he noticed a book that didn't belong.
It was wedged between a guide to regional birds and a dictionary of the Picard dialect, and it looked older than both of them put together. The cover was dark leather, worn smooth at the corners, with no title and no barcode. When Noé pulled it out, it was heavier than he had expected, and strangely warm, as if someone had been holding it a moment before.
Inside, on the first page, someone had drawn a small open eye in faded brown ink. Below it, in elegant, old-fashioned handwriting, were two lines:
This book belongs to no one. It is lent to those who know how to read.

Tucked inside the back cover was an old library card, the kind with dates stamped in purple ink. There was only one date on it, 14 March 1987, and next to it a name that had been scratched out so violently that the pen had torn through the paper.
At the front desk, the librarian scanned the books on the strikes, then turned the leather book over twice, looking for a label.
"That's not one of ours," she said, frowning at her screen. "It isn't in the system at all. Probably a donation someone forgot to register." She slid it back towards him with a tired smile. "Take it, if you like. Just bring it back when you're done. Somebody will sort it out eventually."

That night, alone in his room, Noé opened the book on his desk under the lamp. The pages were thick and slightly yellowed, covered in the same handwriting as the inscription, with diagrams of hands in precise positions, arrows showing movements, and words in a language he didn't recognise. It wasn't Latin, he was almost sure of that. It looked older.
The first chapter had a title he could read, written in French at the top of the page: Of the First Gesture. And beneath it, a single sentence, underlined twice:
Magic is not a gift. It is a craft. Whoever learns it, possesses it.
Noé read it three times. He thought about the sketchbook in the puddle, about Arthur's shaking hands, about Monsieur Delattre staring at his phone. He thought about Maxence's father, whose smiling face was on posters all over town.

Then, almost without deciding to, he copied the first diagram with his right hand: thumb against middle finger, palm turned towards the lamp, a slow half-circle from left to right. He whispered the three words written beside it, sounding them out syllable by syllable, like a player trying a cheat code he didn't really believe in.
The lamp flickered.
Only once, very briefly. It could have been the old wiring in the building; that happened sometimes. But at that exact moment, a wave of exhaustion hit Noé so hard that he had to grab the edge of the desk. His heart was racing. His legs felt heavy, as if he had just climbed all three hundred steps of the slag heap behind the school.
He sat very still for a long time, breathing slowly, looking at his hand.
Then he took a notebook out of his bag, opened it at a clean page, and wrote at the top, in capital letters: TEST 1.
