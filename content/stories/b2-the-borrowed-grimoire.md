# The Wrong Shelf

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-borrowed-grimoire/chapter_1.page_1-1792856976587.mp3
Aubrecourt was the kind of town people drove through on their way to somewhere else. Two slag heaps rose above the rooftops like the backs of sleeping animals, black in winter and covered with thin green grass in spring. The mines had closed forty years earlier, but the town still lived in their shadow: rows of identical red-brick houses, a church that was far too big for the people who still went to it, and a main street where every third shop had a "For Rent" sign in the window.
Noé Garnier had lived there all his life, and at seventeen he had already worked out that Aubrecourt had two kinds of people: those who were planning to leave, and those who had given up on the idea. He belonged firmly to the first group. He just didn't know yet where he would go, or how.

The local history section was on the top floor, at the end of a corridor nobody seemed to use. He found what he was looking for quickly: two books on the strikes of 1948 and a collection of old photographs. He was about to leave when he noticed a book that didn't belong.
It was wedged between a guide to regional birds and a dictionary of the Picard dialect, and it looked older than both of them put together. The cover was dark leather, worn smooth at the corners, with no title and no barcode. When Noé pulled it out, it was heavier than he had expected, and strangely warm, as if someone had been holding it a moment before.
Inside, on the first page, someone had drawn a small open eye in faded brown ink. Below it, in elegant, old-fashioned handwriting, were two lines:
This book belongs to no one. It is lent to those who know how to read.

That night, alone in his room, Noé opened the book on his desk under the lamp. The pages were thick and slightly yellowed, covered in the same handwriting as the inscription, with diagrams of hands in precise positions, arrows showing movements, and words in a language he didn't recognise. It wasn't Latin, he was almost sure of that. It looked older.
The first chapter had a title he could read, written in French at the top of the page: Of the First Gesture. And beneath it, a single sentence, underlined twice:
Magic is not a gift. It is a craft. Whoever learns it, possesses it.
Noé read it three times. He thought about the sketchbook in the puddle, about Arthur's shaking hands, about Monsieur Delattre staring at his phone. He thought about Maxence's father, whose smiling face was on posters all over town.

Then, almost without deciding to, he copied the first diagram with his right hand: thumb against middle finger, palm turned towards the lamp, a slow half-circle from left to right. He whispered the three words written beside it, sounding them out syllable by syllable, like a player trying a cheat code he didn't really believe in.
The lamp flickered.
Only once, very briefly. It could have been the old wiring in the building; that happened sometimes. But at that exact moment, a wave of exhaustion hit Noé so hard that he had to grab the edge of the desk. His heart was racing. His legs felt heavy, as if he had just climbed all three hundred steps of the slag heap behind the school.
He sat very still for a long time, breathing slowly, looking at his hand.
Then he took a notebook out of his bag, opened it at a clean page, and wrote at the top, in capital letters: TEST 1.
