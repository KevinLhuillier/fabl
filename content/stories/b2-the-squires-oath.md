# The Squire

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_1.page_1-1792829569414.mp3
Long before the sun had risen over the hills of Ashford, Edwin was already awake.
For four years he had served as squire to Sir Godfrey, a knight whose name was respected throughout the county, and every one of those mornings had begun in exactly the same way.
He fed the horses, carried water from the well and polished his master's boots until he could almost see his own reflection in the leather.
At sixteen, he was taller than most of the servants in the castle, but he still felt like the nervous boy who had arrived there with nothing but a thin cloak and a borrowed knife.
His father, a poor farmer, had sold two cows to pay for the journey.
"Serve him well," his mother had whispered as he left. "One day, you may become more than we ever were."
Edwin had never forgotten those words.

After breakfast, Sir Godfrey called him to the courtyard for sword practice.
The old knight moved slowly these days, but his blade was still faster than most young men's.
"You are attacking like an angry bull," Godfrey said, after knocking Edwin's sword out of his hand for the third time. "Strength is useless if you cannot control it."
Edwin picked up his weapon, his face red with frustration.
"Then how am I supposed to win, sir?"
"By waiting," the knight replied calmly. "Watch your opponent. Every man reveals his intentions before he strikes. His shoulders move, his eyes change, his feet prepare. If you are patient enough to notice, you will always be one step ahead."
They practised until noon. By the end, Edwin had not won a single fight, but for the first time he had managed to block every attack.
Godfrey nodded, which, from him, was almost a compliment.

In the afternoon, Edwin sat in the armoury, cleaning the rust from his master's chain mail.
Godfrey sat beside him, which was unusual, and for a while neither of them spoke.
"Do you know how I got this?" the knight finally asked, touching the long scar that crossed his left cheek.
Edwin shook his head. Everyone in the castle had a different theory, but nobody had ever dared to ask.
"I was twenty, and I believed that being a knight meant winning. I chased a defeated enemy into a forest because I wanted the glory of capturing him. He was waiting for me behind a tree."
Godfrey smiled sadly.
"A knight is not the man who wins every battle, Edwin. He is the man who knows which battles are worth fighting. Remember that, because one day nobody will be there to remind you."

# The King's Letter

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_2.page_1-1792829602615.mp3
Three days later, a messenger arrived at the gates, covered in mud and exhausted from riding through the night.
He carried a letter sealed with the red wax of the king himself.
Sir Godfrey read it twice before he spoke.
"His Majesty is holding a great tournament at Winchester at the end of the month. Every knight in the kingdom has been invited to take part."
He paused, and his expression became more serious.
"But there is something else. Bandits have been attacking villages along the southern road. Several farms have been burned, and the king's soldiers have failed to catch the men responsible."
Edwin felt a mixture of excitement and fear. He had never been further than the nearest market town.
"Prepare the horses and pack the armour," Godfrey ordered. "We leave at dawn. And Edwin, sharpen your own sword as well. You may need it."

The road to Winchester was longer and harder than Edwin had imagined.
For five days they travelled through thick forests and across cold rivers, sleeping under the stars when there was no village nearby.
It rained almost constantly. Edwin's boots were soaked, his back ached from carrying their equipment, and his hands were covered in blisters.
Sir Godfrey never complained, so Edwin decided that he would not complain either.
On the fourth day, they passed a farm that had been completely destroyed. Only the stone walls remained, black with smoke, and a broken cart lay abandoned in the field.
Godfrey stopped his horse and stared at the ruins for a long time.
"Whoever did this was not hungry," he said quietly. "They took nothing. They only wanted to frighten people."
Edwin did not understand what he meant, but he felt a cold shiver run down his spine.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_2.page_3-1792829623533.mp3
That evening, they found shelter at a small inn called the Crooked Crown.
The innkeeper's daughter, a sharp-eyed girl named Maud, brought them bread, cheese and a pot of hot stew.
While Godfrey went to see to the horses, she sat down opposite Edwin and lowered her voice.
"You are going to the tournament, aren't you? Be careful. People say the bandits are not ordinary thieves."
"What do you mean?" Edwin asked.
"They have new weapons and good horses. Last week, my brother saw one of them pay for his drinks with a silver coin. Since when do bandits have silver?"
She glanced nervously towards the door.
"Someone rich is paying them. Someone who wants the southern villages to be afraid."
Edwin wanted to ask more questions, but at that moment Godfrey came back, and Maud quickly returned to the kitchen.

# The Tournament

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_3.page_1-1792829633820.mp3
Winchester was unlike anything Edwin had ever seen.
Hundreds of colourful tents had been set up outside the city walls, and the air was filled with music, laughter and the smell of roasting meat.
Knights from every corner of the kingdom paraded through the crowd, their shields painted with lions, eagles and dragons.
Among them, one man attracted more attention than the others: Sir Hugh of Blackmoor, a tall knight in silver armour who owned vast lands in the south.
"He has won the last three tournaments," a merchant explained to Edwin. "Some say he will be the king's next adviser."
When Sir Hugh passed in front of them, he looked at Godfrey with open contempt.
"I am surprised to see you here, old man," he said. "I thought you had retired to grow cabbages."
Godfrey simply smiled and bowed his head, but Edwin noticed that his hand had tightened around the hilt of his sword.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_3.page_2-1792829643639.mp3
On the second day of the tournament, Godfrey and Sir Hugh faced each other in the final joust.
The crowd fell silent as the two horses began to gallop towards each other, the ground shaking beneath their hooves.
On the first pass, both lances broke against the shields. On the second, Godfrey struck Hugh so hard that the younger knight almost fell from his saddle.
The spectators cheered. For a moment, Edwin was certain that his master was going to win.
Then, on the third pass, something went terribly wrong.
Just before the impact, Godfrey's saddle suddenly slipped to one side. He lost his balance, Hugh's lance hit him in the chest, and the old knight crashed to the ground.
He did not get up.
Edwin jumped over the wooden barrier and ran towards him, ignoring the guards who were shouting at him to stop.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_3.page_3-1792829654447.mp3
Godfrey was alive, but his leg was badly broken and he could barely breathe.
While the doctors carried him to his tent, Edwin went back to examine the horse. Something about the fall had seemed strange to him.
He knew that saddle better than anyone. He had checked every buckle and every strap that morning, as he always did.
When he lifted the leather, his heart started to pound.
The main strap had not torn. It had been cut, cleanly and deliberately, almost all the way through, so that it would break under pressure.
Edwin looked around. Not far away, Hugh's squire, a thin young man with nervous eyes, was watching him. As soon as their eyes met, the squire turned and disappeared into the crowd.
Edwin's hands were trembling. Someone had tried to kill his master, and he was almost sure he knew who.

# Without Proof

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_4.page_1-1792829665968.mp3
That evening, Edwin asked to speak to the king.
Everyone told him it was impossible, that a squire had no right to address His Majesty directly. But Edwin refused to leave, and in the end, the king agreed to see him.
Standing in the royal tent, surrounded by lords in fine clothes, Edwin showed them the cut strap.
"My master did not fall by accident, Your Majesty. Someone wanted him to lose, or worse."
Sir Hugh, who was present, laughed loudly.
"A clumsy squire forgets to check his master's saddle, and now he invents a conspiracy to protect himself. How convenient."
Several lords laughed with him. The king, however, studied the strap carefully.
"This is a serious accusation," he said at last. "Without proof, I cannot punish a knight of the realm. But I will not forget what you have shown me."

Two days later, as Godfrey and Edwin began the slow journey home in a hired cart, they met a group of terrified travellers coming from the south.
"Don't go that way!" a woman shouted. "The bandits are heading for Little Fenwick. They say they will burn it tonight!"
Edwin knew the name. Little Fenwick was a village of farmers and shepherds, less than an hour away, with no walls and no soldiers to protect it.
He turned towards his master. Godfrey was pale with pain, his leg tied between two wooden boards.
"I cannot fight, Edwin," the old knight said. "Not like this."
For a long moment, neither of them spoke. Then Godfrey looked him straight in the eyes.
"But you can. Take my sword. A knight is the man who knows which battles are worth fighting. This one is."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_4.page_3-1792829686441.mp3
When Edwin arrived at Little Fenwick, the villagers were already packing their belongings, ready to flee into the forest.
"Running will not save you," he told them, trying to sound more confident than he felt. "If you leave, they will burn everything you own. If we stay together, we have a chance."
At first, nobody listened to him. He was only a boy with a sword that was too big for him.
Then an old shepherd stepped forward. "The lad is right," he said. "My grandfather built this house. I will not let it burn."
For the rest of the afternoon, Edwin organised the defence. The men dug a ditch across the main road and filled it with dry branches. The women gathered buckets of water in case of fire. The children were sent to hide in the church, which had the thickest walls in the village.
As the sun went down, Edwin remembered Godfrey's lesson. Watch, wait and be patient.

# Fire in the Night

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_5.page_1-1792829697462.mp3
The bandits came shortly after midnight, about fifteen men on horseback, carrying torches.
They expected an easy victory. Instead, as they galloped down the main road, Edwin gave the signal, and the villagers set fire to the branches in the ditch.
A wall of flames rose in front of the horses. The animals panicked, throwing several riders to the ground, while the others tried desperately to turn back.
In the confusion, the villagers attacked with pitchforks, clubs and axes.
Edwin found himself face to face with a huge man armed with a heavy axe. He was terrified, but he forced himself to watch his opponent, exactly as Godfrey had taught him.
The man's shoulders moved before every blow. Edwin stepped aside, again and again, until the bandit grew tired and careless. Then, with one quick strike, Edwin knocked the axe out of his hands.
Within minutes, the remaining bandits had fled into the darkness.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_5.page_2-1792829708988.mp3
When the fighting was over, the villagers dragged one of the prisoners into the light of the fire. He was a boy, younger than Edwin, with a bleeding arm and eyes full of fear.
"Hang him!" someone shouted. "Hang him from the oak tree, as a warning to the others!"
Several voices agreed. A rope was brought.
Edwin stepped between the boy and the crowd.
"No," he said firmly. "We are not bandits. If we kill a prisoner who cannot defend himself, we are no better than the men who attacked us."
The villagers hesitated, then slowly lowered their weapons.
The boy stared at Edwin in disbelief. Then, to everyone's surprise, he began to cry.
"I never wanted to burn anything," he sobbed. "They promised us money. A knight paid our leader. A tall knight in silver armour. I saw him myself, in the forest, three nights ago."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_5.page_3-1792829723202.mp3
A week later, Edwin stood once again in front of the king, this time with Sir Godfrey sitting beside him on a wooden chair, and the young bandit kneeling at his side.
The boy told his story to the whole court: the meetings in the forest, the bags of silver, the orders to burn the southern villages so that frightened landowners would sell their farms cheaply to the lord of Blackmoor.
When he had finished, there was complete silence.
Sir Hugh's face had turned white. He tried to protest, but then his own squire stepped forward and confessed that he had cut Godfrey's saddle strap on his master's orders.
The king rose slowly from his throne.
"Sir Hugh of Blackmoor, you have betrayed your oath, your king and the people you were supposed to protect. Your lands are taken from you, and you will leave this kingdom forever."
As the guards led Hugh away, the king turned towards Edwin, and for the first time, he smiled.

# Sir Edwin

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_6.page_1-1792829734631.mp3
According to tradition, a squire must spend the night before his knighthood alone in a chapel, praying and reflecting on the duties he is about to accept.
Edwin knelt in front of the altar, wearing a simple white tunic, his sword lying on the cold stone in front of him.
The candles flickered, and the hours passed slowly. His knees hurt, and twice he almost fell asleep.
He thought about his mother's words, about the burned farm on the road, about the frightened boy he had saved from the rope.
He realised that he was no longer afraid of fighting. What frightened him now was something else: the responsibility of deciding, every day, what was right.
Just before dawn, he heard the door open behind him. Sir Godfrey limped inside, leaning on a stick, and knelt painfully beside him.
"Nobody should spend this night completely alone," the old knight murmured. "Not even a hero."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-the-squires-oath/chapter_6.page_2-1792829745032.mp3
The ceremony took place in the great hall of the castle, in front of the king, the lords of the realm and, to Edwin's astonishment, his own parents, whom the king had invited from Ashford.
Edwin knelt before the throne. The king raised his sword and touched him lightly on each shoulder.
"Do you swear to protect the weak, to defend the truth, and to show mercy even to your enemies?"
"I swear it," Edwin answered, his voice steady.
"Then rise, Sir Edwin of Ashford."
The hall erupted with applause. Sir Godfrey fastened a pair of golden spurs to Edwin's boots, a gift that had once belonged to Godfrey's own master.
Edwin looked at his mother. She was crying, but she was smiling too.
He understood, at that moment, that her words had finally come true.

Many years later, people across the kingdom still told the story of the night at Little Fenwick.
Sir Edwin became one of the most respected knights in England, known not for the tournaments he won, but for the battles he chose to fight.
He never forgot where he had come from. When he received lands of his own, he lowered the taxes of every farmer who lived on them, and he built a school in the village where he had been born.
And every morning, long before the sun rose, he could be found in the courtyard, teaching his own squire how to hold a sword.
"You are attacking like an angry bull," he would say, smiling at the memory. "Strength is useless if you cannot control it."
Sir Godfrey, who lived to a very old age, watched them from his window and laughed.
