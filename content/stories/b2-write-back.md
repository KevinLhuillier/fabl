# The Green Notebook

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_1-1793197545633.mp3
On her ninth day in Durham, Rosalind Hale counted the words she had spoken out loud since breakfast. It did not take long. There had been "thank you" to the woman in the café, "sorry" to a boy whose bag she had touched on the stairs, and four more words in the seminar room, which she was trying very hard not to think about. Eleven words. Twelve, if she counted the "oh" she had whispered when she missed the bus. It was not a record she was proud of.
It was the end of September, and the city was full of people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing. They walked in groups across the old bridges, laughing, carrying pizza boxes and bottles and enormous plans for the evening. Rosalind walked alone, close to the walls, with her hands deep in the pockets of a coat that was too warm for the weather. She had chosen it because it had big pockets. Big pockets were useful when you did not know what to do with your hands.

She came from Kettlewell, a village in the Yorkshire Dales with one shop, two pubs, a river called the Wharfe and more sheep than people. Everybody there knew her. Everybody knew that she was "the clever Hale girl", the one who read books at the bus stop and won the poetry prize at school three years in a row. Nobody there expected her to talk very much, and that had suited her perfectly. In Kettlewell, being quiet was simply part of who she was, like her red hair or her grandad's old watch on her wrist.

In Durham, nobody knew her, and being quiet was not a quality. It was a problem. It was the reason she had spent the whole of Freshers' Week in her room with a packet of biscuits, listening to the parties through the walls. It was the reason her phone had only three new numbers in it, and one of them was the university library. And it was the reason for what had happened that morning, in Room 14 of the English department, in her very first seminar.

The tutor was a small, sharp woman called Dr Margaret Pell, who wore large silver earrings and looked at people as if she could read the footnotes in their heads. "Let's go round the table," she had said. "Your name, where you're from, and one book that changed your life. Nothing too long." Twelve students had smiled. Rosalind had felt her stomach turn into a cold stone. She had read hundreds of books. She could have talked for an hour about any one of them, in her head. But her head and her mouth were two different countries, and the border between them was closed.

The students spoke one after another. A tall boy with a Scottish accent said something about a novel by Muriel Spark, and everybody laughed at his joke. Rosalind had kept diaries before, of course. Her grandad Alf had given her the first one when she was eight. But tonight, walking home in the rain with the green notebook under her coat, she thought she might try again.

# The Rules

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_2.page_1-1793199812425.mp3
When Rosalind woke up, the notebook was still on her pillow and the sun was shining through the thin curtains in a way that made everything look ordinary. For a few seconds she was sure she had dreamt it all. Then she saw the small green mark on the tip of her finger, and her stomach dropped. She sat up, opened the notebook and looked at the first page for a long time. The green words had not disappeared in the night. They looked even more real in daylight.

She was a sensible person. Everybody said so: her teachers, her mum, the woman at the post office in Kettlewell. Sensible people did not believe in magic notebooks. Sensible people made experiments. So she took her pen, turned to the second page, and decided to ask questions, the way a scientist or a detective would. She wrote slowly and carefully, pressing hard, as if the pen could hold her steady.
> Who are you?
> Are you a ghost?
> Grandad, is it you?

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_2.page_3-1793199834075.mp3
She waited. Nothing happened. She waited longer, counting her breaths, and began to feel foolish. Perhaps the thing only worked at night. Perhaps it had been a hallucination after all, and she should phone her mum and say she wanted to come home. > I'm not a ghost. I'm not your grandad either. I'm sorry. I know you hoped I was. I miss him too.
> I can't tell you who I am. Not yet. Please don't ask me every day, because the answer will be the same, and we'll waste a lot of paper.
> — R.

"I miss him too." Rosalind read the line again and frowned. How could somebody miss Grandad Alf if they were not from Kettlewell? There was only one place to look for an explanation, and it was at the top of Saddler Street.

# Small Steps

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_3.page_1-1793200801528.mp3
The week went by faster than Rosalind wanted it to. Every morning she woke up and thought, "Friday," and every night she went to bed and thought, "Friday," and in between she did all the normal things a student does: she went to lectures, she read in the library, she ate toast standing up in the kitchen. But the word was always there, at the back of her mind, like a stone in her shoe. One full sentence. To another student. Something true.
On Tuesday night she wrote to R. She did not mean to complain, but the words came out that way, the way water comes out of a tap that has been turned too far.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_3.page_2-1793200815870.mp3
> I can't do it. I've tried to imagine it a hundred times and every time my mouth goes dry and my mind goes white. It's not that I have nothing to say. It's that when everybody looks at me, the words run away and hide.
> How do other people do it? Callum just opens his mouth and jokes come out.
> Callum practises more than you think. Everybody does. Confident people aren't born with a different brain. They've just had more rehearsals.
> So rehearse. Read the poem for Friday. Find one thing you really think about it. Write the sentence here, exactly as you'll say it. Then say it out loud ten times a day. In the shower is good: Ines sings so loudly she won't hear a thing.
> Words that have been practised don't run away so easily. They know the way out.
