# The Green Notebook

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_1-1793197545633.mp3
On her ninth day in Durham, Rosalind Hale counted the words she had spoken out loud since breakfast. It did not take long. There had been "thank you" to the woman in the café, "sorry" to a boy whose bag she had touched on the stairs, and four more words in the seminar room, which she was trying very hard not to think about. Eleven words. Twelve, if she counted the "oh" she had whispered when she missed the bus. It was not a record she was proud of.
It was the end of September, and the city was full of people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing. They walked in groups across the old bridges, laughing, carrying pizza boxes and bottles and enormous plans for the evening. Rosalind walked alone, close to the walls, with her hands deep in the pockets of a coat that was too warm for the weather. She had chosen it because it had big pockets. Big pockets were useful when you did not know what to do with your hands.

She came from Kettlewell, a village in the Yorkshire Dales with one shop, two pubs, a river called the Wharfe and more sheep than people. Everybody there knew her. Everybody knew that she was "the clever Hale girl", the one who read books at the bus stop and won the poetry prize at school three years in a row. Nobody there expected her to talk very much, and that had suited her perfectly. In Kettlewell, being quiet was simply part of who she was, like her red hair or her grandad's old watch on her wrist.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_3-1793197564049.mp3
In Durham, nobody knew her, and being quiet was not a quality. It was a problem. It was the reason she had spent the whole of Freshers' Week in her room with a packet of biscuits, listening to the parties through the walls. It was the reason her phone had only three new numbers in it, and one of them was the university library. And it was the reason for what had happened that morning, in Room 14 of the English department, in her very first seminar.

The tutor was a small, sharp woman called Dr Margaret Pell, who wore large silver earrings and looked at people as if she could read the footnotes in their heads. "Let's go round the table," she had said. "Your name, where you're from, and one book that changed your life. Nothing too long." Twelve students had smiled. Rosalind had felt her stomach turn into a cold stone. She had read hundreds of books. She could have talked for an hour about any one of them, in her head. But her head and her mouth were two different countries, and the border between them was closed.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_5-1793197584049.mp3
The students spoke one after another. A tall boy with a Scottish accent said something about a novel by Muriel Spark, and everybody laughed at his joke. A girl from London talked about poetry and used the word "liminal" twice. Then there was a silence, and Rosalind understood that the silence was for her. Twelve faces turned towards her. Her cheeks became hot. "My name is... Rosalind," she said. "Sorry." And that was all. Dr Pell waited for a second, nodded kindly, which was somehow worse, and moved on to the next student.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_6-1793197591944.mp3
For the rest of the hour, Rosalind did not hear a single word. She stared at the notes in front of her and wrote nothing. When the seminar ended, she was the first to leave, and she walked so fast down the hill that she almost ran. She did not go home. Home, at the moment, was a narrow terraced house on Hallgarth Street, which she shared with two engineering students who were never there and a French girl called Ines, who was always there, and always singing.

Instead, she crossed Framwellgate Bridge and climbed up through the narrow streets towards the cathedral. It had started to rain, the fine, patient kind of rain that does not fall so much as hang in the air. On Saddler Street, between a phone shop and a place that sold expensive candles, she noticed a door she had not seen before. Above it, in faded gold letters, a sign said: PENNICK & DAUGHTER, STATIONERS, EST. 1921. The window was full of ink bottles, fountain pens and notebooks of every colour.

A small bell rang when she pushed the door. Inside, it smelled of paper, dust and something sweet, like old wood. Behind the counter sat a very old woman with white hair pinned up in a bun and a pair of glasses on a chain around her neck. She was doing a crossword with a pencil. She looked up at Rosalind, then at the rain on her coat, and smiled as if she had been expecting her. "Take your time, love," she said. "Nothing in here is in a hurry."

Rosalind walked slowly between the shelves. She touched the covers of the notebooks: red leather, blue cloth, yellow paper with pressed flowers. Then, on the lowest shelf, almost hidden behind a box of envelopes, she saw a green one. It was a simple exercise book with a hard cover the colour of moss and a black elastic band to keep it closed. It was not beautiful, exactly. But when she picked it up, it felt heavier than it looked, and warm, as if somebody had just been holding it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_10-1793197632853.mp3
"Ah," said the old woman, when Rosalind put it on the counter. "That one." She turned it over in her thin hands and looked at Rosalind over the top of her glasses. "It's been on that shelf a long time. Waiting for the right person, I expect." Rosalind did not know what to say to that, so she said nothing, which was what she usually did. The notebook cost four pounds. As the woman wrapped it in brown paper, she added, almost to herself: "Write the truth in it. It doesn't work otherwise." Then she went back to her crossword.

Rosalind had kept diaries before, of course. Her grandad Alf had given her the first one when she was eight. "When you can't say a thing, pet, write it down," he had told her, in his slow Dales voice. "Paper's patient. Paper doesn't interrupt." Grandad Alf had died two winters ago, and since then she had not written a single diary entry. It had felt like talking to an empty room. But tonight, walking home in the rain with the green notebook under her coat, she thought she might try again.

At Hallgarth Street, the kitchen light was on and Ines was cooking something that smelled of garlic and butter, while a French pop song played very loudly from her phone. "Rosalind! You want pasta? I made too much, I always make too much!" Rosalind smiled, shook her head, said "thanks, I've eaten", which was a lie, and went upstairs. She heard Ines sigh behind her. It was not an angry sigh. It was more like the sigh of somebody who has knocked on a door three times and is starting to think nobody lives there.

Her room was small and cold, with a bed, a desk under the window and a wardrobe that would not close properly. She sat at the desk, took off the brown paper and opened the green notebook at the first page. The paper was thick and slightly cream, with pale blue lines. She found a black biro in her bag. For a long minute she simply looked at the empty page. Then, because the old woman had told her to write the truth, she wrote the truth.

Day 9, Durham.
Today I said four words in my first seminar. "My name is Rosalind." Then I said sorry, because apparently I apologise for existing. Everybody else had something clever to say. I had something clever to say too, but it stayed inside. It always stays inside.
I think I made a mistake coming here. I think some people are built for big places and some people are built for small ones, and I am a small-place person.
Grandad, if you were here, you would tell me to be patient. I am trying. I don't know how long I can try.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_15-1793197687257.mp3
She put down the pen and read what she had written. It was not poetry. It was not even a good sentence. But her chest felt a little lighter, as if she had put down a heavy bag after carrying it up a hill. She was reaching out to close the notebook when something on the page moved. At first she thought it was a shadow from the lamp. Then she saw that it was ink. Green ink, appearing on the line just below her own words, slowly, letter by letter, as if an invisible hand were writing it at that very moment.
Five words, actually. You forgot the "sorry". We'll work on that.
You are not a small-place person. You are a small-voice person, which is different, and voices can grow.
Don't be scared. I'm on your side.
— R.

Rosalind did not scream. She was not a person who screamed. Instead, she pushed her chair back so fast that it hit the wardrobe, and the wardrobe door swung open with a long, sad creak. She stood in the middle of the room with both hands over her mouth. Downstairs, the French song had changed to another French song. Somewhere in the street, a group of students was singing "Happy Birthday" badly. The world was completely normal. Only the notebook on her desk was not.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_17-1793197705727.mp3
After a while, she came back to the desk and looked at the page without touching it. The green words were still there. The ink was shiny, as if it had not completely dried, and the letters leaned slightly to the right in a neat, confident hand. She touched one of them with the tip of her finger. It left a small green mark on her skin. So it was real ink, and it was really there. She checked the back of the page. Nothing. She held it up to the lamp. Nothing. She smelled it, feeling stupid. It smelled of ink.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_18-1793197716790.mp3
There had to be an explanation. A trick from the shop, perhaps: some kind of special paper, a chemical that reacted with biro. A joke for tourists. But how could a chemical know that she had said "sorry"? How could it know anything at all? Her hands were shaking a little. She closed the notebook, put the elastic band around it, and then, after a moment of hesitation, put it on the top shelf of the wardrobe, behind her winter jumpers. She pushed the door until it almost closed. Then she got into bed with all her clothes on and turned off the light.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_19-1793197730998.mp3
She did not sleep. She lay in the dark and listened to the house: Ines washing up and still singing, the engineers coming in late and dropping their keys, the pipes knocking in the walls. At three o'clock in the morning, she gave up. She turned on the lamp, opened the wardrobe and took the notebook down. Her heart was beating in her ears. She opened it at the first page, ready to find nothing, ready to discover that she had imagined everything because she was tired and lonely and very far from home.
Her own words were there, in black. The green words were there too. And below them, there was a new line that had not been there before.
You can hide me behind your jumpers for as long as you like. I'll still be here in the morning.
Go to sleep, Ros. Tomorrow we start.
— R.

Rosalind read the words three times. Nobody had called her Ros since Grandad Alf. She did not know whether to be frightened or comforted, and in the end she was a little of both. She did not put the notebook back in the wardrobe. She put it on the pillow next to her, with her hand on the cover, the way a child holds a book she is afraid somebody will take away. And for the first time in nine days, Rosalind Hale fell asleep before the sun came up.
