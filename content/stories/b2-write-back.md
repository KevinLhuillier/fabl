# The Green Notebook

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_1.page_1-1793197545633.mp3
On her ninth day in Durham, Rosalind Hale counted the words she had spoken out loud since breakfast. It did not take long. There had been "thank you" to the woman in the café, "sorry" to a boy whose bag she had touched on the stairs, and four more words in the seminar room, which she was trying very hard not to think about. Eleven words. Twelve, if she counted the "oh" she had whispered when she missed the bus. It was not a record she was proud of.
It was the end of September, and the city was full of people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing. They walked in groups across the old bridges, laughing, carrying pizza boxes and bottles and enormous plans for the evening. Rosalind walked alone, close to the walls, with her hands deep in the pockets of a coat that was too warm for the weather. She had chosen it because it had big pockets. Big pockets were useful when you did not know what to do with your hands.

She came from Kettlewell, a village in the Yorkshire Dales with one shop, two pubs, a river called the Wharfe and more sheep than people. Everybody there knew her. Everybody knew that she was "the clever Hale girl", the one who read books at the bus stop and won the poetry prize at school three years in a row. Nobody there expected her to talk very much, and that had suited her perfectly. In Kettlewell, being quiet was simply part of who she was, like her red hair or her grandad's old watch on her wrist.

# The Rules

When Rosalind woke up, the notebook was still on her pillow and the sun was shining through the thin curtains in a way that made everything look ordinary. > Are you a ghost?

# Small Steps

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-write-back/chapter_3.page_1-1793200801528.mp3
The week went by faster than Rosalind wanted it to. Every morning she woke up and thought, "Friday," and every night she went to bed and thought, "Friday," and in between she did all the normal things a student does: she went to lectures, she read in the library, she ate toast standing up in the kitchen. But the word was always there, at the back of her mind, like a stone in her shoe. One full sentence. To another student. Something true.
On Tuesday night she wrote to R. She did not mean to complain, but the words came out that way, the way water comes out of a tap that has been turned too far.

> Words that have been practised don't run away so easily. They know the way out.

"Chin up, kettle on." Rosalind read it and felt as if the floor had moved. It was Grandad Alf's expression. He had said it every time she fell off her bike, every time she failed a test, every time the rain ruined a day out. "Chin up, kettle on, pet." He had said it on the last day she saw him, from his hospital bed, with a weak smile. It was not a common phrase. She had never heard anybody else use it. She had never written it down, not once, not anywhere.
