# The Flyer

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_1-1793341056095.mp3
On the first Saturday in June, Maya Holt woke up to the smell of clean washing. It came up through the floor of her new flat, warm and sweet, from the launderette downstairs. For the first few weeks she had hated it. It was the smell of a life that was not hers, of other people's sheets and other people's towels. Now, after twelve weeks, she almost liked it. It meant that the machines were already turning. It meant that the world was already awake, even if she was not.
The flat was on Magdalen Street, in the north of Norwich. It had one room, a tiny kitchen and a bathroom where she could touch both walls at the same time. It was not a beautiful place. But it was hers, and it was cheap, and when she had needed somewhere to go in a hurry, it had been there. She had moved in with two suitcases, a box of books and a plant that was now, very slowly, dying on the windowsill.

Before Magdalen Street, there had been Unthank Road, and a flat with a blue front door, and Marcus. Five years of Marcus. They had met at university, they had moved in together at twenty-four, and Maya had always believed, without really thinking about it, that one day they would get married. Then, on the fourteenth of March, on a grey Saturday morning, Marcus had taken her for breakfast at the Copper Kettle, the little café at the market. He had waited until her tea was cold. Then he had told her that he had met someone at work.
"It's nobody's fault," he had said. He had said it three times, as if he had practised it in the car. Maya did not remember much after that. She remembered walking out of the café. She remembered sitting on a bench near the war memorial, and crying in a way that made people look and then quickly look away. She remembered the bus home. And she remembered that, somewhere between the bench and the bus, she had lost her sketchbook.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_3-1793341083131.mp3
It was only a small blue book, but it had been with her for almost two years. It was full of birds: robins, pigeons, the seagulls that fought over chips in the market square. There were also notes in it, and lists, and things that she had written when she was sad and did not want to say out loud. She had phoned the bus company twice. Nobody had found it. She had not drawn anything since that day. Every time she picked up a pencil, her hand felt empty and stupid.
She got up and made tea: strong, with milk, no sugar. On the fridge, under a magnet shaped like a lighthouse, there was a flyer. She had found it in her letterbox on Thursday evening, between a pizza menu and a letter from the electricity company. It was printed on thick, cream-coloured paper, and in the middle there was a drawing of two red cherries on one stalk. Under the cherries, in neat green letters, it said: "Brannock's Orchard. The first cherries of summer. Norwich Market, every Saturday."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_4-1793341096813.mp3
She did not know why she had kept it. She never kept flyers. But cherries were special to her. When she was a child, her grandmother had had a cherry tree at the bottom of her garden in Suffolk, and every July Maya had climbed it and eaten until her fingers were red and her stomach hurt. Her grandmother had called her "Pip", like the stone in the middle of a cherry, because she was small and hard to get rid of. Nobody had called her Pip since the funeral, eleven years ago.
Her phone rang. It was Gemma, of course. Gemma had been her best friend since they were eleven, and since March she had phoned every Saturday morning at exactly nine o'clock. "Are you dressed?" Gemma asked. "Define dressed," said Maya. "Put on real clothes. Not the grey thing. I'm coming to get you. We're going to the market." Maya looked at the flyer on the fridge. "Not the market," she said. "Anywhere but the market." There was a short silence. "Maya," said Gemma, more gently, "it's a market. It's not his. You're allowed to go there."

Half an hour later they were walking up the hill towards the centre. The sky was a clean, washed blue, and the city looked new, as if somebody had painted it during the night. Norwich Market lay below the City Hall like a box of sweets: hundreds of stalls under roofs of red, yellow, blue and green stripes. Maya had always loved it. She loved the noise, the smell of hot doughnuts and fresh fish and coffee, the way the narrow paths between the stalls twisted like the streets of an old town.
Gemma talked all the way. She talked about her new boss, about her sister's wedding, about a man at her gym who had tried to sell her a boat. Maya was grateful. When Gemma talked, Maya did not have to. She kept her eyes away from the corner where the Copper Kettle was. She did not want to see the window, or the little table, or the place where her tea had gone cold. "Where do you want to start?" asked Gemma. Maya surprised herself. "Cherries," she said. "There's a cherry stall somewhere."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_6-1793341124809.mp3
They found it at the end of the third row, between a stall that sold flowers and a stall that sold old records. It was simple: a long wooden table, a green-and-white striped roof, and boxes and boxes of cherries, so red and so shiny that they looked almost false. A small blackboard said, in chalk: "Cherries. Sweet as a first kiss. £4 a bag." Under it, in smaller letters, someone had added: "Sour ones free for people who are already in a bad mood."
Behind the table there was a man of about thirty. He was tall, with untidy brown hair, a sunburnt nose and a green apron that said "Brannock's" across the front. He was laughing with an old lady who was trying to pay with a very large note. "I can't take that, Mrs Stannard," he was saying. "If I take that, I'll have to give you all my change, and then I'll have to go home, and then who will sell the cherries? Pay me next week." The old lady pretended to be cross, then gave in, and he put an extra handful into her bag.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_7-1793341137986.mp3
Then he turned and saw Maya, and for half a second his face did something strange. It was very quick. It was the face of a man who had walked into a room and found something he had lost. Then it was gone, and he smiled the easy smile he had given Mrs Stannard. "Good morning," he said. "What can I do for you?" "A bag of cherries, please," said Maya. He picked up a paper bag and moved towards the nearest box. Then he stopped. "Not those," he said. "You want the dark ones."
"How do you know?" asked Maya. He looked at her seriously, as if he were a doctor. "You look like a dark-cherry person," he said. "Serious on the outside. Sweet in the middle." Gemma laughed so loudly that the man at the record stall turned round. Maya felt her face go hot. "That's the worst thing anyone has ever said to me," she said. "No, it isn't," he said, filling the bag with almost black cherries. "But I'm sorry if it's in the top ten."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_1.page_8-1793341152382.mp3
He weighed the bag, then took a big handful of cherries from another box and put them on top. "Those are extra," he said. "For bad weeks." Maya stopped with her purse in her hand. "Do I look like I'm having a bad week?" He thought about it for a moment. He did not smile this time. "No," he said quietly. "You look like you're having a bad year. But it's June now. Things usually get better in June." Then he winked, and the moment was over, and he was just a man selling fruit again.
Maya did not know if she wanted to be angry or to cry. So she did something she had not done for months: she made a joke. "Why did the cherry go to the doctor?" she asked. He raised his eyebrows. "Why?" "Because it wasn't peeling very well." There was a pause. Gemma closed her eyes in pain. It was a terrible joke. It was not even about the right fruit. But the man laughed: not a polite laugh, but a real one, from his stomach, with his head back.

"That," he said, "is the best joke I've heard all year." "You need to meet more people," said Maya. "Maybe," he said. He took her four pounds and gave her the bag. Their fingers touched for a second. His hands were rough and warm and a little red from the fruit. At the flower stall next door, a woman of about seventy with short white hair and big gold earrings had been watching everything. "Don't mind him, love," she said to Maya. "He talks to everyone." She looked at the young man for a moment. "Well. Not everyone."
All the way back down the hill, Gemma would not stop. "He liked you." "He sells cherries, Gem. He's nice to everybody. That's his job." "He wasn't nice to everybody. He was nice to you. 'Serious on the outside, sweet in the middle.' I'm going to put it on a T-shirt." "Please don't." "And he laughed at your joke. Nobody laughs at your jokes. I've known you for eighteen years and I have never laughed at one of your jokes." Maya held the paper bag against her chest and, without meaning to, smiled.

That evening, she sat at the window with the cherries in a bowl on her knees. The launderette was closed and the street was quiet. She ate them slowly and put the stones in a cup, the way her grandmother had taught her. They were the sweetest cherries she had ever eaten. Each one was dark, almost black, and cold, and tasted of summer. Somewhere in the middle of the bowl, she realised that for nearly an hour she had not thought about Marcus at all.
She thought instead about the cherry man. She did not even know his name. She thought about the way he had laughed at her joke, and she realised that Marcus had never laughed at her jokes. Not once, in five years. At the beginning he had smiled politely. Later he had sighed and said, "Please, Maya, not in front of my friends." Slowly, without noticing, she had stopped making them. She had stopped drawing in front of him too, because he always asked what the point was. It was strange, she thought, how much of yourself you could put away in a cupboard, little by little, until you forgot it was there.
Before she went to bed, she took the flyer off the fridge. She turned it over. The back was white and empty. She found an old pencil in the kitchen drawer, sat down at the little table, and drew a cherry. It was not a good cherry. The line was nervous and the shadow was in the wrong place. But it was the first thing she had drawn in twelve weeks. She looked at it for a long time. Then she put the flyer back on the fridge, with the drawing facing out. "Next Saturday," she said to the empty room. "Just for the cherries."

# Every Saturday

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_2.page_1-1793342034226.mp3
The next Saturday, Maya went to the market alone. She told herself that it was because Gemma was busy with her sister's wedding plans. That was true. But it was also true that she did not want Gemma there, laughing and making comments. She wanted to see if the cherry man had really been as kind as she remembered, or if she had imagined it because she was sad and lonely and ready to believe anything.
He was there, behind the long wooden table, in the same green apron. He was arguing happily with a man about football. When he saw her, he did not say "Good morning" or "What can I do for you?" He simply said, "Dark ones?" and reached for a paper bag. Maya felt something small and warm open inside her chest, like a window in a cold house. "Dark ones," she said. "You remembered." "I always remember the people who tell terrible jokes," he said. "There aren't many of you. You need to be protected."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_2.page_2-1793342049244.mp3
While he filled the bag, he said, "I'm Leo, by the way. Leo Brannock. Like the sign." "Maya," she said. He repeated it, slowly, as if he were tasting it. "Maya. It suits you." "You don't know me," she said. "That's true," he agreed. "But it suits the person who tells the cherry-doctor joke. I'll find out about the rest." He put the extra handful on top again. "For bad weeks," he said. "Was it a bad week?" "It was a medium week," said Maya, and he nodded as if that was important information.
The woman at the flower stall leaned over between two buckets of sweet peas. "Since he's being polite for once," she said, "I'm Doreen. Doreen Ainsworth. I've had this stall for forty-one years, and I knew his grandfather when he still had all his hair." "She's lying," said Leo. "He never had any hair." Doreen laughed and pushed a single sweet pea into Maya's hand. It was pale pink and smelled like honey. "For your table," she said. "A kitchen with no flowers is a sad kitchen."

That was the beginning of Saturdays. Maya did not plan it. It simply happened, the way a path appears across a field when enough people walk the same way. Every Saturday morning, after Gemma's nine o'clock phone call, she walked up the hill to the market. She bought her cherries. She talked to Leo for five minutes, then ten, then twenty, while he served other customers and argued about football and gave free cherries to children who were crying.
She liked watching him work. He knew the name of almost every customer, and he knew which of them wanted to talk and which of them only wanted to pay and leave. He gave Mrs Stannard her credit every week and never once wrote it down. When a thin young man with a sleeping bag under his arm stopped in front of the stall, Leo filled a bag without saying anything and passed it across the table as if it were the most normal thing in the world. The young man said thank you very quietly. Leo said, "See you next week, Kieran," and went back to arguing about football. Maya noticed that he did not look round to see if she had seen.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_2.page_4-1793342078771.mp3
He was easy to talk to. That was the thing she noticed most. With Marcus, she had always felt that she was saying slightly the wrong thing, a little too loud or a little too much. With Leo, it was like putting down a heavy bag. He asked questions and then he actually listened to the answers. He asked about her job, and she told him that she worked at the library in the Forum, the big glass building next to the market. "So you're surrounded by stories all day," he said. "Mostly by people who want to use the printer," said Maya.
On the third Saturday it rained. It was not a light summer rain but a real Norfolk storm, with water running down the paths between the stalls like little rivers. Maya arrived with her hair stuck to her face and her shoes full of water. Most of the stalls were empty of customers. Leo was sitting on a box under his striped roof, reading a newspaper. When he saw her, he stood up so fast that the newspaper fell into a puddle.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_2.page_5-1793342094135.mp3
"You came," he said. "In this?" "I needed cherries," said Maya, and then felt stupid, because nobody needed cherries. But he did not laugh at her. He reached under the table and brought out a paper bag that was already full. On the side, in black pen, someone had written "Maya – dark ones". Under the words there was a small drawing of a robin, round and serious, with its red chest pushed out. "I kept them for you," he said. "In case you came. If you didn't, I was going to eat them myself and feel sorry for myself."
Maya looked at the robin for a long time. Robins had always been her favourite bird. Her lost sketchbook had been full of them: robins on fences, robins in the snow, a robin that had come every morning to the window of the flat on Unthank Road. "Why a robin?" she asked. Her voice sounded strange to her. Leo shrugged. "It's the only bird I can draw," he said. "My gran taught me when I was six. I can do a robin and a very bad horse. I thought the horse would frighten you."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_2.page_6-1793342110814.mp3
It was a good answer. It was a normal, funny, believable answer. Maya laughed, and the strange feeling went away, and she sat with him under the striped roof for an hour while the rain drummed above their heads. Doreen gave them both tea in paper cups from a flask. They talked about everything: films they had hated, the worst jobs they had ever had, whether pineapple belonged on pizza. Leo said yes. Maya said that this was the first terrible thing she had learned about him.
When the rain finally stopped and Maya went to leave, Doreen caught her arm. Her hand was cold and strong from forty years of flower buckets. "Forty-one years I've been on this market," she said, in a low voice, so that Leo could not hear. "I've seen that boy grow up behind that table. I've seen him sell cherries to half of Norwich." She looked at the bag in Maya's hands, at the robin. "And I've never once seen him keep a bag back for anyone. Not once." Maya did not know what to answer. Doreen let go of her arm and patted it. "Go on," she said. "Get those wet shoes home."

On the fourth Saturday, at the end of June, the market was hot and crowded and smelled of strawberries. At four o'clock, when the stallholders began to pack up, Leo pulled off his apron and said, very casually, as if it did not matter, "Do you want to get a coffee? Or a walk? Or both? I've been standing behind this table since six this morning and I've forgotten how my legs work." Maya heard herself say yes before she had decided anything.
They bought two coffees and walked down through the old streets towards the river. The Wensum was slow and green and full of ducks. They followed the path past the old water gate at Pull's Ferry, where the cathedral rose behind the trees like a ship. People were sitting on the grass with picnics. A boy was trying to teach a dog to swim, and the dog was clearly not interested. The evening light was gold and soft, and for the first time in a long time Maya did not feel tired.

Leo told her about the orchard. It was near a village called Saxby Green, about twenty miles from the city. His grandfather had planted the first cherry trees there in 1964, when everyone told him that cherries would never grow in Norfolk. "He was very stubborn," said Leo. "It's a family problem." Four years ago, his father had died suddenly, of a heart attack, in the middle of the harvest. Leo had been working in London at the time, in an office, doing something with insurance that he did not want to talk about.
"I came home for the funeral," he said, "and I walked through the trees, and there was fruit everywhere, falling on the ground, and nobody to pick it. And I thought: I can't let it die. So I didn't go back to London. My sister Tess thinks I'm mad. She helps me anyway. She does the accounts and shouts at me about money." He smiled, but his eyes were somewhere else. "It's hard work. But it's real. In London nothing I did was real."

Maya told him things too. Not everything. She did not tell him about Marcus, or about the café, or the bench. But she told him about her grandmother's cherry tree, and about climbing it, and about the red fingers. She told him that she used to draw, before. "Birds, mostly," she said. "I had a sketchbook I carried everywhere. I lost it in March." Leo was quiet for a moment. He looked at the river, not at her. "That's sad," he said. "Did you try to find it?" "Everywhere. It's gone."
"You should draw again," he said. "Even without it." "I've started," she admitted. "A cherry. On the back of your flyer." She expected him to laugh, but he turned and looked at her with an expression she could not read. "On my flyer," he repeated softly. "Well. Then it was worth printing them." A duck landed on the water in front of them with a loud, stupid splash, and they both laughed, and the moment passed.

When they reached the end of the path, near the old bridge, Leo stopped. He put his hands in his pockets and looked suddenly young and nervous. "Next Sunday," he said, "we have an open day at the orchard. People come and pick their own cherries. There's music, and my sister makes terrible cakes, and children fall out of trees. It's chaos. Would you like to come? You can bring your friend. The one who laughed at you." "Gemma," said Maya. "She'll come. She'd never let me go alone."
That night Gemma screamed so loudly down the phone that Maya had to hold it away from her ear. "He asked you to his orchard! That's basically a proposal in Norfolk!" "It's an open day, Gem. Lots of people are going." "Lots of people didn't get a robin on their bag." Maya was quiet. "Is it too soon?" she asked at last. "It's only been three months since Marcus." Gemma's voice changed. "Maya, it's cherry picking, not a wedding. You're allowed to have a nice Sunday. You're allowed to be happy before you've finished being sad."
On Monday, in her lunch break, Maya went to the art shop on Elm Hill and bought a new sketchbook. She did not choose a blue one. She chose a red one, the colour of the dark cherries. That evening she sat at her window and drew for two hours. She drew the ducks on the river and the cathedral behind the trees. Then, without really deciding to, she drew a pair of hands, rough and strong, holding a paper bag. She looked at the drawing for a long time before she went to bed. She did not know yet that the hands had already held something of hers.

# The Orchard

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_3.page_1-1793342674240.mp3
Gemma drove, because Maya did not have a car any more. The car had been Marcus's, like the sofa and the television and most of the friends. They left the city on a bright Sunday morning at the beginning of July and drove north, along roads that became smaller and smaller, between fields of golden wheat and fields of bright yellow flowers. The sky in Norfolk was enormous. It sat on the flat land like a huge blue bowl, and the clouds moved across it slowly, like ships.
Saxby Green was a village of perhaps two hundred people. It had a church with a round tower, a pub called the Plough, a post office that was also a shop and also, according to a sign in the window, a dry cleaner. On the other side of the village, a hand-painted sign at the side of the road said "Brannock's Orchard – Pick Your Own – Open Day Today!" Under it, someone had painted a very bad horse. Maya laughed out loud. "What?" said Gemma. "Nothing," said Maya. "Private joke."

The orchard was bigger than she had imagined. Long rows of cherry trees went down a gentle hill towards a line of tall poplars and a little stream. Between the trees, the grass was long and full of white and yellow flowers. There were people everywhere: families with baskets, old couples on wooden ladders, children with red mouths running in circles. Near the farmhouse, an old man was playing the accordion, badly but with great enthusiasm. It smelled of cut grass and warm fruit and, from somewhere, burnt sugar.
Leo saw them from the top of a ladder. He waved so hard that the ladder moved, and three children below him screamed with happiness. He climbed down and came towards them across the grass. He was not wearing his apron today, just an old blue shirt with the sleeves pushed up, and he looked different here: taller, more relaxed, as if the orchard was the place where he fitted best. "You found it," he said. "Your horse helped," said Maya. He grinned. "I told you it was frightening."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_3.page_3-1793342703928.mp3
He took them to a table near the farmhouse, where a woman was cutting a cake into squares. She was a few years older than Leo, with short dark hair, a sharp nose and the same brown eyes. "This is my sister, Tess," said Leo. "Tess, this is Maya, and this is Gemma." Tess put down the knife and wiped her hands on her jeans. She looked at Gemma for one second. She looked at Maya for much longer. "So you're Maya," she said. Her voice was not unfriendly. It was careful, like someone walking on ice.
"Have some cake," said Tess, and pushed two squares towards them. "It's terrible. He's probably told you." "He did," said Gemma happily, and took a big bite. Her face changed. She chewed for a long time. "It's…" she began. "Yes," said Tess. "I know. I put salt in it instead of sugar at six o'clock this morning and I didn't have time to make another one. Nobody has noticed except the children. Children always notice." For the first time she smiled, and suddenly she looked a lot like her brother.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_3.page_4-1793342719994.mp3
Leo gave Maya a basket and took her down the hill, to the oldest part of the orchard. The trees there were bigger and twisted, with thick grey trunks and branches that bent almost to the ground under the weight of the fruit. He stopped in front of one that stood a little apart from the others. "This is Grandad's tree," he said. "The first one he planted, in 1964. Everybody said it would die in the first winter. It's still here. It still gives the best cherries on the farm. I don't let anybody pick from it." He paused. "Usually."
He held the ladder, and Maya climbed. She had not climbed a tree since she was fourteen, and for the first few steps her legs were shaking. Then something in her body remembered. She reached the first big branch and sat on it, with leaves all around her and the sun coming through in small bright pieces. The cherries hung in pairs and threes, dark and heavy. She picked one and ate it, and it was warm from the sun, and it tasted exactly like her grandmother's garden.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_3.page_5-1793342734904.mp3
She did not know how long she stayed up there. She filled the basket and passed it down to Leo and he gave her an empty one, and she filled that too. Her fingers became red, then purple. Below her, Leo was quiet. He did not try to talk. He simply stood under the tree, holding the ladder, and sometimes when she looked down he was looking up at her with an expression that made her want to look away and look back at the same time.
At lunchtime, Maya went into the farmhouse to wash the cherry juice off her hands. The kitchen was big and old-fashioned, with a stone floor, a huge black cooker and a long table covered in boxes of jam jars. # Two Years

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-cherry-seller/chapter_6.page_1-1793345243782.mp3
Maya did not answer Leo's message that night. She did not answer it the next morning either. She went to work and stamped books and helped an old man print a photograph of his cat, and all day the question sat inside her like a splinter. How had he known about the dark cherries? She told herself there was an easy answer. Most people preferred dark cherries. It was a lucky guess, a joke, a line he used with every customer. But she had watched him work for two months now, and she had never heard him say it to anyone else.
On Friday, she made a decision. She did not like it, and she did not feel proud of it, but she made it anyway. If Leo would not tell her everything, she would find out by herself. She would ask the people who had been there. She would ask questions the way a detective in one of the library's crime novels would ask them: calmly, carefully, without saying why.

On Saturday morning she set her alarm for half past five. By seven o'clock she was at the market. It was a different place at that hour: grey and quiet and smelling of wet stone. The striped roofs were still rolled up. Delivery vans were parked in the narrow paths, and men in thick jackets were carrying boxes and shouting to each other. Leo's stall was empty. He usually arrived at half past seven, after the long drive from Saxby Green. But Doreen was already there, putting buckets of sunflowers and dahlias in neat rows, with a cigarette behind her ear that she never smoked.
"You're early, love," said Doreen, surprised. "He's not here yet." "I know," said Maya. "I wanted to talk to you." She helped Doreen carry a heavy bucket of water, and then another. Then, as casually as she could, she said, "Doreen, how long has Leo had this stall? I mean, how long has he been here every Saturday, in summer and winter?" Doreen laughed. "Since his dad died. Four years, near enough. Before that, his dad did it, and before that his grandad. There's always been a Brannock on this corner."

"So he must have seen me before June," said Maya. "When I came here with… with someone else." Doreen stopped arranging the flowers. Then Doreen leaned forward and put her rough, cold hand on Maya's knee. "Listen to me, love. I'm an old woman and I've seen a lot of men. That boy is the best lad I know. He carried his father's coffin and then he came straight here and sold cherries, because the cherries wouldn't wait.
