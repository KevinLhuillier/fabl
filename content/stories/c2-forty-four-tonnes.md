# The Duchess

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_1.page_1-1792948777792.mp3
At ten to four on a Monday morning in late January, the port of Immingham resembled nothing so much as a city abandoned mid-sentence. Gantry cranes stood frozen against a sky the colour of wet slate, their booms raised like questions nobody had stayed to answer, and the sodium lamps along the quayside laid down pools of orange light that the drizzle could not quite extinguish. Gideon Thewlis crossed the yard with the unhurried gait of a man who had made the same walk several thousand times, a flask in one hand and, in the other, his driver card in its battered plastic wallet.
He was sixty-one years old, and in four days' time he would hand that card back for good. The firm had been courteous about it, in the way that firms are courteous when they have already made up their minds: a card signed by the whole office, a voucher for a garden centre, a speech promised for Friday afternoon. Gideon had accepted all of it with a nod. He had no quarrel with retirement as such. What unsettled him was the prospect of waking at three in the morning, as his body would insist on doing, with nowhere whatsoever to go.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_1.page_2-1792948796123.mp3
The Duchess was waiting for him at the end of the third row, her cab beaded with condensation. She was a Scania, eleven years old and well past the age at which the firm normally sold its tractor units on to hauliers in Lithuania, but the transport manager had a soft spot for Gideon, and Gideon had a soft spot for the truck, and the arrangement had quietly survived three rounds of fleet renewal. He had named her after his late wife's favourite expression for anything that refused to be hurried — "she'll go when she's ready, the duchess" — and Pauline had pretended to be offended for the better part of a decade.
He began his walk-round check by torchlight, as the regulations required and as habit would have compelled him to do regardless: tyres, wheel nuts, lights, mirrors, the fifth-wheel coupling, the airlines and the suzies coiled behind the cab. Only then did he climb up, slot his card into the tachograph and watch the little screen wake, blink and begin, impassively, to count. From this moment every minute of his day would be recorded, classified and, should anyone ever wish to examine it, held against him. Younger drivers resented the machine. Gideon had long since come to regard it as a kind of conscience.

The paperwork told him what he already knew. His load, collected from the quayside the previous evening, consisted of ninety sherry butts, shipped from Jerez de la Frontera and bound for a distillery called Glen Corrach, on the banks of the Spey, some four hundred and ninety miles to the north. Empty, each cask weighed scarcely more than a grown man. In forty years he had hauled steel coil, frozen fish, bagged cement and, on one memorable occasion, a dismantled fairground ride; the Duchess was rated for forty-four tonnes, and today she would be carrying barely six. There was, he reflected, something almost insulting about ending a career on so light a load.
Yet he had insisted on this run, and had called in a favour or two to get it. Pauline had been born in Grantown-on-Spey, and every summer for thirty-one years they had driven north to see her sister, stopping without fail at the same lay-by above Dalwhinnie to eat sandwiches and argue amiably about the route. He had not been back since the funeral. It had seemed fitting, in a way he would have been embarrassed to articulate, that the last road he drove for money should be the one he had driven most often for love.

He pulled out of the port gates at twenty past four. The A180 was deserted save for a gritting lorry lumbering ahead of him, its amber beacon sweeping the hedgerows in slow, hypnotic arcs, and Gideon settled in behind it without impatience. The radio offered him the shipping forecast — Tyne, Dogger, Fisher, German Bight — recited in the hushed, liturgical cadence he had always found more soothing than any music. Somewhere beyond the Humber the wind was backing northerly, increasing seven to severe gale nine, and he noted it as a farmer might note a darkening sky: without alarm, but without forgetting.
By the time he joined the M180 and swung west towards the A1, the drizzle had thickened into sleet that slanted across the windscreen like interference on an old television. He adjusted the wipers, tightened his grip by an imperceptible degree, and let the Duchess find her pace. People who had never driven an articulated lorry imagined it as a feat of strength. It was nothing of the kind. It was a matter of anticipation — of reading the road a quarter of a mile ahead, of thinking in long, patient curves, of never once asking twenty-odd tonnes of momentum to do anything abruptly.

At the junction with the A1, a quarter past five, his phone buzzed in its cradle. He did not answer while moving — he never had — but at the next lay-by he glanced at the message. It was from his daughter, Becca, who lived in Leeds and worried about him in the brisk, managerial fashion she had inherited from her mother. "Weather's turning nasty up north. Don't be a hero, Dad. Pull over if you have to. Love you." He typed a reply with one stiff forefinger — "Never been a hero in my life, love" — and was rather pleased with it.
Then he rejoined the carriageway and turned the Duchess north, into the long grey throat of the morning. The road unrolled before him as it had a thousand times: the cat's eyes ticking past like the beads of a rosary, the overhead gantries announcing distances to places he could have found blindfold. Wetherby. Scotch Corner. Newcastle. Beyond them, unseen but somehow already present, lay the mountains, the snow and whatever else the day had in mind for him. He would not have admitted it to Becca, nor perhaps to himself, but for the first time in years he felt something very like anticipation.

# Scotch Corner

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_2.page_1-1792948862500.mp3
Scotch Corner services, at half past seven on a winter's morning, was a place of strange and fleeting communion. The lorry park filled and emptied with the rhythm of a tide; drivers from Gdańsk and Constanța and Coleraine climbed down stiff-legged from their cabs, nodded to one another without speaking and shuffled towards the warmth of the café like pilgrims approaching a shrine. Gideon had taken his break here so often over the decades that he could have drawn the place from memory, down to the chipped tile by the hand dryers and the laminated notice forbidding the overnight parking of caravans.
He was halfway through a bacon sandwich, the tachograph dutifully recording his forty-five minutes of rest, when a voice behind him said, "Well, if it isn't the Duchess's chauffeur." He turned to find Nell Ostrowska grinning down at him, a paper cup of tea in each hand. Seven years earlier she had been a nervous twenty-year-old with a brand-new licence and a habit of apologising to the gearbox; he had been assigned to sit beside her for her first month on the road, and had done so with a forbearance she still described, to anyone who would listen, as saintly.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_2.page_2-1792948879847.mp3
She sat down opposite him and pushed one of the cups across the table. She drove for a supermarket chain now, chilled goods out of a depot near Wakefield, and was on her way to a distribution centre outside Inverness. They talked as drivers talk, which is to say mostly about other things — her mother's hip, his daughter's new kitchen, a mutual acquaintance who had finally been caught by a speed camera he had been boasting about evading for fifteen years. Only when he mentioned, almost in passing, that this was his last week did her face change.
"Your last week?" she said. "As in, ever?" He shrugged, and she studied him for a moment with the frankness that had once made her so unsettling a pupil. "You'll hate it," she said at last. "You do know that, don't you? You'll be pottering round some garden centre in a fleece, rearranging the seed packets, and you'll hear a lorry change down on the bypass and you'll want to weep." Gideon told her she had a morbid imagination. He did not tell her that she had, with uncanny precision, described his recurring dream of the past three months.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_2.page_3-1792948894706.mp3
It was Nell who raised the subject of the weather. She held out her phone so that he could see the Traffic Scotland page, on which the A9 north of Perth was marked with a scattering of ominous yellow triangles. A band of heavy snow was forecast to move in off the Cairngorms by mid-afternoon, and the Met Office had issued an amber warning that ran, with a cartographer's indifference, straight across the Drumochter Pass. "They'll shut it by teatime," she said. "Bet you anything. I'm going to try to get over before two, and if I can't, I'll sit it out in Perth."
Gideon did the arithmetic in his head, as he had done ten thousand times before. A driver was permitted nine hours behind the wheel in a single day, extendable to ten no more than twice a week; after four and a half hours of driving, a break of forty-five minutes was obligatory. He had used three hours already. From here to Glen Corrach was the better part of three hundred miles, a good seven hours for a lorry on a clear day, and this would not be a clear day. Even with his extension, the figures did not so much add up as lean against one another precariously.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_2.page_4-1792948910488.mp3
"You could stop in Perth too," Nell said, reading his silence with disconcerting accuracy. "Deliver tomorrow. Nobody's going to sack you in your last week." It was sensible advice, and he had given it himself, more than once, to younger drivers who mistook stubbornness for professionalism. But the distillery had asked for the casks by Monday evening — a filling run was scheduled for Tuesday morning — and besides, there was the lay-by above Dalwhinnie, which he had resolved, without quite admitting it, to reach before dark. He said he would see how the road looked, which both of them understood to mean no.
They walked out to the lorry park together. The sleet had stopped, and a hard, pale light was breaking through in the east, glazing the wet tarmac so that the rows of parked trailers seemed to stand on a sheet of beaten pewter. At the door of her cab, Nell hesitated, then hugged him — briefly, awkwardly, in the manner of someone who has never hugged a grown man in a car park before and is uncertain of the etiquette. "Text me when you're over the top," she said. "I mean it, Gideon. Not when you get there. When you're over."

North of Scotch Corner the A1 narrowed and emptied, and the land on either side began to rise and stiffen. Beyond Newcastle the fields grew stonier, the farmhouses lower and more squat, as if they had learned over centuries to crouch beneath the weather. Near Berwick he caught a glimpse of the sea, gunmetal grey and flecked with white, and then the road crossed the border, where a roadside sign and a fluttering saltire announced, with characteristic understatement, that he had entered another country.
He took his second break at a truck stop outside Edinburgh, ate nothing, and pressed on across the Queensferry Crossing, whose great white cables flashed past him like the strings of some colossal instrument. By Perth it was twenty to two, and the sky to the north had taken on a bruised, swollen quality that he did not like at all. He pulled in to check the forecast, found it unchanged and ominous, and sat for a full minute with his hands on the wheel. Then he indicated, checked his mirror and turned onto the A9, towards the mountains.

# Drumochter

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_3.page_1-1792948939117.mp3
The A9 has a reputation among lorry drivers that it has done little to deserve and nothing whatever to dispel. It threads its way north from Perth through a succession of glens, now dual carriageway, now single, so that impatient motorists are forever being lured into overtaking on stretches that narrow without warning. Gideon had seen the aftermath of such optimism more times than he cared to remember. He drove as he always drove on this road: steadily, a little below the limit, letting the queue of cars behind him dissolve and re-form in his mirrors like weed in a current.
Past Pitlochry the first flakes began to fall, fat and lazy and apparently harmless, melting the instant they touched the windscreen. By Blair Atholl they had stopped melting. The hills closed in on either side, their flanks streaked with old snow, and the river below the road turned the colour of slate. The temperature display on the dashboard slid from two degrees to zero, then to minus one, and then, with a kind of casual malice, to minus four. Gideon switched off the radio. He wanted nothing in the cab but the engine, the wipers and the faint, continuous hiss of the tyres on the thickening road.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_3.page_2-1792948954900.mp3
The Drumochter Pass marks the highest point on the whole of the A9, a desolate saddle of moorland some fifteen hundred feet above sea level, and in winter it can turn from merely bleak to genuinely lethal in the space of half an hour. Today it was doing precisely that. By the time Gideon neared the summit, a little after three, the snow was coming at him horizontally, driven by a wind that buffeted the high-sided trailer like a hand slapping a sail. The light had drained away to a dim, sourceless grey in which the road, the verges and the sky were no longer distinguishable from one another.
He was acutely aware, now, of the lightness he had scorned that morning. A high-sided trailer carrying six tonnes of empty casks offered the wind an enormous surface and almost nothing with which to resist it; laden with steel, the Duchess would have shrugged off these gusts, but today she was little more than a box kite on wheels. Twice he felt the trailer lift and shiver on its springs. He dropped to twenty miles an hour, then to fifteen, and fixed his eyes on the snow poles at the roadside, the striped markers that were now the only thing telling him where the tarmac ended.

He saw the car only because its hazard lights were still blinking — a faint, rhythmic orange pulse half buried in a drift on the verge ahead. A small silver hatchback had slid off the road and come to rest nose-down in the ditch, tilted at an angle which suggested that it would not be leaving under its own power. Snow had already banked against its windows. For one long, shameful second, Gideon's mind went to his tachograph, to the minutes ticking away, to the closure Nell had predicted and that must now be imminent. Then he switched on his hazards and brought the Duchess to a halt.
Inside the car were two people he took at first to be dead, so still did they sit. When he hammered on the window, however, the driver turned his head with great slowness and wound it down an inch. He was an old man, eighty at least, with a magnificent white moustache and a tweed cap pulled low over his ears, and beside him a woman of similar age sat swaddled in a tartan travel rug, her lips an alarming shade of blue. "Ah," said the old man courteously, as though Gideon were a neighbour who had dropped in for tea. "I'm afraid we appear to have got ourselves into a bit of a pickle."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_3.page_4-1792948988321.mp3
Their names, he learned over the next ten minutes, were Alasdair and Isla Beaton, and they had been driving home to Newtonmore from a granddaughter's christening in Stirling when the snow had overtaken them. The engine had died an hour earlier. Their phones, Alasdair explained with a certain gloomy relish, had been left fully charged on the kitchen table, where they would no doubt be of great comfort to the cat. Gideon did not waste breath on reproach. He helped Isla out first, then Alasdair, half carrying each of them in turn through the knee-deep snow towards the towering, rumbling bulk of the Duchess.
Getting two octogenarians up into the cab of an articulated lorry, in a blizzard, without breaking anything, is not a manoeuvre covered by any training course of which Gideon was aware. He achieved it by a combination of brute force, improvised engineering and outrageous flattery, and when at last both of them were installed — Isla on the bunk behind the seats, cocooned in his sleeping bag, Alasdair in the passenger seat with the heater roaring at his feet — he discovered that his hands were shaking and that he was, for no reason he could identify, on the verge of laughter.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_3.page_5-1792949002499.mp3
He dialled 999 on his own phone, gave their position as precisely as he could, and was told, by a calm young woman in Inverness, that the A9 had been closed at both ends twenty minutes earlier, that ploughs were on their way but could not promise to reach them before morning, and that, since nobody was injured and the lorry was warm, the best thing he could possibly do was stay exactly where he was. He thanked her, rang off and sat for a while staring at the tachograph, on which his remaining hours were now of purely theoretical interest.
Then he sent two messages. To Becca he wrote: "Stuck on Drumochter. Safe and warm. Not being a hero, promise." To Nell he wrote: "Not over the top. Not going to be tonight. Got company though." The replies came back within seconds of each other, as though the two women had been sitting with their phones in their hands waiting for precisely this — which, he supposed, they probably had. Outside, the wind had risen to a steady, inhuman howl, and the snow piled itself against the windscreen faster than the wipers could clear it away.

# The Long Night

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_4.page_1-1792950827074.mp3
Night fell, though it was hard to say exactly when; the grey simply thickened, by imperceptible degrees, into black. Gideon ran the engine for twenty minutes in every hour, enough to keep the cab warm without burning more diesel than he could spare, and in between the three of them sat in a dim, companionable gloom lit only by the soft green glow of the dashboard. He had shared his cab before, with trainees and with the occasional stranded colleague, but never with anyone quite like the Beatons, who seemed to regard their predicament as a mildly diverting episode rather than a brush with death.
Isla, revived by warmth and a cup of tea from his flask, turned out to be a retired schoolteacher with an encyclopaedic knowledge of Gaelic place names and very decided opinions about the Scottish Parliament. Alasdair had spent forty years as a harbour pilot at Invergordon, guiding rigs and tankers in and out of the Cromarty Firth, and he spoke of tides and channels with the same unhurried precision that Gideon brought to gradients and gear ratios. Within the hour the two men had established, to their mutual satisfaction, that they had spent their lives in essentially the same trade, merely conducted on different surfaces.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_4.page_2-1792950842766.mp3
Every hour, before starting the engine, Gideon climbed down into the storm with a shovel to clear the snow from around the exhaust. It was a precaution that had been drilled into him forty years earlier by an old Glaswegian driver who, he swore, had once found two men asleep for ever in a snowbound cab on Rannoch Moor, and Gideon had never forgotten the flat, matter-of-fact way in which he had told the story. Out in the darkness the cold was almost solid. The beam of his torch carved a narrow tunnel through the whirling flakes, beyond which there was nothing at all — no hills, no road, no world.
When he climbed back in, stamping and gasping, Isla was asleep on the bunk and Alasdair was gazing out at the snow with the expression of a man reading something written there. "Isla tells me this is your last week," he said, without turning his head. Gideon had mentioned it, in passing, over the tea. "I had my last day at sea in 1999," the old man went on. "Brought a Norwegian supply vessel in on the morning tide, flat calm, not a breath of wind. Easiest job I ever did. I remember being thoroughly disappointed. I'd always imagined I'd go out in some tremendous storm."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_4.page_3-1792950861410.mp3
Gideon laughed, and then, rather to his own surprise, found himself telling Alasdair about the lay-by above Dalwhinnie — about Pauline and the sandwiches and the thirty-one summers, about the funeral and the long, numb months that had followed it, about the half-formed notion that had brought him north on this particular run with ninety empty casks rattling behind him. He had never said any of it aloud before, not even to Becca. It came out haltingly, in no particular order, and when he had finished he sat staring at the wheel, mortified, waiting for the inevitable kindly platitude.
It did not come. Alasdair was silent for a long time. Then he said, "Do you know what we do with casks, up here?" Gideon admitted that he had only the vaguest idea. "We fill them," said Alasdair, "with spirit that's as clear as water and as raw as paint stripper, and then we roll them into a dark warehouse and we leave them alone. Ten years. Twelve. Twenty, sometimes. And nobody who fills a cask can say for certain who'll be standing there when it's opened. That's the whole point of it, you see. It's an act of faith in a future you'll very likely not attend."

Some time after midnight the wind began to slacken. Gideon dozed in his seat and woke at intervals to find the world outside transformed: the snow had stopped, the clouds had torn apart, and a lopsided moon hung above the pass, pouring a cold, unearthly radiance over a landscape that seemed to have been newly invented. Every contour of the moor was sheathed in white. The road had vanished altogether. Not a single light showed anywhere, in any direction, and the silence was so complete that he could hear, from the bunk behind him, the faint and steady rhythm of Isla's breathing.
He thought, unexpectedly, of the tachograph, which had at some point in the evening clicked over to a rest period and was even now recording, with bureaucratic serenity, that he was doing nothing whatsoever. It struck him as faintly comic that a machine so exacting about the hours he drove should be utterly incapable of recognising the most useful thing he had done all year. Then it struck him that the same was true of most of the instruments by which people measure their lives, and he sat for a while with that thought, turning it over, until the sky in the east began to pale.

The first snowplough appeared at a quarter to seven, a great yellow apparition crawling south out of the half-light with its blade raised and its beacons strobing across the drifts. Behind it came a second, and behind that a police four-by-four, from which emerged a sergeant of formidable dimensions who listened to Gideon's account, inspected the Beatons, pronounced them "in better nick than most of my relatives" and arranged for an ambulance to meet them at Dalwhinnie as a precaution. Isla protested that she had never felt better in her life. Nobody paid her the slightest attention.
Before they parted, Alasdair took Gideon's hand in both of his own and held it a moment longer than convention required. "When you get to Glen Corrach," he said, "ask for Ruaridh Mackay. Tell him Alasdair Beaton sent you. He'll know what that means." Then the old couple were helped down from the cab and into the four-by-four, Isla waving regally through the rear window like a dowager departing a garden party, and Gideon was alone once more, with his casks and his card and a road that was slowly, mile by mile, being given back to him.

# Glen Corrach

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_5.page_1-1792950908338.mp3
The road reopened at half past eight, and Gideon followed the ploughs north at a stately twenty miles an hour, the Duchess's tyres crunching over packed snow that glittered in the low sun like crushed glass. Thanks to the night he had spent marooned on the pass, the tachograph had registered a full daily rest; as far as the regulations were concerned, he was a new man with a fresh allowance of hours, and he found the notion more consoling than he would have expected. Below him in the valley, the roofs of Dalwhinnie sent thin, straight threads of smoke up into the windless air.
He pulled into the lay-by just above the village. It was exactly as he remembered it — a crescent of gravel, now buried, a low stone wall, a view across the white floor of the glen towards hills that stood shoulder to shoulder like old men at a funeral. He climbed down, waded to the wall and stood there with his hands in his pockets. He had brought no sandwiches. He had not planned what he would say, or think, or feel; he had half expected that he would feel nothing at all. In the event, he stood there for eleven minutes, and when he climbed back into the cab, for once he did not look at the tachograph.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-forty-four-tonnes/chapter_5.page_2-1792950926934.mp3
Then he texted Nell — "Over the top" — and drove on. The A9 swept down into Strathspey in long, generous curves, past Newtonmore and Kingussie and the frozen marshes of the Insh, where a skein of geese rose crying from the reeds as he passed. At Aviemore he turned off towards Grantown, and the road narrowed and grew intimate, winding between birch woods and drystone dykes and fields in which sheep stood motionless, their fleeces yellowish against the snow. He had driven this road every summer for three decades. He had never once seen it in winter, and it seemed to him now both utterly familiar and entirely new.
Glen Corrach Distillery lay at the end of a single-track road a few miles beyond Grantown, in a fold of the hills where a burn came tumbling down to join the Spey. It was a modest place — a cluster of whitewashed buildings, a pagoda-roofed kiln that had not been used for malting in sixty years, and a row of long, low warehouses whose walls had been stained black by the fungus that feeds on evaporating spirit. Gideon reversed the Duchess up to the loading bay at a quarter past eleven, some seventeen hours late, and felt neither triumph nor embarrassment, only a vast and peaceable fatigue.

The warehousemen unloaded the casks with a briskness that suggested they had been waiting for some time. When Gideon handed over the delivery note to the young woman in the office and asked, a little diffidently, whether a Ruaridh Mackay worked there, she looked at him with sudden interest and disappeared without a word. She returned a few minutes later with a stocky, grey-bearded man in a boiler suit, who introduced himself as the distillery manager and enquired, with elaborate casualness, whether it was true that Gideon had spent the night on Drumochter with Alasdair Beaton.
News, it transpired, travelled fast in Strathspey. Alasdair, it also transpired, was Ruaridh's uncle, and had telephoned from the hospital in Inverness — where he and Isla had been taken, much against their will, for observation — to deliver a lengthy and, in Ruaridh's words, "frankly embarrassing" account of Gideon's conduct. "He says you're to be given the tour," said Ruaridh. "The proper one, mind, not the one we do for the coach parties. And he says I'm to show you warehouse four. He was very particular about warehouse four. Wouldn't tell me why. Said you'd understand."

Warehouse four was the oldest on the site, a dunnage warehouse with an earthen floor and walls a yard thick, in which the casks lay three high on wooden runners in a cool, damp, cathedral dark. The air was so heavy with the scent of oak and spirit that Gideon felt it settle on his tongue. Ruaridh led him down a narrow aisle to a single butt near the far wall, its staves darkened almost to black, its head chalked with a date: 1994. "Filled the week my uncle's first grandson was born," Ruaridh said quietly. "He paid for it himself. It's never been for sale."
"Who's it for, then?" Gideon asked. Ruaridh smiled. "That's the thing," he said. "Nobody knows. My uncle always said he'd open it when the moment told him to, and not before. Thirty-two years it's been waiting. Well — he rang this morning, and he says the moment's come. He says it's to be opened today, the day your casks arrived, and he says the first glass is yours." Gideon opened his mouth to protest that he barely drank whisky, that it was far too much, that he had only done what anyone would have done. Then, very slowly, he closed it again.

He drove back south the following day, slowly and without incident, under a sky of rinsed and brilliant blue. On Friday afternoon he handed in his card at the depot in Immingham, listened to the transport manager's speech with an expression of polite suffering, and accepted the voucher for the garden centre. Nell, who had somehow contrived to be in the yard at the time, informed the assembled company that he had once made her reverse a trailer round a traffic cone for three solid hours, and that she had never forgiven him. Becca cried, briefly and furiously, and blamed the wind.
He had drunk that first glass in warehouse four, among people who were no longer quite strangers. Now, on a shelf in his kitchen, beside Pauline's photograph, stands a small bottle filled from the same cask and labelled in Ruaridh Mackay's careful hand; he has not opened it yet. He still wakes most mornings at three, as he knew he would, and lies in the dark listening for lorries on the bypass. But he no longer feels that he has nowhere to go. In a dark warehouse beside the Spey, ninety casks are quietly becoming something, and in twelve years' time, God and the A9 willing, he intends to be there when they are opened.
