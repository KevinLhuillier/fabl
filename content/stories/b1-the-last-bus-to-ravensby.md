# The Last Bus

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_1.page_1-1793300802017.mp3
Callum Reddaway hated Halloween. He was nineteen, he studied engineering at the university in Wetherholme, and he thought that costumes, plastic skeletons and fake blood were for children. But his friends loved it. That year, they had organised a big party in a farmhouse outside the town, and Callum had promised to go. He had even bought a costume: a cheap black cape and a pair of plastic vampire teeth. The plan was simple. His friend Priya had a car. She was going to pick him up at nine o'clock in front of the library.
At nine o'clock, Callum was not in front of the library. He was asleep on the sofa in his small student flat, with his laptop on his knees and an unfinished essay on the screen. When he woke up, it was ten past ten. His phone showed eleven missed calls and a message from Priya: "We waited 30 minutes. We've gone. You're hopeless!!" Callum read it twice and groaned. The farmhouse was fifteen kilometres away, in the middle of the hills, and no taxi in Wetherholme was free on Halloween night.
He decided to forget the party. Instead, he had a better idea.

His grandmother lived in Ravensby, a small village on the other side of the moor, about twenty kilometres north. It was half-term, he had no classes the next day, and Nan always said, "Come whenever you want, love. There's always a bed for you." He could take the night bus, sleep in his old room under the roof, and eat one of her enormous breakfasts in the morning. It was a much better plan than a party full of drunk vampires.
Callum loved his grandmother's house. It was a narrow stone cottage on Chapel Street, in the middle of the village, with a red door, a tiny garden and a kitchen that always smelled of toast and lavender. Nan had lived there all her life. She knew everybody in Ravensby, and everybody knew her. She was seventy years old, but she still walked five kilometres every morning, did the crossword in pen and argued with the television during the news. Callum had spent all his school holidays in that house. When he thought of the word "home", he often thought of Nan's kitchen.

He checked the timetable on his phone. The last bus to Ravensby, the number 7, left the bus station at 23:15. He had time. He put a toothbrush and a clean T-shirt in his backpack. He didn't take off his black cape, because it was warm and it was raining outside. He put the plastic teeth in his pocket, just in case. Then he walked through the wet streets of Wetherholme, past pubs full of witches, zombies and people dressed as giant pumpkins. Everybody seemed happy. Everybody except Callum.
When he arrived at the bus station, it was 23:20. The number 7 was not there. A small yellow paper was stuck on the timetable: "Saturday 31 October: the last number 7 will leave at 22:45. We apologise for any inconvenience." Callum stared at the paper. He felt stupid, then angry, then very tired. He tried to call his grandmother, but his phone made a sad little noise and the screen went black. The battery was dead. Of course it was. It was that kind of night.
The bus station was almost empty. There was only an old man asleep on a bench, a cleaner pushing a machine across the floor, and a lot of fog coming in from the street.

Callum sat down under the big clock and tried to think. He could walk back to his flat. He could wait until the first bus in the morning. Or he could sit here and feel sorry for himself. He chose the third option, at least for a few minutes.
At 23:40 exactly, he heard an engine. It was a deep, heavy sound, very different from the quiet modern buses of Wetherholme. Two round yellow lights appeared in the fog, and then a bus came slowly into the station. It was an old green double-decker, the kind you see in museums or in old films. It had a long silver radiator, a big rounded front and an open platform at the back, with a pole to hold. Above the windscreen, in white letters on a black blind, Callum read: 31 RAVENSBY.
Callum smiled for the first time that evening. He didn't know any bus number 31, but he guessed it was a special Halloween service. Some bus companies did that: old vehicles, staff in costume, a bit of fun for the tourists. The bus stopped in front of him with a long sigh of the brakes. The driver was a thin man of about fifty, with a grey uniform, a peaked cap and a small moustache. He looked down at Callum through the open door and nodded politely.

"Ravensby?" asked Callum. "Ravensby," said the driver. "Last bus tonight, young man. Hop on." Callum climbed onto the platform and held out a two-pound coin. The driver looked at the coin for a long moment, turned it in his fingers, and frowned. Then he shrugged, put it in a leather bag and turned a handle on a strange metal machine hanging from his neck. A small paper ticket came out. "Mind the step," he said. On the ticket, in faded purple ink, Callum read: Fare 1s. 6d.
He laughed quietly. One shilling and sixpence: old money, from before 1971. These people had really thought of every detail. He walked down the aisle. The bus was almost full, and all the passengers were in costume. A young man in an old army uniform. A woman with a huge bun of hair, knitting something red. A couple in long coats and hats. And near the back, alone by the window, a girl of about sixteen in a yellow raincoat, holding a strange lantern on her knees. The seat next to her was free. Callum sat down, and the bus drove into the fog.
