# The Last Bus

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_1.page_1-1793300802017.mp3
Callum Reddaway hated Halloween. He was nineteen, he studied engineering at the university in Wetherholme, and he thought that costumes, plastic skeletons and fake blood were for children. But his friends loved it. That year, they had organised a big party in a farmhouse outside the town, and Callum had promised to go. He had even bought a costume: a cheap black cape and a pair of plastic vampire teeth. The plan was simple. His friend Priya had a car. She was going to pick him up at nine o'clock in front of the library.
At nine o'clock, Callum was not in front of the library. He was asleep on the sofa in his small student flat, with his laptop on his knees and an unfinished essay on the screen. When he woke up, it was ten past ten. His phone showed eleven missed calls and a message from Priya: "We waited 30 minutes. We've gone. You're hopeless!!" Callum read it twice and groaned. The farmhouse was fifteen kilometres away, in the middle of the hills, and no taxi in Wetherholme was free on Halloween night.
He decided to forget the party. Instead, he had a better idea.

His grandmother lived in Ravensby, a small village on the other side of the moor, about twenty kilometres north. It was half-term, he had no classes the next day, and Nan always said, "Come whenever you want, love. There's always a bed for you." He could take the night bus, sleep in his old room under the roof, and eat one of her enormous breakfasts in the morning. It was a much better plan than a party full of drunk vampires.
Callum loved his grandmother's house. It was a narrow stone cottage on Chapel Street, in the middle of the village, with a red door, a tiny garden and a kitchen that always smelled of toast and lavender. Nan had lived there all her life. She knew everybody in Ravensby, and everybody knew her. She was seventy years old, but she still walked five kilometres every morning, did the crossword in pen and argued with the television during the news. Callum had spent all his school holidays in that house. When he thought of the word "home", he often thought of Nan's kitchen.

He checked the timetable on his phone. The last bus to Ravensby, the number 7, left the bus station at 23:15. He had time. He put a toothbrush and a clean T-shirt in his backpack. He didn't take off his black cape, because it was warm and it was raining outside. He put the plastic teeth in his pocket, just in case. Then he walked through the wet streets of Wetherholme, past pubs full of witches, zombies and people dressed as giant pumpkins. Everybody seemed happy. Everybody except Callum.
When he arrived at the bus station, it was 23:20. The number 7 was not there. A small yellow paper was stuck on the timetable: "Saturday 31 October: the last number 7 will leave at 22:45. We apologise for any inconvenience." Callum stared at the paper. He felt stupid, then angry, then very tired. He tried to call his grandmother, but his phone made a sad little noise and the screen went black. The battery was dead. Of course it was. It was that kind of night.
The bus station was almost empty. There was only an old man asleep on a bench, a cleaner pushing a machine across the floor, and a lot of fog coming in from the street.

Callum sat down under the big clock and tried to think. He could walk back to his flat. He could wait until the first bus in the morning. Or he could sit here and feel sorry for himself. He chose the third option, at least for a few minutes.
At 23:40 exactly, he heard an engine. It was a deep, heavy sound, very different from the quiet modern buses of Wetherholme. Two round yellow lights appeared in the fog, and then a bus came slowly into the station. It was an old green double-decker, the kind you see in museums or in old films. It had a long silver radiator, a big rounded front and an open platform at the back, with a pole to hold. Above the windscreen, in white letters on a black blind, Callum read: 31 RAVENSBY.
Callum smiled for the first time that evening. He didn't know any bus number 31, but he guessed it was a special Halloween service. Some bus companies did that: old vehicles, staff in costume, a bit of fun for the tourists. The bus stopped in front of him with a long sigh of the brakes. The driver was a thin man of about fifty, with a grey uniform, a peaked cap and a small moustache. He looked down at Callum through the open door and nodded politely.

"Ravensby?" asked Callum. "Ravensby," said the driver. "Last bus tonight, young man. Hop on." Callum climbed onto the platform and held out a two-pound coin. The driver looked at the coin for a long moment, turned it in his fingers, and frowned. Then he shrugged, put it in a leather bag and turned a handle on a strange metal machine hanging from his neck. A small paper ticket came out. "Mind the step," he said. On the ticket, in faded purple ink, Callum read: Fare 1s. 6d.
He laughed quietly. One shilling and sixpence: old money, from before 1971. These people had really thought of every detail. He walked down the aisle. The bus was almost full, and all the passengers were in costume. A young man in an old army uniform. A woman with a huge bun of hair, knitting something red. A couple in long coats and hats. And near the back, alone by the window, a girl of about sixteen in a yellow raincoat, holding a strange lantern on her knees. The seat next to her was free. Callum sat down, and the bus drove into the fog.

# The Girl with the Lantern

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_2.page_1-1793302524367.mp3
The inside of the bus smelled of old leather, wet coats and cigarette smoke. The seats were covered in a green and brown fabric, and the windows had small curtains. Yellow lamps on the ceiling gave a soft, warm light. Somewhere at the front, a bell rang twice, and the bus turned out of the station. Callum looked at the girl next to him. She had short dark hair, freckles on her nose and very bright, curious eyes. She was looking at his black cape with great interest.
"Are you going to a party?" she asked. "I was," said Callum. "But I missed my lift. So now I'm going to my nan's house." The girl laughed. "Me too, in a way. I'm going home to my little sister. She's ten, and she made me promise to be back before midnight. We always light the lanterns together on Halloween." She lifted the strange object on her knees. It was not a pumpkin. It was a big round vegetable, white and purple, with a scary face carved into it and a candle inside.

"Is that a turnip?" asked Callum. "Of course it's a turnip," said the girl, surprised. "What else? My dad grows them in the garden. It took me two hours to carve it. Look, it has real teeth." She smiled proudly. Callum didn't want to be rude, so he said nothing about pumpkins. He had read somewhere that, before pumpkins became popular, people in England and Scotland used turnips for Halloween lanterns. Again, he thought, these actors had really done their homework.
"My sister helped me choose the face," Dot went on. "She wanted a happy one, but I said Halloween lanterns must be scary, or the bad spirits will come into the house. That's what our gran told us. You put the lantern in the window, and the spirits see the ugly face and run away. My sister is carving her own lantern at home tonight. Hers is going to be very small, because she's not allowed to use the big knife." She laughed. "Every year she says she's going to make the scariest lantern in the village. Every year it looks like a smiling potato."

"I'm Dot, by the way," said the girl, and she held out her hand. "Dorothy, really, but nobody calls me that except my teachers and the vicar." "Callum," he said, and shook her hand. It was very cold, colder than the night outside. "Callum? That's a Scottish name. Are you Scottish?" "No, my mum just liked it." Dot nodded seriously, as if this was important information. Then she asked him what he did, and he told her about the university and his engineering course.

Next year I want to go to London. Everybody is in London now. The music, the clothes, everything. Have you heard the new Beatles record? My friend Sheila has it. We listened to it ten times on Sunday." Callum smiled. "Which one?" "The new one, silly! The one that came out in the summer."
At that moment, the young man in the army uniform turned around in his seat. He had red ears and a friendly face.

"Don't listen to her, mate," he said. "She talks about the Beatles all day long. I'm Eddie. Eddie Larkin. I'm going home on leave. Three whole days! My mum is making a steak and kidney pie." He showed Callum a photo of a young woman with a big smile. "That's my girl, Pauline. I'm going to ask her to marry me tomorrow. Don't tell anyone."
Callum promised not to tell anyone. He was starting to enjoy himself. These people were very good. Nobody ever stopped playing their role. The woman with the big bun of hair, Mrs Bramwell, offered him a peppermint from a paper bag. The couple in the long coats talked quietly about the price of coal. The driver whistled an old song. And outside, through the fog, the lights of Wetherholme went past the window slowly, like lights under water.
The bus stopped at a place called Tanner's Row. Nobody got on and nobody got off. Callum looked out of the window and felt a small, cold shock in his stomach. On the other side of the street, there was a cinema. Its name, THE REGAL, was written in pink and blue lights, and people in hats were coming out of the doors, laughing.

But Callum knew this street. He walked down it every week. There was no cinema here. There was a big, ugly supermarket with a car park.
"Dot," he said slowly. "That cinema. Has it always been there?" Dot looked at him as if he was a little strange. "The Regal? Since before I was born. My mum and dad had their first date there." Callum didn't answer. He told himself that it was a decoration, that the bus company had organised everything, that it was all part of the show. It was Halloween, after all. People did crazy things for Halloween. But a small voice in his head said: nobody builds a whole cinema for a bus ride.
The bus started again. Callum looked up at the small round clock above the driver's head. It said 23:47. He watched it for a long time while Dot talked about her sister, her favourite dress and a boy called Roy who was "really not her type". The bus left the town and climbed onto the dark road across the moor. Dot laughed, Eddie sang, Mrs Bramwell knitted. And the clock above the driver still said 23:47. The hands did not move at all.

# The Evening Post

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_3.page_1-1793304013448.mp3
Outside, there was nothing. No houses, no trees, no other cars. Only the fog, thick and white, pressing against the windows like cotton wool. The bus moved slowly, and its engine made a low, regular noise, like an old animal breathing. It was getting colder. Callum pulled his cape around his shoulders and breathed on his hands to warm them. A small white cloud came out of his mouth. Then he noticed something, and he stopped breathing for a second.
Dot was talking about her sister again, and her breath made no cloud. Eddie was singing, and his breath made no cloud. Mrs Bramwell was counting her stitches out loud, and nothing came out of her mouth either. In the whole cold bus, Callum was the only one whose breath was visible. He told himself it was a question of light, or of where they were sitting. He was an engineer. Engineers did not believe in ghosts. Engineers believed in facts.
So he looked for facts. Under the seat in front of him, there was a newspaper, folded in two. He picked it up. It was the Wetherholme Evening Post. The paper was not yellow or old. It was fresh and white, and the ink came off on his fingers.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_3.page_2-1793304029804.mp3
On the front page there was a photo of a football team and a long article about a new road. And at the top of the page, in small black letters, he read the date: Monday, 31st October 1966.
Callum read the date three times. His hands were shaking a little. He turned the pages. There were advertisements for televisions in wooden boxes, for cigarettes "recommended by doctors" and for a new washing machine that cost forty-nine pounds. On page six, there was the weather forecast: "Thick fog expected on the moors tonight. Drivers are advised to stay at home." Callum folded the newspaper carefully and put it back under the seat, as if it was dangerous.
He needed to know more. He leaned forward and touched Eddie's shoulder. "Eddie," he said, trying to sound normal, "what was the best thing that happened this year? In the news, I mean." Eddie turned around with an enormous smile. "Are you joking? The World Cup, of course! England won the World Cup, mate! I watched the final in the barracks with two hundred other lads. When the fourth goal went in, we all jumped on the tables. I'll remember that day until I die." Then he turned back to his seat and started singing again. Callum sat very still. England had won the World Cup only once. In the summer of 1966.

"Dot," he said, and his voice sounded strange to him. "What's your surname?" "Tolley," she answered. "Dorothy Tolley, from Ravensby. Number four, Chapel Street. Why?" Callum did not answer. He could not. Tolley. He knew that name very well. It was his grandmother's name before she married his grandfather. Nan was born June Tolley, in Ravensby, in a small house on Chapel Street. And Nan had once had an older sister.
It was an old family story, and nobody liked to talk about it. When Nan was ten years old, her big sister took the last bus home from Wetherholme on Halloween night. There was a terrible fog on the moor. The bus never arrived in Ravensby. The police looked for it for weeks, with dogs and men and even a helicopter, but they never found the bus, the driver or any of the passengers. It disappeared completely, as if the fog had eaten it.
Callum remembered something else. Every year, on the 31st of October, Nan put a lamp in the front window of her house and left it on all night.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_3.page_4-1793304063471.mp3
When he was small, he thought it was for Halloween, to scare the witches. Later, his mother explained: "It's for Dot. So she can find her way home." Nan had done it for sixty years. Callum looked at the girl next to him, at her yellow raincoat and her turnip lantern, and he felt tears in his eyes.
The bus slowed down and stopped. Through the fog, Callum saw a small wooden sign: KIRK MOOR. There was nothing else: no shelter, no house, no light. He stood up suddenly. He needed air. He needed to think. He walked quickly to the front. "Excuse me," he said to the driver. "Can I get off here, please? Just for a minute?" The driver did not turn his head. "Nobody gets off before Ravensby, son," he said calmly. "Company rules."
Callum went to the open platform at the back. There was no door, nothing to stop him. He held the cold metal pole and looked down. He could not see the road. He could not see the grass or the stones of the moor. Under the bus, there was only white fog, moving slowly, like a sea without a bottom. He did not jump. He stayed there for a long time, holding the pole, with his heart beating very fast. Then he went back to his seat.

As he walked down the aisle, he passed in front of the driver's small mirror. In the mirror, he could see Mr Quill's eyes, and Mr Quill's eyes were looking at him. They were not angry or frightening. They were simply very, very tired, like the eyes of a man who has driven the same road for too long. For a second, Callum had the strange feeling that the driver knew. That he knew what Callum had found in the newspaper, and what it meant. Then the driver looked back at the fog, rang the bell twice, and the bus moved on.
"Are you all right?" asked Dot. "You look like you've seen a ghost." Callum almost laughed. "I'm fine," he said. "Just tired." Dot smiled and pressed her face against the window. "Don't worry. We're nearly there. After Kirk Moor, there's only the bridge, and then Ravensby." "Which bridge?" asked Callum, but he already knew the answer. "Blackwater Bridge, of course," said Dot. "Over the river. There's no other way."

# Blackwater Bridge

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_4.page_1-1793304648989.mp3
Callum knew Blackwater Bridge. Everybody in Ravensby knew it. It was an old stone bridge over a deep, fast river, at the bottom of a steep hill. For a hundred years, it was the only way into the village from the south. But it was narrow and dangerous, and in the early seventies the council built a new road and a new bridge, two kilometres further up the river. The old bridge was closed. Today, it was still there, covered in moss, behind a fence with a sign: DANGER – NO ENTRY.
He suddenly remembered the article in the newspaper, on the front page, next to the photo of the football team. It was about the new road. In 1966, the new road did not exist yet. It was only a project, an idea on paper. For the people on this bus, there was only one way home: the old bridge. Callum felt that this was important, but he did not understand why yet. The bus started to go down the hill, slowly, very slowly, with its brakes crying.
At the bottom of the hill, the fog became darker. It was not white any more. It was grey, then almost black, like smoke.

The yellow lights of the bus could not cut through it. The passengers stopped talking. Eddie stopped singing. Mrs Bramwell put down her knitting. Even Dot was silent, holding her lantern very tightly. The driver stopped the bus. In front of them, Callum could just see the beginning of the stone bridge, and then nothing at all. A wall of darkness.
"Here we are again," said the driver quietly. He sounded very, very tired. He put the bus into gear and drove forward onto the bridge. The darkness came into the bus like water. For a moment, Callum could see nothing and hear nothing. He felt cold, so cold that his teeth hurt. Then the light came back. The bus was moving slowly through white fog. And through the window, Callum saw a small wooden sign: KIRK MOOR.
"No," said Callum. "No, no, no. We passed this already." Mrs Bramwell looked at him with sad, kind eyes. "We always pass it, dear," she said. "Every time. We get to the bridge, and the fog takes us back. We've been going round and round all night." "How long is 'all night'?" asked Callum. Mrs Bramwell thought about it. "I don't know," she said. "It's always twelve minutes to midnight. It feels like a very long time."

Mr Quill spoke without turning around. "I've tried everything, son. I've driven fast and I've driven slow. I've stopped and waited for the fog to go. I've tried with the lights on and with the lights off. Once, I tried to turn the bus around and go back to Wetherholme. It doesn't matter. Every road on this moor ends at Blackwater Bridge, and Blackwater Bridge ends at Kirk Moor." He hit the steering wheel softly with his hand, once. "Twenty-two years I've driven this route. I know every stone of it. And I can't get my passengers home."
Callum understood then. These people were not actors. They were not playing a game. They were trapped: trapped on the moor, trapped in the fog, trapped in one night of 1966 that never ended. Every Halloween, the number 31 left Wetherholme and tried to go home over Blackwater Bridge, and every Halloween it failed. Maybe the bridge was too dangerous in the fog. Maybe something terrible happened there that night. He did not know. But he knew one thing: there was another way.

He walked to the front of the bus again. This time, he did not ask to get off. "Excuse me," he said. "What's your name?" The driver looked at him for the first time. "Quill. Stanley Quill. Twenty-two years with the company, and I've never lost a passenger." "Mr Quill," said Callum, "I know another road to Ravensby. A new bridge, further up the river. It's safe. I've been on it a hundred times. Please, let me show you."
He thought of all the times he had come to Ravensby in his mother's little red car. Every summer, every Christmas, every half-term. They never went near the old bridge. After the Kirk Moor sign, his mother always turned left at the big standing stone, onto the new road. As a child, Callum used to wave at the stone and call it "the giant". His mother laughed every time. He knew that road with his eyes closed: the farm, the barn, the sharp bend, the long straight line down to the river, and then the new bridge with its orange lamps. It was a road that did not exist in 1966.
Mr Quill shook his head. "There's no other bridge, son. And the number 31 goes over Blackwater. It's on the timetable. I can't change the route."

"But it doesn't work!" said Callum. "You've tried a thousand times!" The driver said nothing. His hands held the big steering wheel very tightly. Behind them, the passengers were listening. Then Dot stood up, with her lantern in her hands, and walked down the aisle to the front.

Then he opened them and looked at Callum. "All right, young man. Show me the way."
Callum stood next to the driver and looked hard into the fog. "After the Kirk Moor sign, there's a big standing stone on the right. Turn left just after it." For a minute there was nothing. Then a tall grey shape appeared on the right, old and silent. "Left!" said Callum. Mr Quill turned the wheel. The bus left the old road and went onto a smaller one, and the fog became a little thinner. Callum looked up at the clock above the driver's head. With a small click, the minute hand moved. It was 23:48.

# Before Midnight

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-the-last-bus-to-ravensby/chapter_5.page_1-1793305297918.mp3
The small road went down towards the river between two stone walls. Callum gave directions, and Mr Quill followed them without a word. Left at the farm. Straight on past the old barn. Careful, there's a sharp bend. The fog was getting thinner and thinner, and the passengers came to the windows to look. Then, in front of them, they saw the new bridge. It was wide and made of grey concrete, with tall orange lamps on each side. It looked very ordinary to Callum. To the people on the bus, it looked like something from another planet.
"Look at those lights," whispered Eddie. "Orange! Like in a dream." Mrs Bramwell put her hand on her heart. The couple in the long coats laughed nervously. Mr Quill slowed down, took a deep breath and drove onto the bridge. Callum held the pole next to him very tightly. He waited for the darkness, for the cold, for the white fog that always took them back. But nothing happened. The wheels rolled smoothly over the concrete, and under them the river shone in the orange light.

In the soft light of the bus, everything looked the way she remembered it. Maybe, Callum thought, tonight Ravensby belonged a little to 1966, and a little to him. Maybe it could belong to both.

The village was quiet and dark. The rain had stopped. The bus went slowly down the main street and stopped in the market square, in front of the old church, at 23:59 exactly. Mr Quill pulled the handbrake, turned off the engine and stood up. He took off his cap. "Ravensby," he said, in a clear, proud voice. "End of the line, ladies and gentlemen. Thank you for travelling with us tonight." Then he looked at Callum and nodded once, like a man who has finished a very long day of work.
The passengers got off one by one. The couple in the long coats went first, holding hands. When they stepped onto the ground, something strange happened: they became lighter, softer, like a photo slowly losing its colours, and then they were gone. They did not look afraid. They looked happy. Mrs Bramwell gave Callum her last peppermint and patted his cheek. Eddie shook his hand and said, "Thanks, mate. Pauline would like you." Then they stepped off the platform and disappeared too, like breath on a cold window.
Dot was the last. She stood on the platform with her lantern and looked across the square, towards a narrow street on the left. Callum knew that street. It was Chapel Street.

At number four, in the front window, a small lamp was shining. "Look," said Dot softly. "June left a light for me." She turned to Callum, and her bright eyes were wet. "You know her, don't you? My little sister. You have her smile." Callum could not speak. He only nodded.
Dot put the turnip lantern in his hands. The candle inside was still burning. "Give her this," she said. "And tell her I kept my promise. Tell her I came home before midnight." She kissed his cheek. Her lips were not cold any more. Then she jumped down from the platform and ran across the square towards Chapel Street, her yellow raincoat shining under the streetlamp. Halfway there, she became light, and then she was gone. Above Callum's head, the church clock began to strike twelve.
On the twelfth stroke, Callum turned around. The bus was not there any more. There was no green double-decker, no Mr Quill, no tyre marks on the wet ground. There was only the empty square, the silent church, and Callum, standing alone in his black cape, with a turnip lantern in his hands.

He walked slowly to Chapel Street. Before he could knock, the door of number four opened. His grandmother was there, in her blue dressing gown, as if she had been waiting for him all night.
"Callum? What are you doing here at this time of—" Then she saw the lantern. She looked at the carved face, at the small crooked teeth, and her hand went to her mouth. "Dot always gave them teeth," she whispered. "Real teeth. Nobody else did that." Callum gave her the lantern and told her everything: the bus, the fog, the bridge, the promise. He thought she would not believe him. But Nan listened until the end, and then she cried and laughed at the same time. "She always kept her promises," she said.
The next morning, Nan turned off the lamp in the front window for the first time in sixty years. Callum found the little paper ticket in his pocket, with its faded purple ink: Fare 1s. 6d. He kept it in his wallet. One year later, on Halloween night, he went back to the bus station in Wetherholme and waited under the big clock. At 23:40, nothing came. At midnight, nothing came. Callum smiled, put his hands in his pockets and walked home through the fog. The number 31 had finally arrived.
