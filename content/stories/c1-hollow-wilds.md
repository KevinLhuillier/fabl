# The Woodcutter's Nephew

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-hollow-wilds/chapter_1.page_1-1792455775270.mp3
The oak had been dead for three winters, and the forest had finally decided to let it go.
Rowan Ashby felt it before he heard it: a faint shudder through the soles of his boots, the kind of tremor that runs ahead of a fall the way a rumour runs ahead of a storm. He stepped back without being told. Ten paces behind him, Uncle Bram lowered his axe, wiped his brow with a forearm as thick as a fencepost, and waited.
The tree groaned. A crow lifted from its crown, complaining loudly, and then the whole grey mass of it tilted — slowly at first, almost reluctantly — before crashing down through the bracken with a sound that rolled across the valley and came back twice.
Silence settled. Somewhere, a woodpecker resumed its work.
"Clean," Bram said, which from him was high praise — for the tree, not for himself.
Rowan grinned anyway. He had turned fourteen at midsummer: lean where his uncle was broad, with a shock of dark hair that defeated every comb and hands already roughened by years of rope and bark. People in the village said he had his mother's eyes, grey-green like moss after rain. They said it the way people mention a ghost — quickly, and then they changed the subject.

They worked through the morning. Bram stripped the fallen oak with short, economical strokes while Rowan dragged the branches to the pile and sorted them by thickness: kindling, fencing, firewood, nothing wasted. That was the first rule of the Ashby household, and very nearly the only one Bram had ever bothered to say out loud. We take what the forest is finished with. Never more.
Other woodcutters in the Hollow Wilds were less particular. They felled whatever stood straight and sold whatever sold. Bram Ashby would walk the woods for days before choosing a single trunk, laying his broad palm against the bark like a physician feeling for a pulse. Rowan had once asked him what he was listening for.
"Whether it's still home," Bram had said, and refused to elaborate.
At noon they sat on the stump to eat — black bread, a wedge of hard cheese, two wrinkled apples from the cellar. Light came down through the canopy in long, slanting columns, thick with drifting pollen. In the old days, the songs claimed, the trees of the Hollow Wilds had grown so tall that their crowns brushed the clouds, and the people who lived beneath them could ask a root to part or a branch to bend, and be answered. The Wood-wardens, they had been called. Every child in the Wilds could recite their names, the way they learned the names of the stars.
No one had commanded Wood in over three hundred years.
These days, the people of the Wilds were simply Marked — Earth, Water, Air or Fire, one element apiece and, very rarely, two — and they drew on the Well inside them, whether they felled trees in the forest, cut stone in the northern mountains or hauled nets on the western coast. The forest remained, vast and ancient and indifferent. It simply no longer listened.

"Nine days till the Marking," Rowan said, through a mouthful of apple.
Bram grunted.
"Tamsin reckons she'll be Earth. Her whole family's Earth, four generations back. Her father can lift a cartwheel out of the mud without touching it." He swallowed. "What were they? My parents?"
The question slipped out before he could stop it. He had asked it before, in different ways and at different ages, and the answer had always been some variation on the same silence.
Bram's jaw worked on a crust of bread for a long moment.
"Your mother," he said at last, "was very good at what she did."
"Which was what?"
"Eat your apple."

By mid-afternoon the cart was loaded, and Patience — a mule whose name had been chosen with considerable irony — was persuaded, after the usual negotiations, to haul it down the track to Brackenford.
The village sat where the forest thinned beside a slow brown river: a huddle of timber houses with roofs of moss-green thatch, a mill, a forge, and a market square that was crowded twice a week and deserted the rest of the time. At its heart stood the shrine, a ring of seven carved posts so old that the weather had worn their faces into smooth, patient suggestions of faces. The Wood-wardens. Children left acorns at their feet for luck. Rowan had stopped doing so at ten, mostly because Tamsin had told him it was babyish — and had then carried on doing it in secret for another two years.
"Rowan! Rowan Ashby!"
Tamsin Reed came through the crowd at a speed that cleared a path ahead of her. She was a head shorter than him, freckled, and permanently windswept, as though she had just come in from a gale even on the stillest of days.
"Have you seen it?" She seized his sleeve. "Look who's examining us this year. Come and look."
Pinned to the board beside the shrine was a sheet of heavy cream paper bearing the seal of Thornhall: a thorned branch encircling a well. The script was formal and faintly impatient, the way official documents always seemed to be.
By order of the Wardens of Thornhall Academy, the Marking of all children of Brackenford and the surrounding villages who have attained their fourteenth year shall take place at the Standing Stone of Brackenford on the first morning of Leaf-Fall. Master Corvin Hale, Head of Fire, will preside, with the examiners of the Academy in attendance. Attendance is compulsory.

Rowan had known the date for as long as he could remember. The Marking always fell on the first morning of Leaf-Fall, as regular as the first frost, and he had been counting the days since midsummer. It was the name in the last lines that made him read the notice twice.
"Hale himself," Tamsin breathed. "Head of Fire. My cousin says he hardly ever bothers coming this far south. Normally they just send a couple of junior examiners with a ledger."
"Your cousin says a lot of things."
"My cousin is usually right." She grinned. "Earth for me. I can feel it already, you know — in my feet. Like the ground's humming."
"That's the mill wheel."
She punched his arm, not gently.
Across the square, the blacksmith's son, Edric Vane, was entertaining a half-circle of younger children by snapping his fingers and conjuring a spark — just one, bright and brief as a firefly. He had been Marked the previous autumn: Fire, as everyone had predicted, since his father had Fire and his grandfather before him. He caught Rowan watching and made a great show of blowing the spark out like a birthday candle.
"Ignore him," Tamsin said. "His Well's about as deep as a puddle."

Rowan was barely listening. Bram had drawn the cart up beside the cooper's yard to unload, and a pedlar Rowan didn't recognise — a stooped, sharp-eyed woman with a tray of tin pots and needles slung from her neck — had sidled up to him and was talking in a low, urgent voice.
Rowan drifted closer, feigning an interest in a basket of eggs.
"…three families from Ashwater," the pedlar was saying. "Gone between dusk and dawn. Doors left wide open, supper still on the table. Not a drop of blood. And on every lintel—" she lowered her voice further still, and Rowan had to strain to catch it, "—a root, drawn in black. Burnt right into the wood."
Bram said nothing. But his hands, which had been gripping a plank to lift it from the cart, had gone utterly still.
"Marked twice, those folk were," the pedlar went on. "That's what they're saying in Ashwater. Two elements, the lot of them. Folk with two never did last long on the open road, did they?" She peered up at him. "You'd know about that, Bram Ashby, I should think."
Bram set the plank down very carefully.
"I know nothing about it," he said. "And neither, if you've any sense, do you."

He paid the cooper without haggling — something Rowan had never once seen him do — and was back on the cart within minutes, his face closed like a shuttered window.
"Uncle—"
"Get in."
Patience, for once, did not argue either.
Bram said nothing the whole way home. That in itself was not unusual; he could go a full day on a dozen words and consider himself talkative. But this was a different kind of silence — tight and watchful, like the hush that falls over the forest before a storm, when even the birds think better of singing.
That evening, Rowan sat by the hearth with his whittling knife and a block of lime wood, working on a figure he had been carving for a week: a fox, curled nose to tail, asleep. Across from him, Bram sharpened the axe he had already sharpened that morning, the whetstone rasping in long, slow strokes.
Twice he got up to check the shutters. He had never checked the shutters in his life.

He bent over the fox again. His hands were not quite steady, and the knife slipped — a clean, shallow stroke that should have taken a curl of wood off the tail and sent it fluttering to the floor.
It didn't fall.
Instead, the shaving curled upward, slowly, the way a fern unfurls in spring, and wound itself once, lightly, around the tip of his finger. It was warm. For a single heartbeat, Rowan could have sworn he felt the faint, distant thrum of something alive.
Then it was only a wood shaving, dry and pale, and he shook it off into the fire, where it flared and was gone.
He looked up. Bram had stopped sharpening. He was staring — not at Rowan, but at the fire, at the spot where the shaving had burned — and his face in the orange light wore an expression Rowan had never seen there before.
"Bed," Bram said quietly. "Long day tomorrow."

Rowan woke some time after midnight to the scrape of the hearthstone.
He lay still in the loft, listening. Below, the fire had sunk to a sullen red glow, and by its light he could make out the bulk of his uncle kneeling on the floor. The broad, flat stone in front of the hearth — the one Rowan had sat on every winter evening of his life — had been levered aside. Bram lifted something from the hollow beneath it: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine.
He unwrapped it the way a man handles something that might break. Inside lay a folded letter, its paper yellowed with age, and pressed flat between its pages was a single leaf — not oak, not ash, not any tree Rowan knew, but long and silvery and veined with the faintest trace of gold.
Bram unfolded the letter and read it. Then he read it again. Peering through a gap between the loft boards, Rowan could make out none of the words, only their shape: a quick, slanting hand, nothing like his uncle's laborious capitals.
Except for the last line. It had been underlined so hard that the pen had torn the page, and as Bram tilted the letter towards the embers, it caught the light for a moment.
Whatever happens, Bram — do not let them test him.

# The Marking

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-hollow-wilds/chapter_2.page_1-1792781503626.mp3
The first morning of Leaf-Fall arrived wrapped in fog, as if the forest had pulled a blanket over its head and had no intention of getting up.
Rowan had barely slept. For nine nights the same words had circled in his head like crows over a field — do not let them test him — and for nine days he had said nothing about them. He had tried. Twice he had opened his mouth at supper, and twice the question had shrivelled before it reached the air. How did you ask a man why he had been reading your dead mother's letters in the middle of the night? How did you admit you had been watching?
So he had done what the Ashbys did best. He had kept quiet and kept working.
Bram was already up when Rowan came down the ladder, stirring a pot of porridge with the grim concentration of a man defusing something dangerous. He had shaved, which he did twice a year at most, and put on the good coat — the brown one with the mended elbow that smelled faintly of cedar from the chest where it lived.

They ate in silence. Halfway through, Bram put down his spoon.
"We could be late," he said.
Rowan looked up. "Late?"
"Patience could throw a shoe. The bridge at Hob's Ford could be out." Bram studied the table as though the grain of the wood might offer him counsel. "Things happen on the road."
For a moment the words hung between them, heavy with everything Rowan was not supposed to know. He thought of the torn page, the underlined line, the hearthstone.
"The notice said attendance was compulsory," he said carefully.
"I can read, boy."
"So what happens to someone who doesn't turn up?"
Bram was silent for a long time. Outside, a wood pigeon was calling the same three notes over and over, as if it had forgotten the rest of the song.
"They get noticed," he said at last. "And being noticed for not coming is worse than being noticed for coming." He stood, scraping back his chair. "Get your boots on."

The Standing Stone of Brackenford was older than the village, older than the shrine, older — some said — than the forest itself. It rose from the meadow beyond the mill like a single grey finger raised in warning: twice the height of a man, crusted with gold and silver lichen, and carved all over with spirals so worn that you could only see them when the sun was low and the shadows did the work.
There were Standing Stones all across the Hollow Wilds — a dozen or so in the southern forest alone, and hundreds more scattered throughout the land: squat grey pillars on the passes of the northern mountains, needles of black basalt rising from the shallows off the western coast, slender white spires on the windswept eastern heights. Nobody knew who had raised them. They were older than every town and village, older even than Thornhall, and no one had ever managed to move a single one, so the children of the Wilds went to the Stones, and never the other way round. They were also the only sure way to read a Well. A Well does not settle until around the fourteenth year, which was why the Marking waited until then: before that age the Stones stayed silent, and afterwards they simply gave the same answer again, as though faintly bored at being asked twice. Every answer, in every village, was recorded at Thornhall, and had been for three hundred years.

By the time Rowan and Bram arrived, half the countryside seemed to have crowded into the meadow. Families had come from Fernhollow and Hob's Ford and the scattered farms along the river, and the fog was lifting off them like steam off a horse. Nineteen children stood in a ragged line before the Stone, scrubbed and combed and dressed in clothes that had clearly been borrowed for the occasion. Their parents stood behind them, pretending not to be nervous and failing.
Tamsin was near the front, bouncing on her heels. She caught Rowan's eye and mouthed something that might have been Earth or might have been hurry up.
The two examiners from Thornhall stood beside the Stone.
The first was a tall man in a long coat of charcoal wool, with close-cropped grey hair and the bearing of someone who had spent his life being obeyed. He wore black leather gloves despite the mild morning, and there was a faint warmth in the air around him, like standing too near a banked fire. Fire, Rowan thought at once. Nobody needed to tell him.

Everyone in the Wilds knew what the answers looked like; they had heard them described a hundred times at a hundred firesides. Earth made the Stone hum, and the ground around it shiver. Water drew beads of dew across its surface, even on the driest day. Air called a wind from nowhere, which spun around the child like a playful dog. Fire made the old spirals glow, one by one, like embers being blown on. And very rarely — once in a generation, if that — the Stone gave two answers at once.
The first child was a tow-headed boy from the mill. His hands were shaking so badly that it took him two attempts to find the Stone. For a long, breathless moment nothing happened. Then a thin, curling breeze rose out of the still morning, lifted his fringe off his forehead and set the grass rippling around his ankles.
"Air," said Master Hale, and the woman with the braid made a note in a small black book.
The boy's mother burst into tears. The meadow broke into applause.

The walk to the Stone could not have been more than twenty paces. It felt like a mile.
Rowan was aware of everything at once and of nothing in particular: the damp grass soaking through his boots, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, a baby crying somewhere at the back, Bram's stillness behind him like a wall. Master Hale watched him approach with an expression of polite, faintly bored patience. The woman with the braid had stopped circling. She was looking at him now, her pen poised above her little black book.
He raised his hands and laid them flat against the Stone.
It was cold — colder than it had any right to be on a mild autumn morning, as though it had drawn all the chill out of the night and kept it for itself. The lichen was rough under his palms. He closed his eyes, because he didn't know what else to do, and waited.
Nothing.
No hum. No dew. No wind. No glow. Only the cold, and the silence, and the sound of his own heart, which seemed to have climbed into his ears.

And then — so faint that afterwards he would wonder whether he had imagined it — something stirred. Not in the Stone. In him. A pull, deep down, as if something were rising towards the surface of a dark pool, slow and patient and very old. Then the cold of the Stone pressed against it, and it shrank back the way a fern curls up at a touch, and sank out of reach.
Then it was gone, and there was only the cold again.
"Again," said Master Hale. "Press harder. Breathe."
Rowan pressed until his wrists ached. He breathed. He thought about the forest, about the oak falling, about the shaving that had curled around his finger like something alive. He thought about his mother.
The Stone did not answer.
After what felt like an hour, Master Hale lifted one gloved hand.
"Enough. You may step back." He did not raise his voice; he did not need to. "No response. Unmarked."
The word dropped into the meadow like a stone into a well, and Rowan heard the ripples spread behind him: a murmur, a hiss of indrawn breath, someone saying poor lad in the tone people use for a lame horse. Unmarked. It happened, everyone knew it happened — one child in fifty, perhaps, born with a Well too shallow to register. They went home. They became farmers, or carters, or woodcutters. They did not go to Thornhall.

The strange thing was that he had always half expected it. He was a woodcutter's nephew from the deep end of the Wilds. He had never really pictured himself at Thornhall, among the sons and daughters of village elders and merchants and old Marked families. # A Crack in the Stone

She came to the clearing at dusk that same day, on foot, as though she had walked the six miles from Brackenford for the pleasure of it.
Rowan saw her first. "Maren Vell." She inclined her head. "Keeper of Records at Thornhall, among other dull titles. May I come in? I've walked a long way, and I'm told that your hospitality is famous throughout the Wilds."
"Whoever told you that was lying."
"Yes," said Maren Vell pleasantly. "I thought they might be. May I come in anyway?"
For a moment Rowan genuinely thought his uncle would shut the door in her face. Then Bram stepped aside, with the air of a man admitting a draught he knew he would regret.
She sat at their table as if she had been sitting there for years. She accepted a bowl of stew and ate it all, complimented the bread, asked the name of the mule, and admired Rowan's half-finished fox on the mantelpiece with what seemed like real interest. At one point a drop of gravy fell onto the cuff of her sleeve; she touched it with one fingertip, and a bead of clear water gathered out of nowhere, rolled across the stain and lifted it away. # Thornhall

The only way onto the hill was across the water.

In the grey morning light, Rowan could see the line of the shore quite clearly from up here: the torn-up bank, the black tangle of roots thrust up through the ice, and the trees beyond, every one of them still bowed towards the hill.

They were all waiting for him. The Warden, in a high-backed chair by the fire. Master Hale, standing by the window with his gloved hands clasped behind him. Mistress Crane, folded into a corner like a grey umbrella. Maren Vell, at a writing desk with a great green ledger open in front of her. Then she reached out and took something from the table beside her chair, and held it out to him. It was a twig. A short, dry, grey twig of blackthorn, quite dead.
"Show me," she said.

Rowan's mouth went dry. He took the twig. He held it in both hands and closed his eyes, and the whole room seemed to lean in towards him, waiting.
He did not reach. He did not pull. He tried very hard not to try at all. He sat and breathed and listened, the way he had on the ice, and for a long and terrible moment he heard nothing but the crackle of the fire and the beating of his own heart.
Then, very faintly, something in the twig stirred.
It was so much smaller than the Elderwood — a single, tiny, sleepy thread of life, curled up tight in the dead wood like a seed in a husk. He listened to it. It listened back. And slowly, so slowly that he hardly felt it happen, a single green bud swelled at the tip of the twig, split, and opened into a small white five-petalled flower.

Master Hale caught him on the stairs.

The letters began arriving the following week, in spite of the snow.
They came from all over the Hollow Wilds, carried up from Mossbridge on sledges by carters who charged three times the usual rate and were glad to get it. Letters from village elders asking whether the Wood-boy might visit and bless their orchards. Letters from families whose fields had failed, asking whether he might come and make them grow. Letters from scholars in Rootholm asking to examine him. A letter from a rich merchant on the western coast, offering him a house and a pension for life if he would come and live under his protection. Master Grey brought them up to Hearthside in armfuls, and the Warden had every one of them burned unanswered.

On the ninth evening after he left the infirmary, he was sitting in the common room at Hearthside with Tamsin and the Tanners, trying to ignore the cluster of first-years who were watching him from across the room in case he made something bloom, when the door banged open and Master Grey came in on a gust of snow.
