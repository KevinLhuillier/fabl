# The Blue Tin

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_1.page_1-1793544850674.mp3
It was a grey morning in November, and it was raining in Plymouth. Tom Hollis stood at the window of the small flat on Rope Street and watched the rain. He was fourteen years old. He was tall and thin, with brown hair that was always too long, because nobody had time to cut it. Behind him, in the little kitchen, his sister Lily was making tea. Their brother Sam was sitting at the table. He was six, and he was very quiet today.
Yesterday was the funeral. Their mother, Anne Hollis, was dead. She was ill for a long time, all through the summer and the autumn. Tom and Lily looked after her every day. They cooked, they cleaned, they went to the shops and the chemist. But in the end, the doctor shook his head, and two weeks ago, on a cold night, she closed her eyes and did not open them again.
Their father was dead too. He was a fisherman, and two years ago his boat went down in a big storm near the coast of France. Sam did not really remember him. Lily remembered his laugh and his big warm hands. Tom remembered everything: his father's old blue coat, his pipe, the songs he sang on Sunday mornings. Sometimes, at night, Tom still heard those songs in his head.

"Here is your tea," said Lily. She was eleven, small and quick, with dark hair in two long plaits and very serious grey eyes. She put three cups on the table. There was no milk, because the milk was finished. There was a little bread and a little jam. That was all. "Eat something, Sam," she said gently. Sam took a piece of bread, but he did not eat it. He only held it in his hand and looked at the rain.
At ten o'clock, somebody knocked on the door. It was a hard, quick knock, the knock of a person who is always in a hurry. Tom opened the door. A tall woman stood on the stairs. She wore a long grey coat and a grey hat, and she carried a black bag full of papers. Her face was thin and her mouth was a straight line. "Good morning," she said. "I am Mrs Crane, from the Children's Office. May I come in?"
Mrs Crane sat down at the kitchen table. She did not take off her hat. She took some papers out of her bag and put them in front of her. She looked at the empty milk bottle, at the old curtains, at the cold fireplace. Then she looked at the three children. "I am very sorry about your mother," she said, but her voice was not sorry. It was the voice of a woman who says the same words every day.

"You have no father and no mother," she went on. "Do you have any other family? Uncles? Aunts? Grandparents?" Tom shook his head. "Our grandmother lived in Cornwall," he said. "But she died last year." Mrs Crane wrote something on her paper. "So there is nobody," she said. "Then you cannot stay here. Three children cannot live alone. It is not possible, and it is not allowed."
"Where will we go?" asked Lily. Her voice was very small. Mrs Crane looked at her papers again. "The little boy will go to St Mary's Home for Young Children, in Exeter," she said. "You, Lily, will go to a home for girls in Bristol. And Thomas is fourteen, so he can work. There is a place for him on a farm near Taunton. The farmer needs a strong boy."
For a moment, nobody said anything. Then Tom said, "No. We stay together." His voice was louder than he wanted. Mrs Crane did not look up. "I am afraid that is not your decision, young man," she said. "There are rules. A home for small children is not a home for big boys. It is better this way. You can write letters to each other." She put the papers back in her bag and stood up.

"I will come back on Monday morning at nine o'clock," she said at the door. "Have your things ready. One small bag each. A car will take you to the station." Then she went down the stairs, and they heard the street door close behind her. Sam began to cry. He did not cry loudly. He cried in a very quiet way, and that was worse. Lily put her arms around him and looked at Tom over his head.
"It is Friday today," said Lily. "We have three days." Tom walked up and down the small kitchen. He felt angry and afraid at the same time. "I promised Mum," he said. "Before she died, she held my hand and said, Look after Lily and Sam. Don't let anybody take them away. I promised her, Lily. I can't go to a farm in Taunton and leave you."
That evening, they started to put their mother's things in boxes. It was a sad job. Her dresses still smelled of her soap. Her reading glasses were on the table next to her bed, on top of a library book. Lily folded everything carefully. Sam sat on the bed and held his mother's old scarf. Tom opened the drawer of the little cupboard. At the back, under some old letters, he found a blue tin.

It was an old biscuit tin, with a picture of a ship on the top. Tom knew it. When he was small, his mother kept buttons in it. He opened it slowly. There were no buttons inside now. There was some money: four pounds and seven shillings, in old notes and coins. There was a photograph of their father on his boat, laughing. And there was a big iron key, with a piece of string and a little paper label.
Tom read the label. The writing was his mother's: Gull Cottage, Porthwen. "Lily," he said. "Come and look at this." Lily came and looked. "Porthwen," she said. "That's Grandma Rose's village. Her house by the sea. We went there when Dad was alive. Do you remember, Tom? There was a garden with apple trees, and you could see the boats from the window."
"I remember," said Tom. He remembered the little white house on the hill above the harbour, the blue door, the smell of bread and salt. He remembered Grandma Rose, with her white hair and her strong arms, who always had a cake in the oven. After she died, nobody went to the house. Their mother was already ill. "Does anybody live there now?" asked Lily. "I don't know," said Tom. "But we have the key."

They looked at each other for a long time. They did not need to say anything. Lily understood immediately. Her eyes became very big. "Tom," she said slowly, "are you thinking what I'm thinking?" Tom nodded. "Mrs Crane comes on Monday at nine," he said. "If we are not here on Monday at nine, she cannot take us anywhere."
"But it's wrong," said Lily. "We can't just run away. We are children. What will we eat? Where will we get money? And Sam is only six." Tom sat down on the bed next to her. "I know," he said. "I know it's a crazy idea. But listen. If we go with Mrs Crane, we will never live together again. Never. Sam will grow up with strangers. Do you want that?"
Lily looked at Sam. He was asleep now, on their mother's bed, with the old scarf under his cheek. His face was still wet from crying. She thought about Exeter, and Bristol, and Taunton. Three cities. Three different lives. Letters once a month, maybe, and then not so often, and then never. She took a deep breath. "All right," she said. "But we do it properly. We make a plan."

So they made a plan. They sat at the kitchen table with a pencil and the back of an old envelope. Lily wrote everything down, because her writing was better. Money: four pounds seven shillings. Train to Penzance: how much? Food for the journey. Warm clothes. Blankets. Mum's photograph. Dad's photograph. The key. Tom's knife. Sam's teddy bear. "We can't carry everything," said Tom. "Only what is really important."
On Saturday, Tom went to the station and asked about trains. The man behind the window told him a child's ticket to Penzance was eleven shillings. "Three children?" he asked, and looked at Tom over his glasses. "My aunt is meeting us," Tom said quickly. His face became hot. It was his first lie, and he did not like it. But the man only nodded. "There's a train at half past six on Sunday evening," he said.
That night, Tom could not sleep. He lay in his bed and listened to the rain on the roof. He thought about the journey. Penzance was the end of the railway line. After that, Porthwen was still eight miles away, along the coast. There was a bus, maybe, but buses cost money. They could walk. Eight miles was not so far, he told himself. But with Sam and with heavy bags, in November, in the dark? He did not know. He only knew that he could not stay.

On Sunday, they packed. Lily took three blankets, all their warm jumpers and socks, two towels, soap, a comb, and a small saucepan. Tom took his father's old fishing knife, a box of matches, a candle, some string, and the photographs. Sam took his teddy bear, whose name was Captain, and a book about ships. They took bread, cheese, four apples and a bottle of water. It was not much. But it was everything they had.
Before they left, Lily wrote a short letter and put it on the kitchen table. Dear Mrs Crane, she wrote. Thank you for your help. We are going to stay with family. Please do not worry about us. Yours sincerely, Thomas, Lily and Samuel Hollis. "It's not completely a lie," she said. "We are family. And we are going to stay with ourselves." Tom almost smiled.
At six o'clock, it was already dark. They put on their coats. Tom wore his father's old blue coat, which was much too big for him, but it was warm. He looked around the flat one last time. The empty bed. The cold kitchen. The window where their mother sat every evening to watch the street. "Goodbye," he said quietly. Then he closed the door and put the key under the mat.

The streets were wet and shiny under the lamps. They walked quickly to the station, Tom in front with the big bag, Lily behind with Sam's hand in hers. Nobody looked at them. In the station, there were soldiers and old ladies and a man with a dog. Tom bought three tickets. His hands were shaking a little when he put the money on the counter. Thirty-three shillings. Now they had less than three pounds.
The train was old and noisy and full of smoke. They found an empty compartment and sat close together by the window. When the train began to move, Sam pressed his face against the glass. "Where are we going, Tom?" he asked. Tom put his arm around him. "We are going home, Sammy," he said. "To Grandma's house by the sea." And slowly, slowly, the lights of Plymouth disappeared behind them in the rain.

# The Road to the Sea

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_2.page_1-1793545017112.mp3
The train went slowly through the night. Outside the window, everything was black. Sometimes they saw the lights of a small station, or a farmhouse, or a village, and then the black came back. The train stopped many times. Doors opened and closed. People got on and got off. A conductor came and looked at their tickets. He was an old man with a white moustache. He did not ask any questions. He only said, "Penzance, end of the line," and went away.
Sam fell asleep with his head on Lily's lap. Lily did not sleep. She looked at the dark window and saw her own face in the glass. She looked tired and older than eleven. She thought about Mrs Crane. Tomorrow morning at nine, Mrs Crane will climb the stairs on Rope Street. She will knock. Nobody will answer. She will find the key under the mat and the letter on the table. What will she do then? Will she call the police?

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_2.page_2-1793545035981.mp3
"Tom," she whispered. "Are you awake?" "Yes," said Tom. He was sitting opposite her, with his arms around the big bag. "What if they look for us?" Lily asked. Tom was quiet for a moment. "They will look for us in Plymouth," he said. "Or in Exeter, or Bristol. Nobody knows about Grandma's house. Mum never talked about it to anybody. And the letter says family. They will think of an aunt or a cousin, not an empty house in Cornwall."
At half past ten, the train arrived in Penzance. The station was cold and almost empty. A strong wind came from the sea, and they could smell salt and seaweed. Lily woke Sam up. He was confused and cross, and he did not want to walk. Tom carried him on his back, and Lily carried the big bag. It was very heavy. They went out of the station and stood on the dark street. There was no bus. There was nobody.
"We can't walk eight miles now," said Lily. "Not in the dark." Tom looked around. Next to the station, there was a small waiting room with a wooden bench and a big clock on the wall. The door was not locked. Inside, it was not warm, but it was dry, and there was no wind. "We'll sleep here," Tom said. "We'll leave very early in the morning, before people come." They put the blankets on the bench and lay down close together.

Tom did not sleep much. Every time a door banged or the wind whistled, he opened his eyes. He listened to the big clock: tick, tock, tick, tock. At one o'clock, a man walked past the window with a lamp, and Tom held his breath. But the man did not come in. At three o'clock, it began to rain again. At five o'clock, the sky was still black, but Tom sat up. "Lily," he said. "Wake up. It's time."
They ate a little bread and cheese and one apple, and they drank some water. Then they folded the blankets and left the waiting room. The rain had stopped. The streets of Penzance were quiet and grey. A milkman was driving his cart slowly along the road, and the horse's feet made a loud noise on the stones. Tom asked him the way to Porthwen. The milkman pointed with his whip. "Along the coast road, west," he said. "You'll see the sign. Long way for little legs, though."
They walked out of the town. Slowly, the sky became lighter. First it was dark blue, then grey, then pale pink above the hills. On their left, the sea appeared. It was enormous and silver, and the waves were white near the rocks. Sam stopped and looked at it with his mouth open. "Is that the sea?" he asked. "The real sea?" "Yes," said Lily. "The real sea. We are going to live next to it."

The road was narrow, with high stone walls on both sides. Behind the walls were small green fields with cows and sheep. Sometimes a car or a lorry passed, and they pressed themselves against the wall. The bag became heavier and heavier. Tom and Lily carried it together, one hand each. Sam walked between them. After two hours, his feet hurt and he began to cry. "I can't walk anymore," he said. "My legs are broken."
"Your legs are not broken," said Lily, "but they are tired. Let's sit down for five minutes." They sat on a big stone at the side of the road. Tom took out the last three apples. They ate them slowly, and they watched the seagulls flying over the fields. The birds screamed and turned in the wind. "They're laughing at us," said Sam. Lily laughed too. It was the first time she laughed in many weeks, and it felt strange.
While they were sitting there, they heard a noise on the road. It was a cart, pulled by a big brown horse. A farmer sat on it. He was a large man with a red face and a cap. The cart was full of cabbages. When he saw the three children, he stopped. "Hello there," he said. "Where are you three going, so early, with that big bag?" Tom stood up quickly. His heart was beating fast.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_2.page_5-1793545095073.mp3
"To Porthwen," he said. "To our grandmother's house." This was almost true. The farmer looked at them for a long moment. He looked at Sam's wet face, at Lily's muddy shoes, at Tom's coat, which was too big. "Porthwen's on my way," he said at last. "I'm going to the market at St Just. Get up in the back, with the cabbages. It's not a carriage for a queen, but it's better than walking."
They climbed into the cart and sat between the cabbages. The cabbages were cold and wet, and they smelled strong, but nobody cared. The horse walked slowly, and the cart went up and down the hills. Sam fell asleep again, with his head on a cabbage. The farmer sang an old song in a deep voice. Tom did not know the words, but he knew the tune. His father sometimes sang it on the boat.
After an hour, the farmer stopped at the top of a hill. "There you are," he said. "Porthwen." Below them, between two green hills, was a little village. There was a small harbour with a stone wall, and six or seven fishing boats were sitting on the sand, because the tide was out. The houses were white and grey, with dark roofs. Smoke came out of the chimneys. A church stood on one side, and on the other side, a little higher, was a white house alone.

"Thank you very much," said Tom, when they were down from the cart. "Can I pay you something?" The farmer laughed. "Pay me? For a ride with my cabbages? No, boy. Keep your money." He looked at them again, and his face became more serious. "Who is your grandmother, then?" he asked. "Rose Hollis," said Lily. The farmer was quiet. "Rose Hollis," he said slowly. "Well. Well, well." Then he shook the reins, and the horse walked on.
"Did he know Grandma was dead?" asked Lily. "Probably," said Tom. "It's a small village. Everybody knows everything." This thought made him a little afraid. In Plymouth, nobody looked at them. Here, maybe, everybody will look. They walked down the hill towards the village, but they did not go into the main street. Tom remembered a small path behind the church. It went up the hill, between bushes and old trees, to Grandma's house.
The path was muddy and full of brambles. The brambles caught their coats and scratched their hands. But after ten minutes, they arrived at a low stone wall with a wooden gate. The gate was broken and hung on one side. Behind it was a garden, and in the garden were three old apple trees, with no leaves now. The grass was very long. And there was the house. Gull Cottage. Their grandmother's house.

It was smaller than Tom remembered. The white walls were now grey and green in places. The blue paint on the door was old, and some of it was gone. One window was broken, and somebody had put a piece of wood over it. There were dead leaves everywhere, and a big pile of seaweed near the wall, brought by the wind. But the roof was still there. The chimney was still there. And from the front of the house, you could see the whole harbour and the sea.
Tom took the iron key out of his pocket. His hand was shaking. He put the key in the lock and turned it. At first, it did not move. The lock was old and full of rust. He tried again, harder, with both hands. There was a loud click. Slowly, with a long noise like an old man sighing, the blue door opened. A smell of dust and cold and wet wood came out. They stood in the doorway and looked inside.

It was dark. The only light came from the door and from one small window. They saw a kitchen with a stone floor, a big black stove, a wooden table and four chairs. There were white sheets over the chairs and over an old armchair near the fire. There were cobwebs in every corner. A mouse ran across the floor and disappeared under the cupboard. Sam held Lily's hand very tightly. "Is it a ghost house?" he whispered.
"No," said Lily. "It's Grandma's house. It's only sleeping. We are going to wake it up." She put down the bag and took off her coat. "Tom, open the shutters. Sam, you can take the sheets off the chairs. Carefully. Shake them outside." She did not feel brave. She felt cold and tired and very small. But she knew that if she sat down and cried, Sam would cry too. So she did not sit down.
Tom opened the shutters, and grey light came into the room. Now they could see better. The house had only four rooms: the kitchen, a small sitting room, and two bedrooms upstairs under the roof. In the sitting room, there was a big bookcase full of old books and a clock on the wall that did not work. Upstairs, there were beds with old mattresses and a wardrobe. In one bedroom, rain came through the ceiling, and there was a brown stain on the floor.

In the kitchen, Lily opened the cupboards. They were almost empty. She found some plates and cups, a teapot with a chipped lid, two old pans, a jar of salt and a jar of sugar that was hard like a stone. On a shelf, she found a thick notebook with a red cover. Tom went outside to look for wood. He carried three big armfuls up the hill. Then he cleaned the old stove, put in some paper and wood, and lit a match. The first match went out. The second match went out. The third match caught, and a small yellow flame began to dance.

# An Empty Cupboard

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_3.page_1-1793545196421.mp3
When Lily woke up, the fire was dead and the kitchen was cold. Grey light came through the window. For a moment, she did not know where she was. Then she heard the sea and the seagulls, and she remembered. Porthwen. Grandma's house. Their house. Tom was already awake. He was sitting at the table with the blue tin in front of him, and he was counting the money. His face was serious.
"Two pounds and eighteen shillings," he said. "That's all." Lily sat down next to him. "How long can we live with that?" she asked. Tom did not know. In Plymouth, their mother paid the rent, the coal, the food, the gas. He never thought about the price of things. "We don't pay rent here," he said. "That's good. But we need food every day. Bread, milk, potatoes. And we need to repair the window and the roof before winter."
Sam woke up and sat up in the blankets. His hair stood up on one side. "I'm hungry," he said. Lily looked at the empty cupboard. There was nothing. Not one piece of bread. Not one apple. Only the old sugar, as hard as a stone. "I'll go to the village," said Tom, and he stood up. "There must be a shop. I'll buy bread and milk and some potatoes." He took some coins from the tin and put them in his pocket.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_3.page_2-1793545217837.mp3
"Wait," said Lily. "What will you say? People will ask questions." Tom stopped at the door. She was right. In a small village, a new boy is big news. "I'll say we are Rose Hollis's grandchildren," he said slowly. "That's true. I'll say our mother sent us to clean the house, and she will come later." Lily bit her lip. "That's a lie," she said. "I know," said Tom. "But it's a small one. And it keeps us together."
He walked down the path behind the church. The morning was cold and bright, and the sea was very blue now. In the harbour, the tide was in, and the fishing boats were moving gently on the water. Some men were working on the quay. They were mending nets and carrying boxes. They looked up when Tom passed. One of them said "Morning," and Tom said "Morning" too, and walked on quickly, with his hands in the pockets of his father's coat.
In the main street, there were only a few shops: a post office, a butcher, a small shop that sold everything, and a bakery. The bakery had a green door and a window full of bread and buns. A wonderful smell came out of it. Tom's stomach made a loud noise. He opened the door, and a little bell rang above his head. Behind the counter stood a round woman with red cheeks, a white apron and flour on her arms.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/a2-the-gull-cottage/chapter_3.page_3-1793545238196.mp3
"Good morning, my love," she said. "What can I get you?" Tom asked for a large loaf of bread. The woman put it in paper. She looked at him with bright, curious eyes. "You're not from here," she said. "I know every face in Porthwen, and I don't know yours." Tom took a breath. "I'm Tom Hollis," he said. "Rose Hollis was my grandmother. We are staying in her house for a while."
The woman put her hands on the counter. "Rose's grandson!" she said. "Well, I never! I'm Mrs Pascoe. Rose was my good friend for forty years. She taught me to make saffron buns, you know. Nobody made saffron buns like Rose." Then her face changed. "We? Who is we, my love? Is your mother here?" Tom looked at the bread. "My sister and my brother," he said. "Mum is... Mum will come later."
Mrs Pascoe did not say anything for a moment. She looked at Tom's thin face, at his coat with its long sleeves, at his hands, which were red from the cold. Tom felt that she could see inside his head. Then she smiled. "Fourpence for the loaf," she said. Tom gave her the coins. Then she took two big saffron buns from the window and put them in the paper too. "And these are a present," she said. "For Rose's grandchildren. Welcome to Porthwen."

Tom said thank you and almost ran out of the shop. He bought a small bottle of milk, a bag of potatoes, some tea and a piece of cheese in the other shop. Everything was more expensive than he thought. When he counted his money in the street, he had spent nearly four shillings. At this speed, the money in the blue tin will be finished before Christmas. He walked back up the hill slowly, carrying the bags, and thinking hard.
Meanwhile, Lily was in the garden. She wanted to know what was there. The grass was long and wet, and there were nettles everywhere. Under them, she found some leeks, thin and green but still alive. And at the end of the bed, a row of big green cabbages.

At eleven o'clock, a door opened, and a young man called, "Penrose and Hollis." They went into a big room with tall windows. At a long table sat five people: three men and two women. In the middle sat an old man with white hair and gold glasses. His name was Mr Lanyon, and he was the chairman. At the end of the table, with her papers in front of her, sat Mrs Crane. She did not look at them.
Mr Lanyon asked many questions. He asked Walter about his age, his health, his house, his money, his work. Walter answered slowly and honestly. He did not try to look younger or richer. He asked Mrs Pascoe how often she saw the children. "Every day, sir," she said. "And twice on Sundays." He asked Lily about school and about the bakery. Lily answered in a clear voice, and Tom was very proud of her.
Then Mr Lanyon looked at Tom over his gold glasses. "Thomas Hollis," he said. "In November, you ran away from Plymouth with your brother and sister. You lied to many people. You lived without an adult for two months. Your brother became very ill. Why should this committee believe that you will be responsible now?" The room was very quiet. Tom stood up. His legs were shaking, so he put his hands on the back of the chair.

"You're right, sir," he said. "I lied. And I made mistakes. Big mistakes. When Sam was ill, I was very afraid, and I understood that I can't do everything alone. Nobody can." He took a breath. "Before my mother died, she asked me to look after Lily and Sam. I thought that meant: never let anybody help us. But I was wrong. Looking after them means finding the right people to help. And I found them. They're here."
He sat down. Nobody said anything for a long moment. Mr Lanyon looked at him, and then he looked at the big brown envelope on the table, and then he looked at Mrs Crane. "Mrs Crane," he said. "You have followed this case from the beginning. What is your opinion?" Mrs Crane stood up. She held her papers, but she did not read them. She looked at the three children, one by one, and then at the committee.
"In November, I wanted to send these children to three different places," she said. "I followed the rules. The rules said it was best." She paused. "I was wrong. I visited Porthwen twice. I saw a family. Not a perfect family. An unusual family. But a real one. In twenty years in this job, I have rarely seen children so well looked after. My recommendation is that Mr Penrose becomes their guardian, and that the children stay together. In Porthwen."

The committee went into another room to talk. Walter, Mrs Pascoe and the children waited in the corridor again. It was the longest twenty minutes of Tom's life. Sam fell asleep against Lily's shoulder. Mrs Pascoe held Lily's hand. Walter looked at the ceiling and moved his lips without making a sound. Tom thought he was praying. Then the door opened, and the young man said, "Please come back in."
Mr Lanyon stood behind the long table. He held a piece of paper. "The committee has made its decision," he said. "Mr Walter Penrose, of Porthwen, is named the legal guardian of Thomas, Lillian and Samuel Hollis, until Thomas is eighteen years old. Mrs Crane will visit four times a year. The children will stay together." He looked up from the paper, and for the first time, he smiled. "Good luck to you all."
Mrs Pascoe screamed. She did not mean to, but she did, and then she put her hand over her mouth and laughed and cried at the same time. Lily threw her arms around Tom. Sam woke up and asked, "What? What happened?" and Walter picked him up with both arms, the old one and the new one, and said, "You're staying, Sam. You're staying with me." Sam thought about it. "And Biscuit?" he asked. "And Biscuit," said Walter.

In the corridor, Mrs Crane was putting on her coat. Tom went to her. He did not know what to say, so he held out his hand. Mrs Crane looked at it. Then she shook it, firmly, like a man. "Thank you," said Tom. "For telling the truth." Mrs Crane nodded. "You told the truth too, Thomas," she said. "In the end. That's what matters." She opened her black bag. Inside, between the papers, was a small, strange, slightly burnt dough elephant.
They took the bus back to Porthwen in the afternoon. When it came over the top of the hill, they saw the village below them, and they could not believe their eyes. There were flags on the quay. There were people everywhere: fishermen, children, the vicar, the butcher, old Mrs Rowe in a chair. Somebody had painted a big sign on a sheet and hung it between two boats. It said, in red letters: WELCOME HOME.
That evening, there was a party on the quay, with music and dancing and more saffron buns than anybody could eat. Jack Trevaskis played the fiddle, and Walter played his harmonica, and Miss Treloar taught Sam and Billy an old Cornish dance. Late in the evening, when the stars came out, Walter took Tom, Lily and Sam aside. "I have one more thing to say," he said. "And I want to say it now, while I'm brave."

"My house is cold and dark, down there by the water," he said. "And it's too sad. Too many old memories. But your house is up there on the hill, with the best view in Porthwen, and a garden, and apple trees, and Rose's kitchen." He looked at them. "So I was thinking. They moved at the beginning of May. The fishermen carried Walter's things up the hill: his armchair, his books, his harmonica, the curtains his wife made. Biscuit chose her place immediately, in front of the stove, and nobody could move her. The apple trees were covered with pink and white flowers. Walter sat in the garden in the evenings and smoked his pipe and watched the boats, and Sam sat next to him and asked a hundred questions.

One evening, Tom took the blue tin out of the drawer. He sat at the kitchen table with Lily and Sam. Inside, there was still a little money, the photograph of their father on his boat, and the paper label: Gull Cottage, Porthwen. Lily added the photograph of their mother. Sam added his jam jar of pennies, which was full now. "For a boat," he said. "One day." Tom took the big iron key and put it in the tin too. They did not need to hide it anymore.
Then Tom went outside and stood at the bottom of the garden, where he could see the whole harbour. The sun was going down into the sea, and the sky was red. Red sky at night, sailors' delight. Below him, the boats were coming home, one by one. Behind him, in the yellow window of Gull Cottage, he could hear Lily laughing and Sam singing and Walter's harmonica. Tom looked at the sky. "I kept my promise, Mum," he said quietly. "We're together. We're home."
