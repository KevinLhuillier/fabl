# The Red Box

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_1.page_1-1792915095339.mp3
Every village has its own small traditions, and Ellerdale had a strange one. On the first day of December, Margaret Penrose took a little red box out of the cupboard behind the counter of the post office and put it next to the door. It was made of painted wood, and someone had written on it in white letters, a long time ago: NORTH POLE. Children from the village and from the farms around it put their letters to Father Christmas inside. And every year, every single letter got an answer.
Nobody in the village asked who wrote the answers. It was a kind of secret that everybody knew and nobody said. Margaret was sixty-one. She had been the postmistress of Ellerdale for twenty-two years, since the old postman, Walter Pike, had retired. It was Walter who had made the red box, back in 1968, and it was Walter who had answered the letters for more than thirty years. When he gave Margaret the keys of the post office, he also gave her the box. "Somebody has to answer them," he said. "Children need to know that someone is listening."

Ellerdale was a small village in the hills of Cumbria, in the north-west of England. It had one church, one pub, one bakery, a school with forty children and a river that was too cold for swimming even in August. In winter the hills turned white and the sheep came down to the fields near the houses. The post office was also the village shop. You could buy stamps there, but also milk, bread, newspapers, birthday cards and, in December, little bags of chocolate coins.
That evening, after she closed the shop, Margaret opened the red box. There were already seven letters inside. She smiled. The children of Ellerdale were always quick. She put the letters in her coat pocket and walked home through the snow. Her house was at the end of Church Lane, a small stone cottage with a green door. Inside, her cat Biscuit was waiting for her on the stairs, with the face of a cat who has not eaten for three weeks. He had eaten at lunchtime.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_1.page_3-1792915122022.mp3
Margaret fed the cat, made a cup of tea and lit the fire. Then she sat in her old armchair, put on her glasses and opened the first letter. This was her favourite moment of the year. Her husband Arthur had died six years before, and the house was often too quiet. But in December, the letters filled it with voices. Small, serious, funny voices. For a few weeks, she was never really alone.
The first letter was from Lily Hughes, who was six. She wanted a unicorn. "But a small one please," she wrote, "because our garden is small and Dad says no." The second was from Oliver Grant, who wanted a new bike and, if possible, a different little brother, "one who doesn't bite". Margaret laughed out loud. Biscuit looked at her with disapproval. The third, fourth and fifth letters asked for video games, a guitar and a puppy. The sixth was from a girl who only wanted snow on Christmas Day. "Well," Margaret said to the cat, "that one is easy. Look outside."

The seventh letter was different. The envelope was blue, not white, and there was no name on the back. Most children wrote their full name and address, because they were afraid that Father Christmas would bring their presents to the wrong house. This envelope said only: FATHER CHRISTMAS, NORTH POLE, VERY URGENT. Margaret opened it carefully. Inside, there was one page from a school exercise book, written in pencil. The handwriting was large and a little untidy, and some words were spelled wrong. She read it slowly.
"Dear Father Christmas. I don't want a present this year. I only want one thing. Please can you make the church clock ring again on Christmas Eve? My grandad looked after it for years and years. It stopped the day he fell. Now he is in the hospital on the hill and he is very sad because everything is quiet. If he hears the bell, he will know that he can come home. I live in the house with the blue door. Please don't tell my mum. She cries when we talk about Grandad. Love from S. (eight and a half). P.S. My dog says hello."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_1.page_5-1792915158838.mp3
Under the words, there was a drawing of a dog. It was a small, round dog with short legs, a long tail and one very strange detail: its left ear was up and its right ear was down, as if it was asking a question. Margaret looked at the drawing for a long time. Then she read the letter again. Then a third time. The fire was warm, but she felt a little cold. In twenty-two years, she had read thousands of letters. Children asked for toys, for animals, for snow. Sometimes they asked for things she could not give, like a father who had gone away or a grandmother who had died. But nobody had ever asked her for a bell.
She knew the church clock, of course. Everybody in Ellerdale knew it. For as long as she could remember, it had rung every hour, day and night, and the village had lived by it. Then, one day last spring, it had simply stopped. She had noticed the silence for a week or two, and then, like everybody else, she had stopped noticing. Now she put the blue letter on the table, next to her tea, and looked at it. "No name," she said to Biscuit. "No address. Only a blue door and a dog with a funny ear." The cat closed his eyes. Margaret did not. She already knew that she was going to find this child.

# The Silent Clock

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_2.page_1-1792915170820.mp3
On Wednesday morning, Margaret opened the post office at half past eight, as usual. She sold stamps to old Mr Hughes, a newspaper to the farmer from Low Beck and three bags of chocolate coins to a mother with a very loud baby. But all morning, she kept looking out of the window at the church across the road. At lunchtime, she put a sign on the door, BACK IN TWENTY MINUTES, and crossed the road with her hands deep in her pockets.
St Oswald's was an old church, built of grey stone nine hundred years ago. Its square tower was the tallest thing in the village, and near the top, on the side facing the road, there was a large white clock with black numbers. Margaret stood under it and looked up. The two black hands showed ten past four. They had shown ten past four since March. It was strange, she thought, how quickly people got used to something broken. Every day, two hundred people walked past this clock, and nobody looked at it anymore.

The church door was open. Inside, it was almost as cold as outside, and it smelled of candles and old books. The vicar, Reverend Ruth Amos, was standing on a chair near the altar. She was trying to put a big silver star on top of a Christmas tree that was too tall for the room. She was a tall woman of about forty, with short grey hair and paint on her sleeves. She had arrived in Ellerdale in September, and people still called her "the new vicar".
"Margaret! Perfect timing," said Ruth. "Can you hold the chair? I'm going to fall and break something, and I'd prefer it to be the tree, not me." Margaret held the chair. After two more tries, the star was on the tree, slightly crooked. Ruth climbed down and looked at it. "Good enough," she said. "Nobody looks up anyway." "That's funny," said Margaret. "I wanted to ask you about something up there. The clock. Why doesn't it work anymore?"

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_2.page_3-1792915197612.mp3
Ruth sighed and sat down on the nearest bench. "Ah. The clock. Everybody asked me about it when I arrived, and then everybody forgot. Come, sit down." She explained what she knew. The clock was a mechanical one, more than a hundred and fifty years old. It had no electricity and no battery. Someone had to climb the tower once a week and wind it with a big iron key, like an enormous watch. Every hour, a hammer inside the tower hit the bell, and that was the sound the village heard.
"For years, an old man from the village looked after it," Ruth said. "He did it for free, because he loved it. But last March he had an accident at home, and he went into hospital. Nobody else knew how to wind it. And the key went with him, I think, or he put it somewhere safe. Nobody can find it." She shrugged. "I called a company in Carlisle. They came and looked. They said the hammer is broken too, and some other part inside. They want three thousand pounds to repair it."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_2.page_4-1792915210279.mp3
"Three thousand pounds!" said Margaret. "I know," said Ruth. "The church roof needs repairs too, and the heating, as you can feel. We have about four hundred pounds in the bank. So the clock waits." Margaret wanted to ask the name of the old man. But Ruth stood up suddenly, because a group of children from the school had arrived with their teacher to practise songs for the Christmas concert. In a moment, the church was full of noise, coats and wet boots.
Margaret watched the children for a minute. There were about twenty of them, between six and ten years old. Was S. one of them? Maybe. Probably. She looked at their faces, but children's faces tell you nothing about their letters. One small boy was singing very loudly and very badly. A girl with red boots was laughing at him. Margaret smiled, said goodbye to Ruth with a little wave, and went back to the post office. She was ten minutes late. Mr Hughes was waiting outside, with the face of a man who has been waiting for ten years.

That afternoon, between customers, she took a piece of paper and wrote down what she knew. It was a short list. One: S. was eight and a half. Two: S. lived in a house with a blue door. Three: S. had a dog with one ear up and one ear down. Four: S.'s grandfather had looked after the church clock, and he was now in the hospital on the hill. That meant Fellside, the small cottage hospital above the village, where old people went after an operation or a fall. Five: S.'s mother cried when she talked about him.
Then she wrote a sixth line, and underlined it twice: Three thousand pounds. She looked at the list for a long time. The first five lines were a mystery, and she liked mysteries. The sixth line was a problem, and she did not like that at all. Outside, it was already dark, although it was only four o'clock. She looked up at the tower out of habit. Ten past four, said the clock, as it always did now. "Not for long," said Margaret quietly. A customer turned round. "Sorry, love?" "Nothing," she said. "Just talking to myself."

# Three Blue Doors

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_3.page_1-1792915239299.mp3
On Saturday afternoon the post office closed at one o'clock, and Margaret went for a walk. It was not a normal walk. She took her shopping bag, a few small parcels to deliver and her list, and she walked slowly down every street in Ellerdale, looking at doors. It did not take long. The village only had six streets. When she arrived back at the church, she had found three blue doors.
The first one was on the High Street, next to the bakery. It belonged to Mrs Prudence Bell, who was eighty-nine years old and had lived in the same house since 1956. Margaret knew her well. Every Monday, Mrs Bell came into the post office to buy one stamp and to tell Margaret everything that was wrong with the world. She had no children and no grandchildren, but she had four cats. No dog. Margaret crossed out the first door on her list. Then, to be fair, she knocked anyway, because she had a parcel for Mrs Bell.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_3.page_2-1792915256365.mp3
"Margaret! On a Saturday!" said Mrs Bell. "Is something wrong?" "Nothing's wrong, Prudence. Your parcel came this morning, and I was passing." Mrs Bell took the parcel and looked at it with suspicion. "It's from my nephew in Australia. He sends me a calendar with kangaroos every year. I don't like kangaroos." Margaret asked, as casually as possible, if she remembered the church clock. "Of course I remember it! Nobody can sleep in this village now. It's too quiet. Before, I always knew what time it was without getting up." It was the first time Margaret had heard her say something nice about anything.
The second blue door was on Beck Road, a newer street of brick houses near the school. Here lived the Taylor family: a father who drove a bus to Kendal, a mother who worked at the bank, and twin girls of eight called Sophie and Sienna. Margaret's heart beat a little faster. Two girls of eight, and both their names began with S. She rang the bell. Inside, a dog started barking, and she smiled. But when the door opened, a huge grey dog with two very straight, very pointed ears put its nose against her coat.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_3.page_3-1792915272050.mp3
"Sorry! Duke, no! Sit!" said Mrs Taylor. Behind her, the twins were arguing loudly about the television. Margaret said she was collecting old Christmas decorations for the church. Mrs Taylor went to look in the attic, and Margaret asked the girls what they wanted for Christmas. "A pony," said Sophie. "Two ponies," said Sienna, "because she's not sharing hers." Neither of them mentioned a bell, a clock or a grandfather. Margaret thanked Mrs Taylor for a box of old paper angels and left. Two doors, two wrong answers.
The third blue door was on Mill Lane, the last street before the river. There were only four houses there, small and old, with long gardens that went down to the water. The blue door was on number three. The paint was old and a little dirty, as if nobody had looked after it for some time. In the front garden, a boy was building a snowman. He had made a large body and a small head, and now he was looking for stones for the eyes. Beside him, a small round dog with short legs was helping by eating the snow.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_3.page_4-1792915287310.mp3
The dog saw Margaret and ran to the gate, barking happily. Its left ear was up. Its right ear was down. Margaret stopped. For a moment she could not speak. "Pepper! Quiet!" said the boy. He was thin, with brown hair that needed cutting and a red scarf that was too long for him. "Sorry. She likes people. She's not dangerous, she just wants to say hello." "I know," said Margaret, before she could stop herself. Then she added quickly: "I mean, I can see that. What a lovely dog. What's your name?" "Sam," said the boy. "Sam Pike."
Pike. The name went through Margaret like cold water. At that moment, the blue door opened, and a young woman came out with a bag of rubbish. She looked tired. She had the same brown hair as the boy, tied back quickly, and she wore an old jumper with paint on it. "Can I help you?" she asked, a little unsure. Margaret held up the last parcel in her bag. It was not for this house at all; it was for the farm at Low Beck. But she said the first thing that came into her head: "I'm from the post office. I'm looking for... Mr Pike?"

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_3.page_5-1792915302956.mp3
The young woman's face changed. "Walter? Walter's my father-in-law. He doesn't live here, he lives up on Station Road. But he's not at home. He's been in Fellside since March. He fell on the stairs." She looked away, towards the hills. "My husband died three years ago, and Walter is... well, he's all the family we have here. Sam goes to see him every Sunday. He doesn't talk much now." She tried to smile. "I'm Laura. Do you want to leave the parcel with me?" "No, no," said Margaret gently. "I made a mistake. It's not for Walter. I'm sorry."
She walked back up Mill Lane very slowly. The snow was falling again, soft and silent. Walter Pike. Walter, who had made the red box with his own hands. Walter, who had answered the children's letters for thirty years. Walter, who had told her: children need to know that someone is listening. And now his grandson had written to the red box, and the letter had come to her. She stopped in the middle of the street, under the dark tower of St Oswald's. Ten past four, said the clock. "All right, Walter," said Margaret. "I'm listening."

# A Letter for the Postmistress

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_4.page_1-1792915320147.mp3
Fellside Cottage Hospital stood alone on the hill above Ellerdale, a long white building with green windows and a car park that was always too small. From the windows on the south side, you could see the whole village: the roofs, the river, the bridge, and in the middle of everything, the square grey tower of St Oswald's. Margaret drove up on Monday evening, after work. She had brought a box of shortbread and a newspaper. She did not really know what she wanted to say.
A young nurse with a Christmas hat took her to Walter's room. "You're his first visitor this week who isn't family," she said. "That's nice. But don't expect too much. He's very tired these days. His leg is getting better, slowly, but he's lost interest in things. He doesn't eat much. He sleeps a lot. Sometimes I think he's just waiting." She opened the door quietly. "Walter? You have a visitor."
Walter Pike was asleep in a chair by the window. Margaret almost did not recognise him. She remembered a big man with a loud laugh, red cheeks and a postman's bag on his shoulder, who knew the name of every dog in the valley. The man in the chair was small and thin. His white hair was too long, and his hands, on the blanket, looked like paper. A walking frame stood next to him. His face was turned towards the window, towards the village and the silent tower.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_4.page_2-1792915334576.mp3
Margaret sat down on the other chair and waited. Ten minutes passed. Twenty. Walter did not wake up. She looked at the room: a bed, a small television, a card from Sam with a drawing of a dog on it. One ear up, one ear down. On the table there was a photograph in a frame. It showed a younger Walter at the top of a ladder, inside the church tower, with a big iron key in his hand and an enormous smile. Someone had written on it: Dad and his clock, 1994.
After half an hour, the nurse came back and said that visiting time was over. Margaret stood up. She put the shortbread and the newspaper on the table and, very gently, touched the old man's hand. "Hello, Walter," she said softly. "It's Margaret, from the post office. I've still got your red box. I still answer them, all of them." Walter did not open his eyes. But Margaret thought, although she was not sure, that his fingers moved a little under hers.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_4.page_3-1792915349864.mp3
In the car park, she stood beside her car for a minute before she got in. The snow had stopped, and the village lay below her like a picture on a Christmas card: small yellow windows, white roofs, a thin line of smoke from every chimney. She could see the post office, the bridge, and the dark shape of a workshop by the river, where a light was still on. And in the middle of it all, the tower. From up here, she understood why Walter sat by the window all day. He was not only looking at the village. He was listening to it, and it was saying nothing.
The next morning, Margaret opened the post office at half past eight and, as she did every day in December, she opened the red box. There were four new letters inside. Three were from children. The fourth was a white envelope, old-fashioned and thick, the kind you can't buy anymore. On the front, in black ink, in beautiful, slightly shaky handwriting, someone had written: To the Postmistress, Ellerdale. There was no stamp. Someone had put it in the box by hand, during the night or very early in the morning.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_4.page_4-1792915362906.mp3
Margaret's hands were not completely steady when she opened it. Inside, there was a single card with a small robin printed in one corner. The message was short. "Dear Margaret. The key sleeps where the swallows sleep. But a key is not enough. The clock needs a man who mends things and never smiles. Ask him. He will say no. Ask him again. With thanks, F.C." She read it three times. Then she turned the card over. There was nothing on the back.
F.C. She knew what F.C. meant, of course. Every child in England knew. Father Christmas. Margaret laughed, a short, nervous laugh, and looked around the empty shop as if someone was watching her. Somebody was playing a joke. But who? Nobody knew about Sam's letter except her. She had not told anyone, not even Ruth. And Walter... Walter had been asleep. Walter could not walk without a frame, and Fellside was two miles up the hill, in the snow. It was impossible.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_4.page_5-1792915378312.mp3
She decided not to think about who. It was more useful to think about what. Where do swallows sleep? In summer, swallows came to Ellerdale from Africa and built their nests under roofs, in barns, in old buildings. In winter they were gone, but their nests stayed. And a man who mends things and never smiles? That was easier. Everybody in the village knew exactly who that was. Margaret sighed. "Oh no," she said. "Not Harry Dent."
Harry Dent was seventy-four. He had been an engineer for forty years, first on ships, then in a factory in Barrow. Now he lived alone in a big house by the bridge, with a workshop in the garden where the lights stayed on until midnight. He repaired everything: radios, lawnmowers, bicycles, washing machines. He did it well, and he did it cheaply. But he never said good morning, he never came to the pub, and nobody in Ellerdale had seen him smile since his wife died. Margaret put the card in her pocket. "He will say no," she repeated. "Ask him again."

# Where the Swallows Sleep

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_5.page_1-1792917193443.mp3
Harry Dent's workshop smelled of oil, wood and cold coffee. It was full of things in pieces: a radio with no back, three bicycle wheels, a clock from a kitchen, an old sewing machine. Harry himself was sitting at a long table under a bright lamp, with a small screwdriver in his hand and glasses on the end of his nose. He did not look up when Margaret knocked on the open door. "Shop's closed," he said. "It's not a shop," said Margaret. "It's a shed." "Then the shed's closed."
Margaret came in anyway and closed the door behind her, because it was very cold. She explained, simply, that she wanted to repair the church clock before Christmas, and that she needed someone who understood machines. She did not mention Sam, the letter or the strange card from F.C. Harry listened without stopping his work. When she finished, he said one word. "No." "Why not?" "Because it's a hundred and fifty years old, because the Carlisle people want three thousand pounds, and because it's not my problem. Goodnight, Mrs Penrose."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_5.page_2-1792917209213.mp3
She went home. She had expected this. "He will say no," the card had said. The next evening, she came back. This time she brought a flask of hot tea and two mince pies from the bakery, and she sat down on a wooden box without asking. Harry looked at her over his glasses with an expression of deep suffering. "You again." "Me again," said Margaret. "I'm going to tell you the real reason. But you have to promise to keep it a secret." Harry said nothing. She decided to take that as a yes.
She told him about the red box, and about the blue letter, and about the boy with the long red scarf and the dog with one ear up. She told him about Walter in his chair at Fellside, looking at the silent tower. When she finished, Harry put down his screwdriver. For a long time he did not speak. Then he said, in a different voice: "When my Jean was ill, Walter brought the post every morning. Every morning for two years. He always stayed for a cup of tea. He talked to her about his clock. She loved that."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_5.page_3-1792917222601.mp3
He took a mince pie and ate it slowly. "Where's the key?" he asked. Margaret showed him the card from F.C. Harry read it, and something strange happened to his face. It was not exactly a smile. But it was close. "Where the swallows sleep," he repeated. "Every summer, Walter complained about the swallows in the church porch. They made a mess on the floor and he had to clean it. But he never let anyone take the nests down. He said they were tenants." Harry stood up and took his coat. "Get your torch, Mrs Penrose. I'll get the ladder."
It was nine o'clock and the village was asleep when they arrived at St Oswald's with an aluminium ladder and two torches. The porch was a small stone room in front of the church door, with a wooden roof. Margaret held the ladder while Harry climbed up, slowly, complaining about his knees. Under the roof, in the corners between the old beams, there were five swallows' nests, made of mud and grass, dry and empty now. "Hold the light higher," said Harry. "Higher. Thank you."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_5.page_4-1792917237983.mp3
He looked behind the first nest, then the second, then the third. Nothing. Behind the fourth, his hand stopped. "Well, well," he said quietly. He pulled out a flat tin box, the kind that used to hold biscuits, with a picture of a Scottish castle on the lid. He came down the ladder and opened it under Margaret's torch. Inside, wrapped in an oily cloth, there was a big iron key, as long as Margaret's hand. And under the key, a small notebook full of dates and numbers in Walter's handwriting.
Ruth had given Margaret a key to the tower, and so they climbed it, there and then. The stairs were narrow, steep and very dark. At the top, in a small wooden room under the bell, there was the clock: not the white face that the village saw, but its heart. It was a big iron machine, as large as a kitchen table, full of wheels, chains and weights. Harry stood in front of it for a long time without speaking. Margaret had never seen anyone look at a machine like that. He looked at it the way people look at a sleeping child.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_5.page_5-1792917253374.mp3
Then he began to work. He touched the wheels, tried the key, looked at everything with his torch. After half an hour, he sat down on the floor. "The clock itself is fine," he said. "Walter looked after it well. But there are two problems. The spring on the hammer is broken; that's easy, I can make a new one. And this wheel here, the one that tells the hammer when to strike, has three broken teeth. It's brass. I can make a new wheel, but I need brass, and time. Ten days, maybe more." He counted the teeth on the old wheel with his finger. "Forty-six teeth," he said, and wrote it down.
"Christmas Eve is in fourteen days," said Margaret. Harry stood up and looked at her. In the light of the torch, his face looked younger. "Then we'd better start tomorrow," he said. They went down the dark stairs together, carefully, one step at a time. Outside, it had stopped snowing and the sky was full of stars. At the church gate, Harry stopped. "Mrs Penrose," he said. "That card. F.C. Who do you think wrote it?" Margaret looked up at the hill, where one window was still lit at Fellside. "I really don't know," she said. And it was true.

# The Village Keeps a Secret

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_6.page_1-1792917267985.mp3
A secret in a small village is like snow in April: everybody knows it will not last long. Margaret knew this better than anyone, because the post office was where all the village news arrived first. So she was very careful. She told only three people the truth: Ruth, because it was her church; Harry, who already knew; and Jim Askew, who owned the pub, because he knew everybody and could keep his mouth shut. To everyone else, she said only this: "We're trying to repair the church clock. It's a surprise. Please don't talk about it near the Pike family."
Ruth was delighted. "A Christmas miracle in my first year! The bishop will think I'm a genius." She gave Margaret all the keys of the tower and promised to say nothing, even in her sermons. Harry, meanwhile, had a different problem. He needed brass to make the new wheel, a lot of it, and good quality. "Old brass is the best," he said. "Door handles, candlesticks, old taps. Anything. But I need about three kilos, and I need it this week."

So Margaret put a cardboard box behind the counter of the post office, under a small handwritten sign: FOR THE CLOCK. She did not explain. She did not need to. By Wednesday, the box was half full. The baker brought the old brass weights from his grandfather's scales. Jim from the pub brought a door handle and a bell that nobody had rung for "last orders" since 1990. Mrs Taylor brought a candlestick, and the twins brought a brass button each, very seriously, as if it was the most important gift in the world.
On Thursday, Mrs Prudence Bell came in. It was not Monday, and she did not buy a stamp. She put two heavy brass candlesticks on the counter. "They were my mother's," she said. "I've polished them every Sunday for sixty years. I'm tired of it." Then she looked up at Margaret, and for the first time in twenty-two years, Mrs Bell smiled. "I want to know what time it is again, without getting up," she said. "That's all." And she left before Margaret could thank her.

On Friday, Harry came to collect the box. It was the first time anyone could remember him inside the post office. The queue went silent. He looked at the brass, picked up one of Mrs Bell's candlesticks and turned it slowly in the light. "Good brass," he said. "Very good." Then he lifted the heavy box, walked to the door, stopped, and turned round. "Good morning," he said to the queue, a little awkwardly, as if the words were rusty. And he left. For a few seconds, nobody spoke. Then old Mr Hughes said, "Well, I never," and everybody started talking at once.
That same afternoon, the shop bell rang and Margaret's heart almost stopped. Laura Pike came in, with Sam behind her and Pepper on a lead. The box FOR THE CLOCK was right there on the shelf, behind Margaret's head. Margaret moved very naturally, she hoped, and stood in front of it. Laura needed stamps for her Christmas cards. Sam, meanwhile, walked directly to the red box by the door, looked around to check that his mother was not watching, and pushed a small blue envelope inside.

"Is that the box for Father Christmas?" asked Laura, smiling at her son. "Sam, did you write another one?" "No," said Sam, very quickly. Margaret heard it, and she knew it was not true, and she liked him even more. When they left, she opened the box. The second letter was very short. "Dear Father Christmas. Did you get my first letter? Pepper wants to know. Love from S." Under it, there was a new drawing of the dog. This time both of its ears were down. It looked worried.

She had only just signed it when she realised what she had written. F.C. The same two letters as on the strange card. She looked at them for a long time. Then she shook her head. This time there was no message, only a drawing. It was a careful drawing of a brass wheel, very exact, like a drawing in an engineer's book. Next to it was a number: 48. And under the number, two words: Count again. Margaret ran to Harry's workshop without her coat. Harry was working on the new wheel, which was almost finished and shone like gold under his lamp. He looked at the drawing. He took the old broken wheel, a magnifying glass, and counted again, very slowly, with a pencil.
When he finished, he put the pencil down and took off his glasses. "Forty-eight," he said quietly. "Two of the teeth are so worn that they're almost flat. I missed them. With forty-six, the clock would strike the wrong number of times. Seven at six o'clock. Twelve at ten." He looked at the drawing again. "Mrs Penrose, nobody could know this. Nobody, unless they had looked at that wheel every week for forty years." Margaret said nothing. Outside, the snow was falling on the road to Fellside, where an old man could not walk without a frame.

# Christmas Eve

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_7.page_1-1792917349122.mp3
Harry had needed five more days to make a new wheel with forty-eight teeth, and he had worked every night until two in the morning. On the afternoon of Christmas Eve, it was finally ready. He wrapped it in a soft cloth like a baby and put it in a wooden box. The new spring for the hammer was already in place; he had put it in the day before. "Now we only need to put the wheel in, wind the clock, set the time and pray," he said. "Easy." Margaret had never heard him make a joke before. She was not sure it was a joke.
The weather did not help. Since the morning, the sky had been heavy and yellow, and at three o'clock a storm came over the hills from the west. The wind was so strong that the church door banged like a gun, and the snow came sideways, not down. At four o'clock, all the lights in Ellerdale went out. The whole village was dark. "Power cut," said Ruth, who had come to help, holding a candle. "The lines are down near Low Beck. Thank God the clock doesn't need electricity."

They climbed the tower at half past four: Harry first, with the wooden box; Margaret behind him, with two torches, the iron key and a big flask of hot tea; Ruth last, with her candle, which went out on the third step. In the clock room, it was terribly cold. The wind screamed through the little windows, and above their heads the great bell moved a little and made a low, deep sound, like someone humming in their sleep.
Harry knelt in front of the machine and began. Margaret held the torch exactly where he told her. He took out the old wheel, cleaned the place where it had been, and put the new one in. It shone in the light. Then he needed to fix it with a small bolt. His fingers were stiff with cold. The bolt slipped out of them, hit the floor, rolled, and disappeared through a gap between two old floorboards. Nobody spoke. Down below, somewhere in the dark, they heard a tiny metallic sound.

"Do you have another one?" asked Margaret. "No," said Harry. "It's a special size. I made it." He closed his eyes. It was twenty past five. Margaret did not wait. She took a torch and went down the stairs as fast as she could, to the small room under the clock room, where the bell ropes once hung. The floor there was covered in a hundred years of dust, dead leaves and feathers. She got down on her knees and began to search with her hands, centimetre by centimetre.
At first she found nothing. A coin from 1971. A button. A swallow's feather. Then Ruth came down with the second torch, and together they searched in silence while the storm screamed outside. At twenty to six, Ruth's hand stopped. "Is this it?" In her palm there was a tiny piece of shining metal, no bigger than a fingernail. Margaret almost cried. She ran up the stairs with it, and Harry took it without a word and went back to work.

At ten to six, the wheel was in place. Harry put the iron key into the clock and tried to turn it. It was very hard; the machine had not moved for nine months. "Help me," he said, and Margaret put her hands next to his on the key. Together they turned it, once, twice, ten times, twenty times. Slowly, with a long groan, the heavy weights began to rise. Then Harry moved the hands of the clock, from ten past four to five minutes to six. Something inside the machine began to tick. Slowly, steadily, like a heart.
The three of them stood and watched. Tick. Tick. Tick. Nobody breathed. At exactly six o'clock, the new brass wheel turned, there was a loud click, and the hammer began to lift towards the bell... and stopped. It did not move. It stayed there, halfway, shaking a little. "Frozen," said Harry, and his voice broke. "The pivot is frozen. The cold. I didn't think of the cold." He touched it with his fingers, but there was nothing to do. In a few seconds, the moment would be gone.

Margaret did not think. She opened her flask and poured hot tea over the pivot of the hammer. A cloud of steam rose in the light of the torch. "Tea!" said Harry. "On my clock!" One by one, people came out into the street with candles and torches and looked up at the tower. Someone started clapping. # The Last Letter

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b1-letters-to-the-north-pole/chapter_8.page_1-1792917421120.mp3
On Christmas morning, Margaret woke up at seven o'clock because the church clock told her to. Seven strokes, clear and slow, came across the snowy roofs and through her bedroom window. She lay in bed and counted them, smiling like a child. Biscuit, at the end of the bed, opened one eye, looked at her as if she was completely mad, and went back to sleep. At eight o'clock, the clock rang again. At nine, again. All over Ellerdale, people were hearing the time again, and many of them, Margaret was sure, were counting too.
At eleven, she drove up to Fellside. The roads had been cleared during the night, and the power was back in the village. The young nurse with the Christmas hat met her at the door, and this time she was smiling. "You won't believe it," she said. "Last night, at six o'clock, he was asleep in his chair. When the bell started, he woke up and stood up. Without his frame. He stood at the window and counted all six. Then he asked for his dinner. He ate everything. And then he asked for more."

On Boxing Day, Margaret went to the post office early, although it was closed, to take the red box in for another year. It was not empty. At the bottom, there was one last envelope. It was not blue, and it was not old-fashioned and thick. It was made of a paper she had never seen before, soft and slightly silver, and it smelled of snow and pine trees. There was no stamp. The handwriting was not Walter's, or Sam's, or anyone's that she knew. It said only: Margaret.
Inside, there was a small card and a tiny silver bell, no bigger than a thimble. The card said: "Dear Margaret. Thank you for listening. Somebody always has to. With love, F.C." Margaret sat down on the step of the post office, in the cold, and read it again and again. Then she rang the little bell. It made a sound so clear and so pure that a robin on the church wall turned its head. At that moment, the clock in the tower began to strike. Margaret counted. Ten. Exactly ten. She put the bell in her pocket, close to her heart, and walked home through the snow, smiling.
