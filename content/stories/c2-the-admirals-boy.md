# The Genoese at Palos

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_1.page_1-1793550460139.mp3
I am an old man now, and the sea that once seemed to me the very edge of creation has shrunk, in the telling, to a tavern anecdote. Young men in Seville speak of the Indies as casually as they speak of Cádiz; ships come and go laden with sugar and hides, and nobody remembers that there was a time when the western ocean was a wall. I remember. I was fifteen years old, a grumete — a ship's boy — from Moguer, when I signed on with the Genoese, and I am, so far as I know, among the last of those who sailed with him who still draw breath.
You have asked me, grandson, to set down what he was truly like, for you have read the chroniclers and found them either too reverent or too spiteful. I shall try to be neither. I owe him a great deal, and I bear him a grudge or two; both debts will be honoured here. What I will not do is embellish. Where I did not witness a thing with my own eyes, I shall tell you so, and from whom I had it. An old sailor's yarn is worth nothing if it cannot bear the weight of the truth.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_1.page_2-1793550478291.mp3
It began, as so many of the Crown's favours do, with a punishment. Palos had offended Their Majesties in some matter of dues or disobedience — the particulars were never explained to boys like me — and by way of penance the town was ordered to furnish two caravels, fully fitted out, for twelve months' service. On the twenty-third of May, in the year of Our Lord 1492, the royal letter was read aloud in the church of San Jorge, and I stood at the back beside my father, who muttered that this was the costliest sermon he had ever sat through.
Worse than the expense was the man to whom the ships were to be lent. He was a foreigner, a Genoese who had spent years hanging about the court and the friary of La Rábida, hawking a scheme that the learned men appointed by Their Majesties had found wanting. Let me be clear on one point, since nonsense is now commonly repeated: nobody in Palos believed the world was flat. Every pilot knew it to be round. The quarrel was over its size. The scholars held that Asia lay impossibly far to the west, beyond the reach of any ship's water casks — and, as it turned out, the scholars were right.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_1.page_3-1793550495127.mp3
I first laid eyes on him on the quay a few days later. He was tall, taller than most of our men, with a long face, a high-bridged nose and pale eyes that seemed forever to be measuring something at a distance. His complexion was ruddy, as though weathered by a lifetime of salt wind, and his hair, which they said had been fair in his youth, had turned entirely white, although he could not have been much past forty. He dressed soberly, almost like a friar, and indeed he kept the rule of a pious man more strictly than our parish priest: he fasted on the appointed days, recited the canonical hours, and the strongest oath I ever heard pass his lips was "by San Fernando".
His speech was a curiosity in itself. His Castilian was fluent but bent out of shape, flavoured with Portuguese turns picked up during his years in Lisbon, and when he was angry something older crept in underneath. He did not court affection. He addressed sailors as a craftsman addresses tools he intends to use well: courteously, but without the slightest pretence that we were his equals.

For weeks, the recruitment languished. Nobody in Palos, Moguer or Huelva was eager to entrust his life to a stranger, still less to a stranger proposing to sail where no one had ever sailed. A handful of men took up the Crown's offer of a pardon for their crimes in exchange for service — that much is true — but the tale that the fleet was manned by gaolbirds is a slander. What turned the tide was Martín Alonso Pinzón. Pinzón was one of ours: a wealthy shipowner, a seasoned captain, a man whose word carried weight on every quay of the Tinto. When he threw in his lot with the Genoese, bringing his brother Vicente Yáñez and a throng of kinsmen and neighbours, the doubters fell silent and the muster rolls filled.
It was around this time that I heard, from a clerk who had seen the papers, what the Genoese had wrung from Their Majesties at Santa Fe before consenting to sail. Should he succeed, he was to be Admiral of the Ocean Sea, Viceroy and Governor of every land he discovered, and to receive a tenth of all the riches they yielded — and these honours were to pass to his heirs in perpetuity. A supplicant at court would have taken less. He had not. He had preferred to wait years rather than settle for less.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_1.page_5-1793550531365.mp3
Three ships were made ready. The Pinta, under Martín Alonso, and the Niña, under Vicente Yáñez, were caravels from our own yards, light and handy. The third, the flagship, was a heavier, slower nao out of Galicia, chartered from her master, Juan de la Cosa; we called her La Gallega among ourselves, though the Admiral preferred the name Santa María. It was aboard her that I was placed, with some forty others.
On the morning of Friday, the third of August, every man in the fleet having confessed and received the sacrament, we crossed the bar of Saltés half an hour before sunrise and stood out to sea. I remember the Admiral on the poop deck, bareheaded, his lips moving in prayer.
I remember something else, which I did not understand until much later. In those very days the river mouths were crowded with other vessels, overladen and low in the water, carrying away the Jews whom Their Majesties had expelled from their kingdoms. We sailed out among them. The Admiral, I have since learned, wrote of their expulsion in the same breath as his own commission, as though the one had cleared the way for the other. To me, at fifteen, they were merely people weeping at the rails.

# Two Reckonings

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_2.page_1-1793550547564.mp3
We had been at sea barely three days when the Pinta's rudder jumped from its pintles and she began to wallow like a drunkard. The Admiral could not come alongside to help in such a sea, and I watched him pace the poop in a state of barely contained fury. He did not believe it was an accident. Gómez Rascón and Cristóbal Quintero, who owned the Pinta, had sailed with the greatest reluctance, and the Admiral was convinced, though he said so only to his officers, that they had tampered with the rudder in the hope of being sent home. He consoled himself, he said, with the knowledge that Martín Alonso was a man of spirit and ingenuity who would make shift with ropes until we reached the Canaries.
This was my first lesson in the Admiral's character. He trusted no one entirely, and smelled sabotage where an ordinary captain would have blamed rotten timber. Sometimes he was right. More often, I came to think, he simply could not conceive that the world might be indifferent to his enterprise: everything that befell us was either Providence clearing his path or malice obstructing it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_2.page_2-1793550565437.mp3
We lingered nearly a month among the Canary Islands, longer than anyone wished. The Pinta's rudder was mended at Gran Canaria, and while we were about it the Niña's triangular lateen sails were exchanged for square ones, so that she might run more easily before the wind. Passing Tenerife, we saw fire belching from the great mountain, and several of the younger men crossed themselves and pronounced it an omen. The Admiral took the trouble to explain to them, with a patience he seldom showed, that such fires were natural, and that Etna in Sicily did the very same.
At San Sebastián de la Gomera we took on water, firewood and salted meat. There was talk, too, of Portuguese caravels lurking off the islands with orders to seize us, for King João had never forgiven the Genoese for carrying his scheme to Castile after Lisbon had rejected it. Whether those caravels existed I cannot tell you; I never saw them. But the Admiral believed in them, and it hastened our departure. He was a man who always seemed to have enemies just over the horizon, and I confess that this, oddly, made us feel safer in his hands, as if a man so alert to danger could not be taken unawares.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_2.page_3-1793550589515.mp3
We weighed anchor on the sixth of September. For three days the wind failed us and we lay becalmed within sight of the islands, which was a cruelty of its own; then, on Sunday the ninth, the breeze freshened, and by evening the last peak of El Hierro had sunk beneath the horizon. I had expected to feel exultation. Instead, I felt the whole ship fall silent. Men who had laughed at the Tenerife fire now stood at the rail staring eastward, and more than one wept openly, as though at a graveside. They had sailed to Guinea, to the Azores, to Flanders, but always along known routes, with the comfort of a coast somewhere off the beam. Now there was nothing ahead but the Admiral's conviction.
He came among us that evening, and I will grant him this: he did not mock their fear. He spoke of the wealth of the lands we would find, of honours and rewards, and of the favour God shows those who venture in His service. He spoke well. Whether he persuaded anyone I doubt, but at least the weeping ceased.
It was on that same day, I learned long afterwards, that the Admiral began to keep two reckonings. Each day he would estimate the leagues we had run, and each day he would announce to the crew a smaller figure, so that, as he wrote, they would not take fright should the voyage prove long. The true figure he kept to himself.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_2.page_4-1793550607281.mp3
I have heard men call this a lie, and I have heard others call it wisdom. At the time I could call it neither, for I knew nothing of it. Here is the irony, which I savour in my old age: the Admiral habitually overestimated our speed, so that the false figures he gave us were in truth closer to the mark than those he guarded so jealously. He deceived us, and in deceiving us told us the truth by accident.
But consider what it reveals of him. He did not think the crew fit to bear the facts. He reasoned that men are governed by fear and appetite, and he governed us accordingly, with one hand dangling rewards and the other drawing a veil over whatever might alarm us. It was not cruelty. It was a kind of contempt, worn so naturally that he would never have recognised it as such.
A week out, something befell us that frightened the pilots far more than any distance. On the evening of the thirteenth of September, and again on the nights that followed, they observed that the compass needles no longer pointed true to the Pole Star, but swung a full point or more towards the northwest. To a seaman, the compass is the one certainty in a shifting world; if the needle itself goes astray, nothing is left to trust. I watched Peralonso Niño, our pilot, rap the compass box with his knuckles as if he could knock some sense into it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_2.page_5-1793550615702.mp3
The Admiral heard their alarm with perfect composure and then explained, in the tone of a schoolmaster correcting a dull pupil, that it was not the needle that moved but the star, which circled the true pole like any other, and that the needle was therefore quite sound. The pilots were satisfied, or feigned to be. I have since met learned men who say his explanation was mistaken, and that it is in the needle's nature to vary across the ocean. Perhaps so. But I never saw a man devise a reason so swiftly, nor deliver it with such authority that frightened men believed it.

# The Sea of Weeds

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_3.page_1-1793550633963.mp3
From the middle of September we began to sail through vast rafts of yellowish-green weed, so dense in places that the sea resembled a meadow in late summer. Some of the men were terrified that we would run aground on hidden shoals, or become entangled and rot there like flies in honey; others, more sanguine, insisted that such weed must have been torn loose from rocks not far off. The Admiral inclined to the second view, naturally. In one clump we found a small live crab, which he kept, declaring it a sure token of land. Whales were sighted, and boobies, and terns, and every bird that crossed our wake was hailed as a messenger from some nearby coast.
I mention all this because it shows how he read the world. He was a superb navigator by dead reckoning; I have never known a man with a surer feel for the speed and drift of a ship. But when it came to signs, he read them as a lover reads letters, finding in each precisely what he longed to find. He was persuaded, from his books, that the islands of the Indies lay scattered all about us, and whatever we saw became evidence for what he already believed.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_3.page_2-1793550648571.mp3
On the evening of the twenty-fifth of September, Martín Alonso climbed onto the poop of the Pinta, cried out that he saw land, and claimed the reward. On our deck, the Admiral fell to his knees and gave thanks to God, while Martín Alonso and his people sang the Gloria in excelsis Deo, and the men of all three ships swarmed up the rigging to look. And there indeed it seemed to lie, to the southwest, a dark line low on the horizon some twenty-five leagues off. Nobody slept much that night.
In the morning there was nothing. It had been cloud, as it so often is, for the sea at sunset is a great forger of coastlines. I shall never forget the silence aboard as the truth dawned upon us; it was worse than the silence when the Canaries vanished, for now hope itself had played us false. The Admiral, for his part, simply resumed his westward course. He did not reproach Martín Alonso, but neither did he trouble to console anyone. I think disappointment, in others, bored him.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_3.page_3-1793550664101.mp3
Let me describe how he lived aboard, for that too is part of him. He slept remarkably little. Whenever I stood the middle watch, I would find him on the poop at some hour of the night, wrapped in his cloak, observing the stars or working at his charts by the light of a lantern. He took his own readings and trusted those of the pilots only so far. He wrote in his journal every day without fail, in a small, careful hand, intended, so his steward told me, for the eyes of Their Majesties.
Every evening at dusk, when the glass was turned, the whole crew gathered to recite the Pater Noster, the Ave Maria and the Credo, and to sing the Salve Regina — badly, in our case, for sailors are no choirboys. The Admiral sang with us and seemed genuinely moved. I have known many men who wore religion as a cloak; with him it was the very skin. He believed with all his soul that God had chosen him to carry the name of Christ across the ocean. Christopher, he was fond of observing, meant "Christ-bearer", and he took that literally, as he took everything that concerned himself.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_3.page_4-1793550682116.mp3
By the beginning of October, the mood aboard had soured. We had been longer out of sight of land than any of us had ever been, and the Admiral's closeted conversations with the officers fed every kind of rumour. In the forecastle, men muttered that the Genoese was a madman who would drown us all to make himself a lord; that the winds here blew forever from the east, so that we should never beat home against them; that the water casks were turning foul. I heard one of the older hands — I will not name him, for his grandsons still live in Palos — remark that a man observing the stars at night might very easily lose his footing and go over the side, and that no one in Castile would ever ask questions.
Nothing came of it, but that it was said at all tells you how far things had gone. The Admiral cannot have been ignorant of it. Characteristically, he responded not by softening but by marshalling arguments: when a contrary wind blew from the west, he declared it providential, for it proved that winds here blew both ways and we could return. He was always ready with an argument. It never seemed to occur to him that frightened men are not swayed by reasoning.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_3.page_5-1793550697983.mp3
On the seventh of October, at Martín Alonso's urging, the Admiral altered course from due west to west-southwest, following great flocks of birds that were flying that way. He did so grudgingly, I believe, for it was not his own idea, and he liked to be the author of every decision.
Three days later, on the tenth, the men's patience snapped. A group of them went aft and told him plainly that they could bear no more and wished to turn back. I was close enough to hear his reply. He did not shout. He reminded them of the profit they stood to earn, and then said, with a coldness I can still feel, that it was useless to complain, for he had come to find the Indies and meant to go on until, with the help of Our Lord, he found them.
Some have since written that he promised to turn back if land were not sighted within three days. I did not hear him say so, and I doubt he did, for it was not in his nature to bargain. But the matter was settled the following night, and whether by Providence or by sheer good fortune, his obstinacy was rewarded before it could be tested further.

# A Light at Ten O'Clock

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_4.page_1-1793550712133.mp3
The eleventh of October was a day of signs that even the most despairing among us could not dismiss. The sea ran heavier than at any time on the voyage. The men of the Pinta fished out a cane and a little stick that appeared to have been worked with an iron tool; those of the Niña found a twig laden with small berries, like a branch of dog-rose. Faces that had been sullen for a week grew animated. At sunset we sang the Salve Regina, and afterwards the Admiral addressed us from the poop. He reminded us of God's mercy in bringing us thus far, ordered a vigilant watch to be kept from the forecastle, and repeated that whoever first sighted land would receive a silk doublet from his own hand, over and above the annuity of ten thousand maravedís that Their Majesties had promised.
That night I did not sleep at all. I lay on the deck with my eyes fixed on the dark seam of the horizon, and I prayed — God forgive me — less for the glory of Christendom than for the doublet.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_4.page_2-1793550726613.mp3
At about ten o'clock that night the Admiral, standing on the sterncastle, saw — or believed he saw — a light far ahead, like a little wax candle rising and falling. He was not certain enough to proclaim it land. Instead he quietly summoned Pero Gutiérrez, a gentleman of the King's household who was sailing with us, and asked whether he saw it too. Gutiérrez peered a long while and said that he did. Then the Admiral called Rodrigo Sánchez of Segovia, the royal inspector sent to keep account of Their Majesties' interests, and Sánchez looked and saw nothing, because, as the Admiral explained, he was not standing where it could be seen. The light was glimpsed once or twice more, and then not at all.
I record this exactly as it happened, and I leave you to judge. By any reckoning I have since been able to make, we were then some thirty-five miles from the nearest land, and I know of no fire on that island that could have been seen so far. But the Admiral never doubted that he had seen it, and that night's little candle was to cost a man from Triana dear.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_4.page_3-1793550744827.mp3
Two hours after midnight, when the moon had risen astern and was shining on the sea ahead, a sailor of the Pinta named Juan Rodríguez Bermejo, a man from Triana whom we knew as Rodrigo de Triana, saw a pale line of shore gleaming in the moonlight some two leagues off, and cried out: "Tierra! Tierra!" The Pinta fired a lombard, as had been agreed, and within moments every soul in the fleet was awake and shouting. This time it was no cloud. We shortened sail and stood off and on until daylight, for no sane captain runs at an unknown shore in the dark.
In the morning we came to an island, small and flat, greener than anything I had ever beheld, ringed by a reef on which the sea broke white. We found a passage and anchored in a bay on its western side. The Admiral went ashore in the armed boat bearing the royal standard, and with him went Martín Alonso and Vicente Yáñez, each carrying a banner with a green cross and the initials of the King and Queen, F and Y, crowned. I was among the oarsmen, and so it fell out that I set foot in that world with the first of them.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_4.page_4-1793550764546.mp3
The Admiral knelt, kissed the earth and wept, and the rest of us knelt with him. Then he rose, called upon the two captains, the notary Rodrigo de Escobedo and the inspector Sánchez to bear witness, and took possession of the island for the King and Queen, naming it San Salvador. Its people, we later learned, called it Guanahaní. Nobody thought to consult them.
The people themselves soon came down to the shore, warily at first, then in great numbers. They went as naked as their mothers bore them, and were well made, with handsome bodies and fine faces, their hair coarse and thick, almost like a horse's tail. Some painted themselves black or white or red. They had no iron. When the Admiral showed them our swords, they grasped them by the edge, out of ignorance, and cut themselves. We gave them red caps, glass beads and little hawk's bells, and they gave us balls of cotton thread, parrots and darts tipped with fish teeth, with a generosity that shamed us.
Within two days the Admiral had written in his journal — and I heard him say as much to his officers — that they would make good servants, that they would readily become Christians, and that with fifty men one could hold them all in subjection. He spoke those words in the same breath as his praise.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_4.page_5-1793550781195.mp3
From the first, his questions had turned on a single subject. Some of the islanders wore little pieces of gold hanging from a hole pierced in the nose, and the Admiral wished to know whence it came. By signs, they pointed south, and spoke, or so he understood, of a king there who possessed great vessels of gold. It was enough. From that moment the voyage became a hunt from island to island, the Admiral persuaded at every turn that Cipango, the land of gold described by Marco Polo, lay just over the horizon. When we left Guanahaní, he took seven of its people with him, to learn our tongue and serve as interpreters. Nobody asked whether they wished to come. Before many days had passed, one of them leapt overboard and paddled away for his life.
As for the reward: it was not Rodrigo de Triana who received the ten thousand maravedís. The Admiral claimed it for himself, on the strength of the candle he had seen at ten o'clock, and Their Majesties paid it to him for the rest of his days. Rodrigo got nothing. I have heard it said that he went off to Africa in his bitterness; I do not know whether that is true. I know only that the Admiral, who had been promised the tenth part of all the riches of the Indies, would not leave a common sailor his single prize.

# Christmas on the Reef

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_5.page_1-1793550799659.mp3
We wandered among the islands for weeks, and at the end of October reached a coast so vast that the Admiral pronounced it the mainland of Cathay, the empire of the Great Khan. It was Cuba, which we now know to be an island, but he never conceded the point. Years later, on another voyage, he had his men swear before a notary that it was the mainland, under pain of a heavy fine and of having their tongues cut out should they ever say otherwise. I was not present for that; I tell it as it was told to me, by men who had sworn.
On this first voyage he sent two men inland to seek out the Great Khan's court: Rodrigo de Jerez, of Ayamonte, and Luis de Torres, a converted Jew who knew Hebrew, Chaldean and some Arabic, for the Admiral reasoned that an Oriental prince would surely keep such learned men about him. They returned after several days, having found a village of palm-thatched houses, hospitable people and no Khan whatsoever. They also reported that the people carried smouldering rolls of dried leaves, which they put to their mouths to drink the smoke. It seemed to us a harmless oddity. It has since conquered half of Christendom.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_5.page_2-1793550818404.mp3
On the twenty-first of November, Martín Alonso took the Pinta and sailed off without leave. The Admiral believed he had gone hunting gold on his own account, to be first home to Castile and steal the glory, and he wrote of him afterwards with a rancour he never troubled to conceal. There was jealousy on both sides, I think. Martín Alonso held that without him there would have been no voyage at all, and he was not wholly wrong.
We passed on to a great island the Admiral named La Española, whose mountains and valleys put him in mind of Castile. And there, on Christmas Eve, the Santa María was lost. The sea was as calm as milk in a bowl. The Admiral, who had not slept for two days and a night, had gone below to rest; Juan de la Cosa, who had the watch, went to sleep as well, and the helmsman handed the tiller to a ship's boy, against the Admiral's express orders. Shortly before midnight, the current carried us gently onto a reef. I felt it through my feet before I heard it: a long, grinding shudder, and then a dreadful stillness. I was not the boy at the tiller. But I was a boy, and I have never forgotten how small we all suddenly seemed.

---

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_5.page_3-1793550837642.mp3
The Admiral was on deck in an instant. He ordered Juan de la Cosa to take the boat and lay out an anchor astern, so that we might warp her off. Instead, de la Cosa and his men rowed straight for the Niña. The Admiral never forgave him. By the time anything could be done, the seams had opened and the Santa María was beyond saving.
What followed I would scarcely have believed had I not seen it. Guacanagarí, the lord of that country, sent every canoe he possessed, and his people laboured all day beside us, carrying our goods ashore. Nothing was stolen, not so much as a lace-tag. The king himself wept for our loss. The Admiral was deeply moved, and wrote, I know, that there was no better people and no better land in all the world.
Then he made of the disaster a sign. God, he declared, had wrecked the ship so that a settlement should be founded there. From her timbers we built a tower and a palisade, and he named the place La Navidad. Thirty-nine men were chosen to remain under Diego de Arana, to gather gold against his return. Several were my friends. I was told I might stay. I chose the Niña.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c2-the-admirals-boy/chapter_5.page_4-1793550852618.mp3
In early January, as we were making ready to sail home, the Pinta rejoined us, with Martín Alonso full of excuses that the Admiral pretended to accept, for he could not afford open war between his captains. In February we ran into the worst storm I have known in fifty years of seafaring. The waves broke over us from two quarters at once, and the Niña, short of ballast after so many weeks, rolled until I thought she must capsize. We filled the empty casks with seawater to steady her.
In our terror we resolved on vows. The Admiral had as many chickpeas brought as there were men aboard, cut a cross into one, and shook them all in a cap. Whoever drew the marked pea would go on pilgrimage to Santa María de Guadalupe. The Admiral drew first, and drew the cross. A second lot was cast for Loreto, and a third for a vigil at Santa Clara of Moguer, my own town, and once again the Admiral's hand came out with the cross. Some of the men muttered that he had a talent for being chosen. He, I think, regarded it as further proof of the same thing.

That night, believing we might all perish, he wrote an account of his discoveries on parchment, wrapped it in waxed cloth, sealed it in a barrel and cast it into the sea, so that the news might reach Their Majesties even if he did not.
We came to the Azores, where half our men, going ashore barefoot and in their shirts to fulfil a vow at a chapel, were seized by the Portuguese; then another storm drove us into Lisbon, where the Admiral was received by King João himself, the very king who had once turned him away. I believe he relished that audience more than any other in his life. At last, on the fifteenth of March 1493, we crossed the bar of Saltés at midday and came home to Palos, seven months and twelve days after we had left it. The Pinta came in on the same tide. Martín Alonso, sick and out of favour, was dead within weeks.
The men of La Navidad were all dead when the Admiral returned that autumn. He made three more voyages, and was sent home from one of them in chains. He died at Valladolid in 1506, still insisting that he had reached the shores of Asia.

So what was he? Brave beyond reason, a navigator without equal, devout to the marrow, and proud enough to haggle with kings over a tenth of a world. He saw what he wished to see, and bent other men to his vision without a second thought. Without him I would have died a fisherman's son who had never crossed the bar. I have not decided, after all these years, whether to thank him.
