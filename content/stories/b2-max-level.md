# Game Over

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-max-level/chapter_1.page_1-1792909414362.mp3
At ten past six in the morning, the big doors of the warehouse opened and Max Turner walked out into the cold Leeds air. He had been on his feet for nine hours, pushing heavy cages of tins, bottles and frozen chips from one end of the building to the other. Behind him, the lorries were already starting their engines. In front of him, the sky over the motorway was turning from black to a dirty shade of grey. It was March, and it was raining, of course.
Max was twenty-four years old, and on most days he felt about sixty. He was tall, but he walked with his shoulders bent forward, as if he was always carrying something. His stomach pushed against his work fleece, his knees clicked when he climbed stairs, and he got out of breath faster than his mum, who smoked ten cigarettes a day. He had not done any real exercise since school. Unless you counted his thumbs, which were probably the strongest part of his body.

Because that was the other thing about Max Turner: he was a gamer. Not a professional one, not one of those people who earned money by playing in front of thousands of viewers online. Just a man who came home from work, closed his bedroom curtains and disappeared into other worlds for hours at a time. In those worlds he was fast, strong and brave. He jumped across rooftops, scored goals in front of screaming crowds and saved cities from monsters. In the real world, he struggled to catch a bus.
As if to prove the point, the number 49 was pulling away from the stop just as he turned the corner. Max lifted one hand and started to jog after it. After about twenty metres his chest was burning, and he stopped, bent over with his hands on his knees, and watched the bus disappear into the traffic. A teenager waiting at the stop looked at him with pity. Max pretended to tie his shoelace. Then he sat down on the wet bench and waited eighteen minutes for the next one.

On the top deck of the bus, he leaned his head against the window and watched the red-brick terraces of Harehills slide past. He thought, as he often did at this hour, about the person he had wanted to be. At nineteen he had applied to become a firefighter. He had passed the written tests easily. Then he had failed the fitness test in front of everyone, collapsing halfway through the running part while the instructor looked at his watch. He had never tried again.

The Turners lived in a narrow terraced house at the end of Ashley Road, with a tiny yard at the back and a front door that stuck in wet weather. When Max pushed it open with his shoulder, the smell of toast came down the hallway to meet him. His mum, Carol, was already dressed for her shift at the care home, standing in the kitchen with a mug of tea in one hand and her phone in the other. She looked tired too. Everybody in the house always looked tired.
"Morning, love," she said, without looking up. "There's toast. Don't eat all of it, your sister hasn't had breakfast." Max took two slices anyway and dropped into a chair. From upstairs came the sound of heavy footsteps, a door slamming and someone shouting about a missing school tie. A moment later his sister Lily appeared in the doorway, fourteen years old, hair half tied back, one shoe on and one in her hand.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-max-level/chapter_1.page_5-1792909459375.mp3
"You look terrible," Lily told him, stealing the toast from his plate. "Seriously. You look like a zombie from one of your stupid games." Max did not have the energy to argue. "Thanks," he said. "Love you too." Lily grinned, sat down to put her shoe on and then looked at him more carefully. "You missed the bus again, didn't you? You're all red." He threw a tea towel at her. She caught it easily, of course. Lily played netball for the school and ran everywhere.
When the house was quiet again, Max went up to his room and lay on the bed without taking off his clothes. Through the thin wall he could hear the neighbours' radio. His eyes went to the shelf above his desk, where his old firefighter application letter was still pinned, yellow at the edges. He should take it down, he thought. It was embarrassing. Five years had passed. He was not that boy any more, and the boy had not been good enough anyway.

He did not take it down. He never did. Instead he turned on his console, put his headphones on and played a racing game until his eyes closed by themselves. It was the same every day: work, sleep, games, work. He knew it was not really a life. But it was comfortable, and he was too tired to imagine anything else. He did not know that, in four days, everything was going to change because of fifteen pounds and a grey plastic box.

On Saturday he had a day off, and Lily dragged him into town to buy a birthday present for one of her friends. Kirkgate Market was full of noise and colour: fishmongers shouting prices, stalls selling phone cases, spices, sweets and cheap trainers, the smell of hot doughnuts everywhere. While Lily was choosing between two almost identical bracelets, Max wandered down a narrow side row where the stalls became smaller and older and the customers fewer.
At the very end of the row was a stall he had never noticed before. A hand-painted sign said OKORO REPAIRS – RADIOS, TVS, ODDITIES. The table was covered with old electronics: cassette players, a cracked typewriter, a pile of television remote controls tied together with an elastic band. Behind the table sat an elderly man in a woollen hat, reading a newspaper through a large magnifying glass. He looked up when Max stopped, and his eyes were sharp and curious.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-max-level/chapter_1.page_8-1792909494912.mp3
That was when Max saw the console. It sat on its own at the corner of the table, a heavy grey box about the size of a shoebox, with a crack running across the lid. There was no brand name on it, only a small metal plate with one word engraved on it: HALCYON. Next to it was a cardboard box full of cartridges, the old-fashioned kind you pushed into the top of the machine. None of them had a proper label. Someone had written the names by hand in black marker.
Max picked one up. ROOFTOP RUSH, it said. Then another: GOLDEN BOOT. Another: IRON GLOVE. He had played games for twenty years and he had never heard of any of them, or of a console called Halcyon. "Does it work?" he asked. The old man folded his newspaper slowly. "It works," he said. "Perhaps too well. It came from a house clearance in Otley, many years ago. Nobody has wanted it. Fifteen pounds, with all the games."

It was a ridiculous price for a heavy piece of junk, and Max knew it. But he already had his wallet out. As he handed over the money, the old man held on to the notes for a second longer than necessary. "Mr Okoro," he said, introducing himself. "One piece of advice, young man. Play carefully. This machine plays you back." Max laughed, because he thought it was a joke. Mr Okoro did not laugh. He just went back to his newspaper.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-max-level/chapter_1.page_10-1792909514669.mp3
That night, after his mum and Lily had gone to bed, Max carried the Halcyon up to his room and found an old cable in a drawer that fitted the back of his television. He blew the dust out of the cartridge slot, the way he used to do as a child, and pushed in ROOFTOP RUSH. For a moment nothing happened. Then the screen flickered, the console began to hum, low and warm, like a cat purring, and a title appeared in big orange letters over a city skyline at sunset.
The game was simple. You controlled a runner, a thin young woman in a red hoodie, who had to cross the city by jumping from roof to roof, climbing walls, sliding under pipes and swinging over gaps. The graphics were old and blocky, but the movement was incredible. Every jump felt heavy and real. When she landed, she rolled forward to absorb the impact. When she climbed, her hands searched for the right edge before her feet followed. Max had never seen a game move like that.

He played for an hour, then two, then he stopped looking at the clock. Something strange was happening to his hands. The controller felt warm, almost hot, and a soft tingling ran up his fingers and into his arms, as if he had been holding something electric. Sometimes, when the runner made a big jump, he felt his own legs twitch under the duvet. He told himself he was just tired. He had been awake for almost twenty hours.
By four in the morning he had reached the final level, a race across the roofs of a burning factory. The runner leapt, rolled, climbed a chimney and threw herself across a gap so wide that Max held his breath. She caught the opposite edge with one hand, swung, pulled herself up and sprinted towards the finish line as the factory collapsed behind her. The words LEVEL COMPLETE filled the screen. The console gave a long, satisfied hum and then went quiet.

Max turned off the television and lay back in the dark. His heart was beating fast, as if he had been running himself. His legs felt strange, not tired, but full, like a battery after charging. He thought about Mr Okoro's words. This machine plays you back. What did that even mean? He laughed quietly at himself, closed his eyes and fell asleep almost at once, dreaming of rooftops and orange skies.

He woke up at half past eleven with the sun on his face and a terrible feeling that he had forgotten something. Then he remembered: he had promised to meet Lily outside the leisure centre at eleven to carry her bags home after her netball tournament. He jumped out of bed, pulled on yesterday's jeans, grabbed his phone and ran down the stairs. Six missed calls. One message, in capital letters: WHERE ARE YOU.
The leisure centre was nearly a mile away. The quickest way was through the back alleys behind Ashley Road and across the old railway land, but there was a high brick wall in the way, and Max always walked around it. Today he did not think. He just ran out of the back gate and down the alley, and he was halfway there before he realised that he was not out of breath. His feet were landing lightly, one after the other, in a fast, steady rhythm he had never felt before.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/b2-max-level/chapter_1.page_14-1792909559008.mp3
Then the wall appeared in front of him, more than two metres high, and his body did something on its own. He did not slow down. He accelerated. His right foot hit the bricks halfway up, his hands reached for the top, found it, and pulled. In one smooth movement he swung his legs over, dropped down the other side and rolled forward across the gravel exactly as the runner in the red hoodie had done a hundred times on his screen. Then he was on his feet again and still running.
He stopped only when he reached the metal fence at the edge of the railway land. He turned around and stared at the wall behind him. It was impossible. Last summer he had needed a stepladder just to get a football back from the neighbours' garden. He looked down at his hands. There was red brick dust on his palms and a small cut on one finger. His heart was beating hard, but it was not the painful, desperate beating he knew. It was strong and even. It felt good.

Something warm moved in his legs, like the humming of the grey console. "Okay," he said quietly to nobody. "Let's see." And he ran at the fence.
