# The Phraser

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_1.page_1-1793519763209.mp3
The bridge over the Sull had a crack in it, and it was Ione Halvey's job to make sure that nobody in Lantry panicked about it.
She sat at her desk on the fourth floor of Peel & Ashgrove, a cup of cold tea at her elbow and the engineers' report open in front of her. The report was forty pages long, and it said, in the flat, careful language of engineers, that the Marlow Street Bridge had a fracture in its third support column, that the fracture had grown by eleven millimetres since the spring, and that the bridge should be closed to heavy traffic "within the coming months". The company that owned the bridge did not want to close it within the coming months. The company wanted to close it next summer, when the new road was finished and nobody would be inconvenienced. In the meantime, the company wanted a statement.
That was where Ione came in.
She picked up her pen, wrote a sentence, read it aloud under her breath, and felt her throat tighten slightly on the word "safe". She crossed it out. The body always knew. That was the first thing every child in the country learned, long before they learned to read: if you knew a thing was false, you could not say it. You could think it. You could imagine it in perfect detail. But the moment you tried to push it out through your mouth, or through the nib of a pen, something closed inside you, like a hand around your windpipe, and the words simply would not come. People called it the Stop. Nobody remembered a time without it, and nobody, as far as Ione knew, had ever found a way around it.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_1.page_2-1793519780606.mp3
Nobody needed to, of course. There were a hundred ways to avoid saying something true, and she knew most of them.
She tried again. "Marlow Street Bridge remains open to all traffic, and regular inspections continue." She whispered it. Her throat stayed relaxed. It was true: the bridge was open, and the inspections did continue, if one counted the single inspection in March as the beginning of something regular. She tapped her pen against her teeth. Too weak. People would ask what the inspections had found.
"No inspection has required the closure of the bridge," she wrote. True again. The engineers had recommended closure. They had not required it, because engineers did not have the power to require anything; only the city council did, and the council had not been told. She smiled faintly and added a second line. "Its owners are working closely with engineers on a long-term programme of improvements." That was true as well, if one interpreted "closely" generously and "long-term" literally.
She read the whole statement aloud, slowly, the way she had been trained to do in her first year, listening to her own body as if it were a second opinion. Nothing tightened. Nothing caught. The sentences slid out as smoothly as water. To anyone reading them in the morning paper, they would mean: the bridge is fine. And not one of them said so.

This was what it meant to be a phraser. In a world where nobody could lie, the people with money had quickly discovered that the truth was not a wall but a landscape, full of slopes and shadows and convenient hiding places, and that some people were much better than others at finding their way around it. Phrasers were the guides. They wrote press releases and political speeches, contracts and apologies, product labels and wedding toasts. They were paid, as Augustin Peel liked to say, not to bend the truth but to choose which part of it to stand in front of.
Ione had been doing it for seven years, and she was very good at it. She was also, if she was honest, which she was obliged to be, rather tired of it.
She sent the statement to Augustin's office with a short note — "Bridge. Holds up better than the bridge does." — and went out for lunch.
It was a bright October day, the kind that made the city look recently washed. The river glittered beneath the bridges, including, she noted with a small pang, the one with the crack in its third support column. Lantry was an old city, built on both banks of the Sull, and it had the slightly self-satisfied air of a place that had never been bombed, flooded or seriously embarrassed. Trams rattled along the quays. Students argued on the steps of the university. On the wall of the old customs house, a giant advertisement for a brand of toothpaste declared: "Recommended by more dentists than any other toothpaste we make." Ione admired it every time she passed. Somebody at a rival firm had written that. It was elegant work.

Advertising, in a world without lies, had become an art of precision. You could not say your soap was the best in the world unless you genuinely believed it, and most marketing directors, when pressed, did not. So the posters said things like "Nobody has ever proven a better soap exists" or "Loved by the people who love it". Politicians promised to "do everything that turns out to be possible". Restaurants advertised "food". Ione had once seen a café whose sign simply read: "Coffee. It is here." She had liked that café very much, though the coffee had been terrible.
She went instead to Pennick's, a narrow place on Weaver's Row with green tiles on the walls and a counter made from the hull of an old boat. Bettany was already there, sitting by the window with a bowl of soup and the exhausted expression of a nurse who had just finished a night shift.
"You look awful," Ione said, sitting down.
"Thank you," said Bettany. "I feel awful. That's consistency, at least." She pushed a basket of bread across the table. "How's the world of professional evasion?"
"Thriving. I've just saved a bridge."
"Did you fix it?"
"I described it."
Bettany laughed, though she shook her head at the same time. She had been Ione's closest friend since they were eleven years old, and she had never quite approved of Ione's job. Bettany was the most straightforward person Ione knew. In a society where everyone told the truth, she still managed to be more truthful than most, because she never hid behind the clever omissions that everyone else used. If Bettany thought your new haircut was a mistake, she said so. If she thought you were being a coward, she said that too. It was exhausting and, Ione had come to understand, enormously valuable.

"Someone has to do it," Ione said, the way she always did.
"Someone has to clean bedpans as well," Bettany replied, the way she always did. "But at least I don't pretend they're full of flowers."
"I don't pretend anything. Nobody can."
"Exactly," said Bettany. "That's what makes your job so strange. You're paid to do the closest thing to lying that the human body allows."
Ione did not answer. It was an old argument, and neither of them ever won it. Instead she ordered soup, and they talked about other things: Bettany's new supervisor, who could not finish a sentence without starting another; Ione's mother's old flat, which Ione still had not managed to sell, eight years after the funeral; a film they had both seen and disagreed about. Outside, the light poured down on Weaver's Row and turned the cobbles gold.
It was while they were waiting for the bill that the man came in.
Ione noticed him only because he stood in the doorway for a moment longer than necessary, as if checking the room. He was perhaps thirty-five, tall, with a thin, intelligent face and a grey coat that was slightly too heavy for the weather. His hair was dark and needed cutting. He looked, she thought later, like someone who had not slept in a proper bed for a while, but who was used to hiding it.
He went to the counter. Old Mrs Pennick, who had run the café for thirty years and who disliked everyone equally, asked him what he wanted.
"Just a coffee," he said. "To take away. I don't want to sit down with it — it's raining out there."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_1.page_6-1793519861812.mp3
Ione froze.
She did not understand, at first, why she had frozen. The words had passed through the air so casually that her mind had nearly let them go. Then she turned her head and looked out of the window. Weaver's Row was flooded with sunshine. The sky above the rooftops was a deep, clean blue, without a single cloud. Nobody on the street carried an umbrella. The cobbles were dry.
It was not raining. It had not rained all day. And the man had said that it was.
She looked at Bettany. Bettany was searching in her bag for her purse and had heard nothing. She looked at Mrs Pennick, who was pouring the coffee with her usual expression of mild contempt and showed no sign of surprise. Perhaps, Ione thought, she hadn't been listening. People rarely listened to the small talk of strangers.
But Ione had. Seven years of her life had been spent listening to sentences — weighing them, testing them, feeling for the place where a true statement turned into a false impression. She knew exactly what she had heard. It was not a joke, because jokes still had to be true in some sense: you could say "I'm a teapot" in a silly voice, and your throat allowed it, because everyone understood you were playing. It was not a mistake, because the man had walked in from the street two minutes earlier; he could not possibly believe it was raining. It was not a metaphor. It was a plain statement about the weather, said in a plain voice, to a stranger, and it was false.
He had lied.

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_1.page_7-1793519884282.mp3
She felt the world shift very slightly beneath her, the way a floor shifts when you have been on a boat all day. She stared at him. She could not help it.
He took his coffee, paid, and turned towards the door. And then, perhaps because he felt the weight of her stare, he glanced across the room, and their eyes met.
It lasted perhaps two seconds. But Ione saw something happen in his face. At first there was nothing but polite indifference, the look one stranger gives another. Then, as he registered her expression, the indifference vanished. Something sharper replaced it: recognition, not of her, but of what she had noticed. He knew. He understood that she had heard him, and that she had understood. For a moment he looked almost frightened.
Then he smiled — a small, careful smile — lifted his coffee cup to her in a sort of salute, and walked out into the sunshine.
"Ione?" said Bettany. "Are you all right? You look like you've seen a ghost."
Ione stood up so quickly that her chair scraped against the tiles. "Wait here," she said, and went to the door.
Weaver's Row was busy with lunchtime traffic. A delivery boy wheeled a bicycle past. Two women stood talking outside the bookshop. A tram bell rang at the end of the street. She looked left and right, searching for a grey coat and a head of untidy dark hair. For a moment she thought she saw him, turning the corner onto the quay. She hurried after him, but when she reached the corner there was only the river, flashing in the light, and a crowd of tourists photographing the bridges. He was gone.
She stood there for a long time, her heart beating hard, while the city carried on around her exactly as it always had.

When she went back into Pennick's, Bettany had paid the bill and was waiting by the door with both their coats. "Well?" she said. "Who was he? An old boyfriend? A client you misdescribed?"
"I don't know who he was," said Ione truthfully. Then she hesitated. She wanted to tell her friend what she had heard. But how did you say it? How did you tell someone, in a world built entirely on the impossibility of lying, that you had just watched a man do it, as easily as he drank his coffee? Bettany would think she had misunderstood. Anyone would. A month ago, Ione would have thought so herself.
"He said something strange," she said at last.
"Strange how?"
Ione looked out at the blue sky. "He said it was raining."
Bettany looked at the window, then back at her friend, and frowned. "Well, that's impossible," she said. "Unless he meant somewhere else."
"Yes," said Ione slowly. "Unless he meant somewhere else."

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_1.page_9-1793519914855.mp3
They walked back along the quay together, and Bettany talked about sleep and soup and the unfairness of hospital rotas, and Ione answered when she was supposed to, and all the time her mind kept returning to the same small, impossible sentence. It's raining out there. She turned it over and over, the way she would turn over a client's statement, looking for the trick. Perhaps he had meant that it was raining somewhere in the world, as it surely was. But nobody said "out there" to mean the whole world. Perhaps he believed, for some private reason, that it really was raining — perhaps he was ill, or confused. But his eyes had not been confused. They had been perfectly clear, and in that brief second when he looked at her, they had been afraid.
People who could not lie were rarely afraid of being caught.
That afternoon, back at her desk, Ione found that she could not concentrate. Augustin had approved the bridge statement with a single word in green ink: "Lovely." She had three other jobs waiting: a letter of apology for a hotel chain, a speech for a retiring judge, and the label for a new brand of breakfast cereal that contained, according to the manufacturer's own laboratory, "slightly more than nothing of real nutritional value". Normally she would have enjoyed the cereal. Today she stared at the paper and wrote nothing at all.

At six o'clock she went to the window and looked out over the river. The sun was setting behind the cathedral, and the water had turned the colour of copper. Somewhere in that city, among half a million people who could not say a single false word, there was one man who could.
She had spent seven years learning how to hide inside the truth. It had never occurred to her that someone might simply step outside it.
She picked up her pen, out of habit, and tried to write the sentence herself. It is raining. Her hand stopped before the pen touched the paper. Her fingers would not move. Her throat tightened, though she had not even spoken. The Stop held her, as it held everyone, as it always had.
She put the pen down and found that her hands were trembling.
Then she put on her coat, turned off the lamp, and went out into the dry, clear evening, looking at every face she passed.

# The Corvane Matter

@audio https://wvmjobeketinbzxggzuq.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/audio/c1-the-phraser/chapter_2.page_1-1793523778110.mp3
For three days, Ione looked for him everywhere.
She went back to Pennick's at lunchtime and sat by the window for an hour, letting her soup go cold. She walked the quays in the evening. She took a different tram to work each morning and studied the passengers over the top of her newspaper, like a detective in a cheap film. Once she followed a grey coat for six streets before its owner turned round and revealed himself to be a woman of about sixty with a small dog in her arms.
It was absurd, and she knew it. She was a sensible person. She paid her bills on time, she had never missed a deadline, and she distrusted anything that could not be explained in plain language. And yet she could not let it go.

On the second evening she went to the city library and asked, as carefully as she could, for anything they had on "exceptions to the Stop". The librarian, a young man with ink on his fingers, looked at her as if she had asked for books about square circles. In the end he found her a dusty volume from the medical section, written forty years earlier by a professor who had spent his career studying people with brain injuries. Some of them, the professor wrote, lost the ability to speak altogether. Some lost their memory, and therefore said false things without knowing they were false — the Stop, after all, only prevented you from saying what you believed to be untrue. But in eleven hundred cases, he had never found a single person who could knowingly and deliberately state a falsehood.
"It is not a rule of society," he had written in his conclusion. "It is a rule of the human animal, as fixed as the rule that we cannot breathe underwater."
Ione closed the book and sat in the quiet reading room for a long time. Then she went home, made herself an omelette she did not eat, and lay awake listening to the rain — real rain, this time — against her bedroom window.

On the fourth day, Augustin Peel called her into his office.
Augustin was sixty-eight years old and had been a phraser for longer than Ione had been alive. He was a small, neat man with silver hair, a silver moustache and a wardrobe of beautiful waistcoats, and he spoke so slowly and precisely that clients sometimes mistook him for a fool. They rarely made the mistake twice. He had written speeches for three prime ministers and apologies for two royal princes, and it was said that he had once written a statement for a chemical company that was so perfectly constructed that the newspapers had praised the company for its honesty on the same day it poisoned a river.
He had hired Ione when she was twenty-two, after reading a letter she had written to the university complaining about her exam results. "You managed to accuse three professors of incompetence," he had told her at the interview, "without saying a single thing they could object to. That is a gift. It is also, I should warn you, a slightly dangerous one."
Today he was standing by his window with his hands behind his back.
"Sit down, my dear," he said. "We have a visitor coming in ten minutes, and I want you in the room."
"Who?"
"Odette Crale."
Ione had been reaching for a chair. She stopped. "From the Bureau?"
"From the Bureau," Augustin agreed. "The Deputy Director herself, in fact. She wishes to commission a statement, and she wishes it to be written by the best phraser in the city." He turned and gave her a small bow. "As I am too old and too lazy, I have recommended you."

The Bureau of Candour occupied a vast white building on the north bank of the Sull, with tall columns and a motto carved above its doors: Nothing But What Is So. Officially, its purpose was to protect the truth in public life. It registered phrasers and fined those whose statements were judged "deliberately misleading in effect". It investigated people who refused to answer questions in court. It ran the national Candour Day celebrations each December, when schoolchildren recited the famous true sentences of history and the Prime Minister gave a speech about the honesty of the nation. In practice, as everyone in Ione's profession knew, the Bureau was the most powerful government department in the country, because in a world where nobody could lie, the only real power lay in deciding which questions got asked.
And Odette Crale, it was said, asked better questions than anyone alive.
She arrived exactly on time. She was a tall woman in her fifties, with short grey hair, a long pale face and very light blue eyes, and she wore a dark suit without a single piece of jewellery. She shook Augustin's hand, then Ione's. Her grip was dry and brief.

"Miss Halvey," she said. "I've read some of your work. The statement on the Kessler Hotel fire was particularly good. You managed to make a locked fire door sound like an architectural feature."
"I only described what the hotel told me," said Ione.
"Of course you did," said Odette, and smiled. Her smile did not reach those pale eyes. "That is what all of us do, isn't it? Describe."
They sat around Augustin's small round table. Augustin poured tea. Odette did not touch hers. She opened a thin leather folder and placed it on the table between them, but kept one hand flat on top of it.
"What I'm going to tell you is confidential," she said. "I'm sure I don't need to explain what that means to two members of your profession."
It meant, Ione thought, that if anybody asked them directly, they would have to choose between silence and the truth. Confidentiality, in Lantry, was not a promise to deny things. It was a promise to refuse to answer.
"Six years ago," Odette went on, "the Bureau provided funding to a private medical research centre called the Corvane Institute. It was located on the coast, about forty miles from here, near the village of Saltreach. The institute's official purpose was the study of speech disorders. Its unofficial purpose was more ambitious." She paused. "It was studying the Stop."

Augustin raised one silver eyebrow. Ione kept her face perfectly still.
"Its researchers believed," Odette continued, "that the Stop was not, as most scientists think, a fixed and permanent feature of the human brain, but a kind of reflex — complex, deeply rooted, but in theory treatable. The Bureau supported this work because we wished to understand the Stop better. Naturally."
"Naturally," murmured Augustin.
"Two years ago, the institute was closed. There were problems with the research. Some of the volunteers who took part in the trials became ill. A small number of them died. The institute's director has since left the country, and most of its staff have moved on to other work. The Bureau has completed a full internal review, and we are now satisfied that the project should never have been allowed to continue."
She took her hand off the folder at last and slid it towards Ione.
"Within the next few weeks," she said, "some information about the Corvane Institute is likely to become public. We would like to make a statement first. We would like it to explain the Bureau's relationship with the institute in a way that is complete, accurate, and — let us say — calm."
"Calm," repeated Ione.
"The public does not need to be frightened by stories of mad scientists trying to break the most fundamental law of human nature," said Odette. "Particularly when those scientists failed."

Ione opened the folder. It contained a dozen pages: a short history of the institute, a list of dates, a summary of the Bureau's funding, carefully phrased in a way she instantly recognised as the work of another phraser, probably a good one. There was a photograph of a low grey building on a cliff above the sea. And at the back, held together with a paper clip, there was a list of names: the volunteers who had taken part in the final trial, each with a date of birth and a small photograph.
She turned the pages slowly, as if she were only half interested. Her heart had begun to beat faster, though she could not have said why. Seventeen names. Seventeen faces, most of them young, most of them looking at the camera with the slightly nervous expression of people who had agreed to something they did not entirely understand. Next to eleven of the names, someone had written a single word in neat black ink: Deceased. Next to four others, the word was Hospitalised.
Next to the last two, the word was Missing.
The first of these was a young woman with fair hair. The second was a man of about thirty, with a thin, intelligent face and untidy dark hair that needed cutting.
Lucan Mere, said the name beneath the photograph. Age at time of trial: thirty-three.
Ione looked at the picture for perhaps one second too long. Then she turned the page.

"Are the missing volunteers being looked for?" she asked. Her voice sounded perfectly normal. She had learned, years ago, to keep her voice normal while her mind raced.
"They are," said Odette. "For their own safety. Those who survived the trial were left with certain — neurological difficulties. They may be confused. They may need medical care." She was watching Ione closely now, with those pale, steady eyes. "If anyone were to come across one of them, the Bureau would very much appreciate being informed."
"Of course," said Ione. Then, carefully: "I'll certainly keep the photographs in mind."
That was true. She would.
Odette held her gaze for a moment longer. Then she nodded, closed her own notebook, and stood up. "I'd like a first draft by the end of next week," she said. "Augustin, thank you for your time. Miss Halvey, I look forward to reading what you write."
When she had gone, Augustin sat back in his chair and let out a long breath through his moustache.
"Well," he said. "That was interesting."
"Which part?"
"All of it. But particularly the part where she told us a great deal while appearing to tell us almost nothing." He picked up his tea, found it cold, and put it down again. "Did you notice that she never once said what the trials were trying to achieve? Only what the researchers believed. And that she said the project should never have been allowed to continue — but not that it should never have begun."

"I noticed," said Ione.
"And did you notice," said Augustin, more quietly, "that she was watching you, not me, the entire time she spoke about the missing volunteers?"
Ione said nothing.
Augustin studied her over the rim of his spectacles. He had known her for seven years. He was, in his gentle, old-fashioned way, almost a second father to her. And he was the best phraser in Lantry, which meant that he knew exactly how to hear what a person was not saying.
"My dear," he said, "I am not going to ask you a question. I am going to tell you something instead, and you may do whatever you like with it. Odette Crale does not commission statements. She has a whole department of phrasers of her own, and they are very good. If she came here, in person, it was not because she wanted your sentences. It was because she wanted to look at you."
"Why would she want to look at me?"
"That," said Augustin, "is precisely the question I am not going to ask."
He stood up, which meant that the meeting was over. At the door he paused, with his hand on the frame.
"Be careful," he said. "You and I play with words for a living. The Bureau does not play."

Ione took the folder back to her desk and sat with it for the rest of the afternoon, pretending to work. She read every page twice. She made notes on the history of the institute, on the funding, on the dates. And every few minutes, without meaning to, she turned to the back of the folder and looked again at the face of Lucan Mere.
She had found him. Or rather, she had found his name, which was the next best thing. And she had found something else, too: a reason why a man might be able to say that it was raining on a perfectly dry day.
At seven o'clock, when the office was empty, she copied down the address that was printed beneath his photograph. It was the address he had given the institute two years ago, in a district of narrow streets near the docks called Tallow Reach. It was almost certainly out of date. He was missing, after all; he would hardly be sitting at home waiting for visitors.
But Ione had been a phraser for seven years, and she had learned one useful thing about people who were hiding: very often, they did not go far. They went somewhere that felt safe. They went somewhere they knew.
She took the tram to the docks.

Tallow Reach was a quarter of old warehouses converted into flats, with iron staircases on the outside walls and washing hanging across the alleys. The address on the folder belonged to a building on the corner of a street called Rope Walk. The front door was locked. The name beside the bell for the third floor had been scratched out with something sharp.
Ione stood in the dark and wondered what she was doing. If he was here, what would she say to him? And if he answered — how would she know whether a single word he said was true?
She was still standing there, with her finger above the bell, when a voice spoke quietly from the shadows of the alley behind her.
"You're the woman from the café."
She turned. He was leaning against the wall beneath an iron staircase, in the same grey coat, with his hands in his pockets. In the yellow light of the streetlamp his face looked even thinner than she remembered.
"Yes," she said.
"You followed me."
"No. I found you. It's not the same thing."
That made him smile, very slightly. "You speak like a phraser," he said.
"I am a phraser."
"I know," said Lucan Mere. "That's why I've been waiting for you."

He would not talk in the alley. "Too many windows," he said, glancing up at the dark warehouse walls. "And people in Tallow Reach have very good ears." Instead he led her down to the river, where the last ferry of the evening was waiting at the old stone jetty, its engine coughing and its single lamp swinging in the wind.
The Sull ferry was the oldest boat in Lantry. It crossed the river every twenty minutes between the docks and the fish market on the south bank, and almost nobody used it any more, because the bridges were quicker. That evening there were only three other passengers: a fisherman asleep against a pile of nets, and an elderly couple sharing a bag of chips. Lucan bought two tickets and led Ione to the stern, where the noise of the engine was loudest. They stood side by side at the rail, watching the lights of the city slide across the black water.

For a while neither of them spoke. Ione found that she did not know how to begin. All her professional life she had been listening to people and measuring their words. She knew how to hear the gap between what a person said and what they meant. But that skill depended on a single assumption, so deep that she had never even noticed it: that every word, taken by itself, was true. Without that assumption, she felt like a musician who had suddenly lost her hearing.
"You're wondering whether to believe anything I say," said Lucan.
"Yes."
"That's sensible. In your position, I'd wonder the same thing." He looked at her. In the swinging lamplight his eyes were very dark. "I can tell you that I won't lie to you tonight. But of course, if I were planning to lie to you, I would say exactly the same thing. So it doesn't help much, does it?"
"No," said Ione. "It doesn't."
"Then perhaps you should just ask me questions," he said. "And decide for yourself."

She had expected many things, but not this strange, almost gentle honesty about his own dishonesty. She held the cold iron rail and thought for a moment. Then she began, carefully, as if she were stepping onto ice.
"In the café," she said. "When you said it was raining. You knew it wasn't."
"Yes."
"And you said it anyway. Deliberately."
"Yes."
"Why?"
He considered this. "Because I wanted a coffee to take away, and I didn't want Mrs Pennick to ask me to sit down and chat. She does that, if she likes your face. I don't like being looked at for too long." He shrugged. "It was a small, stupid lie. A lazy one. I tell them sometimes, just to remind myself that I still can."
"Still can?"
"It's not always — " He stopped, and frowned at the water. "It doesn't always work the same way. Some days are easier than others."

Ione filed this away. "Can you do it now? Say something false. Something you know is false."
Lucan looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, quite calmly: "The river is made of milk."
She had known it was coming. She had asked for it. And still, when the words came out, something in her stomach dropped, the way it does when you miss a step on a staircase. There was no tightening in his throat, no hesitation, no trace of strain on his face. The sentence simply left him, as easily as breath, and hung in the cold air above the very black, very un-milky water of the Sull.
She realised that she had taken a step back from him.
"I'm sorry," said Lucan quietly. "Everybody does that. The first time."
"How many people have you shown?"
"Not many. Three. Four, including you." He turned back to the rail. "It's not the sort of thing you can talk about at parties."

The ferry rolled slightly as it moved into the middle of the river, where the current was strongest. On the north bank, high above the water, Ione could see the white columns of the Bureau of Candour, lit up like a temple. She thought of Odette Crale's pale eyes, and the word Missing written in neat black ink.
"You were at the Corvane Institute," she said. It was not a question.
"You've seen the file."
"I've seen a file. I don't know how complete it is."
That made him smile again. "Spoken like a true professional." Then the smile faded. "Yes. I was at Corvane. I was one of the volunteers in the last trial. There were seventeen of us."
"Why did you volunteer?"
"Money, at first," he said. "I'd lost my job. I was a translator — I translated technical manuals, mostly, very boring work — and the company I worked for closed. The institute was paying well. They put an advertisement in the paper asking for healthy adults to help with research into speech. It didn't say anything about the Stop." He paused. "By the time they told us what they were really doing, most of us had already signed the forms."

"And you didn't leave?"
"No. Partly because of the money. Partly — " He hesitated. "Partly because I was curious. Imagine it. Imagine being told that you could be the first person in history to do something that every human being has always believed was impossible. Wouldn't you want to know what it felt like?"
Ione thought of her pen, frozen above the page, refusing to write it is raining. She did not answer.
"The treatment lasted eight months," Lucan went on. "Injections, mostly. Some kind of compound they'd developed. And exercises — hours and hours of exercises. They'd show us a red card and ask us to say it was blue. At first, nobody could. Then, after a few months, a few of us began to — not speak, exactly, but stammer. Get halfway through the word. It hurt. It felt like swallowing glass." He touched his throat without seeming to notice. "Then people began to get sick."

He told her about it in a flat, even voice, as if he were translating a technical manual. Headaches first, then fevers, then seizures. A woman called Agathe who stopped being able to recognise her own family. A young man who began to say false things without meaning to, and then could not stop, and then could not tell any more which of his own words were true. The first death, in the sixth month. The second, two weeks later.
"They should have stopped then," said Lucan. "Some of the researchers wanted to. There were arguments — I heard some of them, through the walls. But the people in charge said we were too close."
"Too close to what?"
"To success." He looked down at his hands on the rail. "And they were right, in the end. In the seventh month, I said that a red card was blue. It hurt, but I said it. Two weeks later I could do it without any pain at all. By the end of the trial, I was the only one."

"The only one who could lie?"
"The only one who could lie, and still knew he was lying." He said it simply, without pride. "There's a difference. I'll explain it to you one day, if you want."
The ferry's horn sounded, long and mournful, as they approached the south bank. The fisherman woke up, looked around him in confusion, and went back to sleep.
"What happened when the trial ended?" asked Ione.
"They closed the institute. Very suddenly. One morning there were men from the Bureau in the corridors — men in dark suits who didn't introduce themselves. They took all the files. They took the remaining samples of the compound. They took the survivors, too — the ones who were ill — and moved them to a hospital in the city. I was supposed to go with them." He gave a short, dry laugh. "But I had learned something important at Corvane. I'd learned that if someone asks you a question, you don't have to tell them the truth. So when a man from the Bureau asked me whether I had any family I could stay with while they arranged my transfer, I said yes. I gave him an address. Then I walked out of the front door and never went back."

"You've been hiding for two years?"
"More or less. I've moved around. I have friends, a few. And I can be very convincing, when I need to be." He glanced at her. "It's surprisingly easy to hide, in a country where nobody believes it's possible to lie. People never check anything."

"No," he agreed. "I hadn't. But I'd heard your name."
"From whom?"
The ferry bumped gently against the south jetty. A boy in a woollen hat jumped onto the stones and began to tie the ropes. The elderly couple stood up, brushing salt from their coats. Lucan did not move.

Ione could not speak.
Her mother had been a chemist. That was true; that was the only thing Ione had ever known about her work. She had worked for a pharmaceutical company. She had travelled a great deal, and come home tired, and talked very little about her job. When Ione asked, she would say things like, "Oh, we're studying how certain compounds affect the nervous system," or "It's complicated, darling, and most of it is very dull." Ione had never pushed. Why would she? Her mother could not lie. If she said it was dull, then she believed it was dull.
But she had not said it was dull. Ione heard the sentence again in her memory, with the trained ear of a phraser. Most of it is very dull. Not all of it. Most.
Her mother had been phrasing.

"Why should I believe you?" said Ione at last. She was gripping the rail so tightly that her fingers had gone white. "You've just shown me that you can say anything you like. The river is made of milk. It's raining. Your mother was murdered. Why should any of it be true?"
"You shouldn't believe me," said Lucan. "Not because I say so. That's the whole point." He was quiet for a moment. "But your mother left something behind. I don't know what it is. She told me once that if anything happened to her, she'd made sure that the truth would end up with the one person who'd know how to read it. She said her daughter would understand, because her daughter knew how to find what's hidden inside a sentence."
"And you want me to find it."
"Yes."
"Why? What do you get out of it?"
He turned his face towards the river. Behind him, on the north bank, the white columns of the Bureau shone in the darkness.
"I'd like to stop being the only one," he said.

Ione looked at him for a long time. She listened to the words, the way she had been trained to listen. She felt for the edges of them, the places where meaning might slip. And for the first time in her life, she realised, it was not enough. She could hear everything he said. She could not hear whether it was true.
The ferry crossed the black water, and neither of them spoke again until it reached the shore.
